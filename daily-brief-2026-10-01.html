Lead

RATES — PCE COOLS, HORMUZ DOESN'T

Fed hike odds crater to roughly 35% on a soft PCE print — but oil is up anyway, after the worst night of tanker strikes in weeks

August PCE, released Wednesday at 8:30a ET, came in cooler than consensus on every line the Fed watches: headline PCE +0.3% m/m (cons. +0.4%) and +3.4% y/y (cons. 3.7%); core PCE +0.2% m/m (cons. +0.3%) and +3.0% y/y (cons. 3.3%, down from 3.3% in July). [fact] Two caveats belong next to the headline, not below it: part of the year-over-year cooling traces to a BEA methodology change in how several price categories are measured, applied retroactively back to 2021, which complicates a clean trend read; and the personal saving rate fell to a near-four-year low, meaning households are outspending income even as prices cool — not the combination a clean soft-landing story is made of. [fact/read]

The market reaction was immediate and large. CME FedWatch's probability of a quarter-point hike at the Oct. 27-28 meeting fell to roughly 35% (one tracker put it at 34.9%), down from about 71% a week earlier; Kalshi and Polymarket converged in the same 33%-39% band by Thursday morning. [fact] That is a reversal of this brief's Tuesday report of 65%-72% hike odds — one of the largest single-data-point swings in this series since the Sept. 16 hike itself. [fact]

What didn't follow is crude. Brent is $100.36 and WTI $92.01 as of roughly 6a ET, up 2.38% and 1.76% respectively with no settle yet [flag] — a dovish inflation print would ordinarily pull oil down with yields; instead both benchmarks are higher, because the overnight story in the Strait of Hormuz overwhelmed it. UKMTO reported three separate tankers struck by unidentified projectiles entering or transiting Hormuz Tuesday night: a crude tanker hit on its port side, a second tanker hit while transiting inbound, and an LNG tanker also struck — the busiest stretch of tanker strikes since the Sept. 23 Cape Dao attack, and on top of the Kuwaiti supertanker Al Funtas hit Sept. 28. [NEW, fact] No casualties were reported in any of the three; UKMTO has not attributed the attacks to any party, and an uncorroborated social-media claim blaming Iran should be read as exactly that. [flag]

The mechanism is the thing to understand, not just the two headlines separately: the oil complex is currently being priced off the Hormuz risk premium, not off the Fed's inflation data, and today is a clean demonstration of that decoupling. A genuinely dovish PCE print should have pulled the 10-year and the dollar down alongside crude easing; instead the 10-year is roughly flat on the day at 5.32% (+2.7bp), the 30-year similarly flat at 5.66% (+2.5bp), and the dollar index actually firmed to 101.91 (+0.45%) [read] — consistent with energy-linked inflation risk offsetting the growth-friendly data rather than confirming it.

The second-order consequence lands squarely inside the Oct. 27-28 decision window. A Fed that was leaning toward a second 2026 hike on sticky, largely oil-driven inflation now has real cover to hold if PCE keeps cooling into the Oct. 14 CPI and Oct. 29 PCE prints — but if Hormuz attacks keep pushing crude higher between now and then, the hike case reasserts itself before the Committee meets, on a different transmission channel than the one that just eased. Tomorrow's payrolls report, tonight's Nike print, and the decisive Yankees-Red Sox game are each lower-stakes than this question, but all land inside the same 48 hours the market now has to use to decide which of this morning's two contradictory signals — a dovish PCE or a hawkish Hormuz — is the one that actually sets the tone into next week. [read]

Watch for a genuine tell: whether the counter-proposal the Trump administration sent to Tehran Wednesday through Qatari mediators (Iran & Hormuz, below) produces any de-escalation signal before the weekend. Absent that, it is Hormuz, not the Fed, setting the risk tone right now — a reversal of the roles those two stories played as recently as Tuesday's issue.

Iran & Hormuz

The Trump administration sent a counter-proposal to Tehran through Qatari mediators Wednesday; Foreign Minister Araghchi presented it to Iran's Cabinet, and neither side has disclosed its terms. [NEW, fact] It follows Trump's public rejection last week of Iran's own seven-day Hormuz-reopening plan and Araghchi's Tuesday statement that Tehran would not "back down" from its conditions while still seeking "real diplomacy" over a resumption of war. [continuity] Whether the US counter-proposal engages the sequencing dispute this brief has tracked since Tuesday — sanctions relief and frozen-asset access before or after a nuclear deal — is not yet known. [flag]

Physically, overnight was the most active stretch in weeks: UKMTO reported three tankers struck by unidentified projectiles Tuesday night — a crude tanker hit on its port side, a second hit while transiting inbound, and an LNG tanker also struck — with no casualties reported and no attribution from UKMTO. [NEW, fact] That is in addition to the Kuwaiti supertanker Al Funtas, struck Sept. 28, and the Sept. 23 Cape Dao torpedo attack that killed one crew member. Despite the escalation, the AIS-visible vessel-holding count fell sharply to 135 as of 23:50 UTC Sept. 30, down from 244 the day before (Board) — a counterintuitive move large enough that this brief treats it as noise, not signal, pending another day's data. [flag] Transit data remains lagged at 1 vessel on Sept. 27 against the 85/day pre-crisis baseline.
