Lead

RATES — 24-YEAR HIGHS MEET RECORD STOCKS

Treasury yields hit their highest since 2002 even as stocks keep setting records — and a stopped-through 10-year auction says demand, not supply, is driving it

The long end of the Treasury curve pushed to levels unseen since 2002 this week even as the S&P 500 and Nasdaq kept making fresh highs — a split that is becoming the market's central tension. The Treasury's own daily par yield curve puts the 10-year at 5.22% and the 30-year at 5.60% at Thursday's close (Oct. 8); secondary reporting on Tuesday-Wednesday's trading had the 10-year intraday as high as roughly 5.32% and the 30-year above 5.60%, both described by multiple outlets as the highest since 2002. [fact, flag: close vs. intraday from different sources] That happened in the same stretch the S&P 500 notched what one count puts as its 28th record close of the year Monday (7,818.93, +0.58%) and the Nasdaq Composite — a different gauge than the Nasdaq-100 futures quoted on the Board — its 23rd or 24th; sources disagree on the exact count. [fact, flag]

The mechanism is a tug-of-war the Fed itself is caught inside. The FOMC raised its target range a quarter point to 3.75%-4.00% on Sept. 17 — confirmed directly against the Fed's own policy-rate page — an unusual hike into a labor market that then promptly delivered a 29,000 September payrolls print, one of the cycle's widest misses (issue No. 23's Lead). [fact] September's FOMC minutes, released Oct. 7, were reported by a single secondary aggregator as signaling that "another hike...is likely appropriate" with timing undecided; this brief could not check that language against the Fed's own minutes text this run, so treat the characterization as unconfirmed rather than settled. [flag] Layered on top: ISM Services' prices-paid index reportedly hit 74.0 in September, its highest since July 2022, per the same tier of secondary sourcing [flag] — a services-inflation signal that, if it holds up, gives hawks something concrete even as headline jobs growth stalls. That combination — a Fed that hiked in September, weak jobs, and hot services prices — is why wires are calling this a 24-year high in yields rather than a one-day blip, and why this brief won't print a single Oct. 28 hike-odds number tonight: this run turned up figures from roughly 17% to 78% depending on source and date, an unusually wide and unreconcilable spread even by this beat's standards. [flag]

The one piece of hard, unambiguous data in the mix is the auction itself: Wednesday's $39 billion 10-year note sale priced at a 5.300% high yield against a 5.317% when-issued level — a stop-through of roughly 1.7 basis points, meaning the bid came in stronger than the pre-auction market was pricing, not weaker. [fact, investinglive.com] That cuts against the "fiscal supply is breaking the bond market" narrative making the rounds: if elevated yields were purely a function of investors refusing to absorb Treasury's issuance calendar, this auction should have tailed, not stopped through. The more defensible read is that term premium and inflation-risk pricing, not a failed auction, are doing the work here — consistent with background this brief has flagged in prior issues, though not confirmed as the specific cause of this week's move. [read]

Second-order consequences: equity leadership stays narrow and AI/tech-concentrated — one strategist's description of the rally as narrow-breadth-and-tech-driven, cited secondhand, matches what this brief has tracked — while the Dow sits roughly 5% below its Aug. 5 record, unable to keep pace. [read] Duration-sensitive sectors and mortgage-adjacent names are the ones to watch if 5%+ 10-year yields persist into Oct. 14's CPI print, now the swing data point for whether the hawkish-minutes read or the soft payrolls number wins the argument heading into Oct. 27-28. Watch too whether Tuesday's bank-earnings kickoff (JPM, Wells Fargo, J&J) gives any read-through on net interest margin at these yield levels — the first hard evidence, rather than another secondary-sourced estimate, this brief will have on how the real economy is absorbing 2002-era borrowing costs.

Iran & Hormuz
