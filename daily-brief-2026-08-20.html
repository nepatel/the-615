Lead

IRAN · MARKETS

UAE severs economic ties with Iran after Hormuz missile alert; Trump vows "most crushing" sanctions yet as Brent tops $93

The UAE said Wednesday it is suspending all trade and financial transactions with Iran "until further notice" — the sharpest break yet between two historically close trading partners — after its Ministry of Defense said two Iranian ballistic missiles were fired toward UAE waters Tuesday evening, one falling short in international waters and the other landing inside UAE territorial waters. [NEW] A missile-threat alert had sounded in Dubai that evening, urging residents to shelter. Iran denied responsibility; state media called it a "false-flag operation," and no group has claimed the launch. [fact: UAE statement and MoD assessment vs. Iran's denial — neither independently verified by us]

Hours later, Trump escalated on Truth Social, promising the "most crushing economic operation" ever mounted against Iran and threatening "TREMENDOUS Economic Consequences" for any country whose banks, businesses, airports or government entities give Tehran "any type of lifeline." [NEW] It extends the administration's five-month "Operation Economic Fury" campaign of oil, shipping and financial sanctions, layered on an existing naval blockade of Iranian ports; specifics of the new measures haven't been published. Iran's FM Araghchi dismissed it as "economic terrorism" and "a diversion from America's own crisis," and state media said Tehran has already adapted to existing restrictions. [read: bravado runs both directions, but the UAE break is the first hard supply-chain fact to come out of this specific flare-up, not just rhetoric]

Oil is pricing the UAE break, not just the rhetoric. Brent is bid to $93.92 (+2.51%) this morning against Wednesday's $91.62 settle, and WTI's October contract has run to $86.55 (+2.56%) — both up roughly $2 on the session, with Brent now within range of the year's highs. [flag: no fresh settle yet this morning, real-time levels only] The mechanism: the UAE has been one of the last Gulf states still moving meaningful non-Iranian-linked volume through Hormuz, with more than 80% of transits over the past two weeks rerouted through the Omani corridor the U.S. Navy now escorts. A state actively severing financial ties with Iran raises the odds Abu Dhabi tolerates a more forceful defense of that corridor — or that Iran retaliates against it directly. [read] The relationship being severed is not trivial: annual UAE-Iran trade runs an estimated $27-30 billion, with the UAE Iran's largest non-oil trading partner after China and its single biggest source of non-oil imports, roughly $21.9 billion in the most recent Iranian fiscal year — a flow that had roughly quadrupled since the U.S. exited the JCPOA in 2018 (see Iran & Hormuz for detail). [NEW] Iran's own oil trade was already collapsing under sanctions that pre-date this week: Kpler puts loadings at 156,000 b/d through Aug. 17, down from 893,000 b/d in July [NEW] — a regime with progressively less left to lose from further isolation, which is its own escalation risk. Wednesday's EIA data added a domestic-supply layer under all of this: U.S. commercial crude stocks stand at 424.4 million barrels, about 2% below the five-year seasonal average, for the week ended Aug. 14, with refinery inputs up 215,000 b/d to 17.4 million b/d and gasoline stocks down 1.0 million barrels and running 6% below their own five-year average. [NEW] That's a domestic cushion already thinner than normal heading into a supply shock that has nothing to do with U.S. production.

The second-order story is what this does to a Fed that had just found its footing. Wednesday's FOMC minutes showed the July hold was closer than the 9-3 vote suggests: Cleveland's Hammack, Minneapolis's Kashkari and Dallas's Logan all dissented for a hike, and minutes quoted other participants warning that "policy tightening would likely be necessary if inflation did not decline" — a more hawkish committee than markets had priced after the July jobs miss. [NEW] Warsh also used the meeting to float cutting the Fed's calendar from eight meetings a year to six, arguing it would let more data accumulate between decisions. [NEW] CME FedWatch has September hike odds back up to 34.6% as of Wednesday, from an estimated 31-33% a week ago. A renewed oil shock landing on top of that debate — Brent up roughly $5 in a week and a half — argues for the hawks just as Warsh prepares his first Jackson Hole keynote on Aug. 28. [DEVELOPING]

Bonds, oddly, aren't confirming the hawkish read this morning, because a separate story is dominating rates. Treasury on Wednesday doubled the size of its long-bond buyback operations, to at least $4 billion per operation for 10-to-30-year debt, effective Sept. 9 — a liquidity move Secretary Bessent has framed as steadying a market that had pushed the 10-year to a 20-month intraday high of 4.75% on Tuesday. [NEW] The announcement did the work: secondary-market quotes had the 10-year falling roughly 6bp Wednesday and the 30-year 9bp, even as Treasury's own official par curve — a different measurement basis — puts Wednesday's close at 4.35% and 5.19% respectively, a gap of roughly 30bp on the 10-year we can't reconcile this morning. [flag: methodology divergence, unreconciled] Equity futures are essentially flat overnight (S&P -0.06%, Nasdaq +0.07%), which reads as the market treating oil, Iran and the bond rally as offsetting each other — for now, and only until one of the three breaks.

For a rates-and-energy portfolio, the setup into today's session is a genuine cross-current rather than a clean directional call: energy is the asset class actually pricing the UAE/Iran escalation, long-duration Treasuries are rallying on a domestic liquidity story that has nothing to do with Hormuz, and equity index futures are sitting between the two waiting for one to dominate. [read] Retail earnings (see Business) complicate the picture further by mixing genuine demand strength with one-time tariff-refund noise on margins, which is exactly the kind of input the Fed said in Wednesday's minutes it doesn't yet trust enough to declare inflation solved. The next clean read on which force wins — energy-driven inflation risk or liquidity-driven yield relief — is likely the Aug. 26 PCE print, landing the same day as Nvidia's earnings and Lisa Cook's response deadline.

Separately, Treasury's doubled buyback program for 10-to-30-year debt drove the more immediate bond-market reaction, pulling long yields off Tuesday's multi-year highs even as the official par curve and secondary quotes diverge (see Board). That Tuesday spike wasn't cosmetic: coverage tied the 20-month-high 10-year print directly to renewed refinancing stress for commercial-real-estate borrowers, on top of bond-supply concerns and the oil-driven uncertainty already in play. [NEW figure due this morning, not yet in hand] Today's claims print, alongside Walmart, Deere and Ross Stores results, is effectively the last full data day before next Wednesday's Nvidia/PCE/GDP cluster — the market's next real chance to reprice the September odds either direction.

Iran & Hormuz

The trade relationship the UAE just suspended was, until this week, one of Iran's few remaining functioning economic lifelines: an estimated $27-30 billion a year, with the UAE serving as Iran's largest non-oil trading partner after China and the source of roughly $21.9 billion of Iran's non-oil imports in the most recent Iranian fiscal year (ended March 2025) — a flow that had roughly quadrupled since the U.S. withdrew from the JCPOA in 2018, largely by absorbing trade that sanctions pushed out of formal channels. [NEW] That is now suspended "until further notice." Trump separately renewed his threat to "bomb the s--- out of" Oman if it "gets in the way" of a Hormuz shipping deal — the second such threat since May, aimed at a U.S. ally that has never been in direct conflict with Washington. [NEW] More than 80% of Strait transits over the past two weeks have run through the Omani corridor the U.S. Navy now escorts, per CNN reporting Wednesday — a routing solution Iran has consistently rejected as illegitimate. [NEW] The chief engineer of the Minoan Dignity, the Greek-managed, Liberia-flagged bulker hit by an unidentified projectile off Oman over the weekend of Aug. 15–16, has died of his injuries; UKMTO and Tradewinds confirmed the death this week, with no group claiming responsibility. [DEVELOPING] Cumulative strike and fatality counts across the wider war remain too disputed across U.S., Iranian and NGO sources to update responsibly this run — Trump has cited 52,000 killed over five months; Iran's Health Ministry and independent trackers report figures an order of magnitude lower for comparable windows.
