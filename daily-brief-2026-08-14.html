Lead

Iran & markets

Basij commander claims Hormuz is "under Iran's control" as attacks intensify — and oil fell anyway

Iran escalated its pushback against Trump's "total control" claim on Thursday, and for the first time did so through the newly reshuffled chain of command rather than only the foreign ministry. Hossein Taeb — the Khamenei loyalist installed atop the Basij paramilitary in Sunday's reshuffle — said the strait is "under Iran's control and management," while Iran's joint military command separately declared that no vessel can transit without Tehran's permission, calling U.S. claims of normal passage "lies and falsehoods." [fact: statements confirmed Thursday] Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi went further, saying Washington has "long miscalculated due to intelligence failures" and calling the war itself "an even bigger miscalculation." [fact] This is the first time the command structure installed after February's succession has been invoked in an actual public dispute rather than described in the abstract — read as evidence that stacking loyalists atop the Basij and IRGC was about building a coherent line of authority for exactly this kind of confrontation, not just internal security. [read]

The rhetoric coincided with the worst 24-48 hours for vessel safety in weeks. A commercial tanker was struck by a drone on an outbound transit overnight (UKMTO-confirmed, minor damage, no injuries), and separately ADNOC said two of its vessels were attacked Thursday evening — the UAE's second such incident in under a week, prompting Abu Dhabi to formally invoke UN Security Council Resolution 2817 on freedom of navigation. [fact] Two attacks in roughly a day, layered onto Trump's Wednesday claim that the U.S. "own[s]" the strait and "might keep it," is a sharper contradiction than the general disagreement carried in prior issues — a live claim of control is now being answered with live attacks on shipping. [read]

Shipping data is unusually unanimous on direction even as vendors disagree on magnitude and timing. IMF PortWatch's lagged AIS count showed just 1 vessel transiting Aug. 9 against a ~73/day pre-crisis baseline; straits.live puts last-24h throughput at roughly 1% of normal; and Lloyd's List's slower weekly aggregate shows both total transits (78, week of 8/3-8/9, down from a revised 95) and the non-Iranian-linked westbound subset (23 in the week to ~8/12, down from 28) falling for a second straight week. [fact, three sources, one direction]

Yet Brent and WTI both fell Thursday — WTI settled down more than 2% near $81, its first drop after five straight sessions of gains — and are only fractionally higher (Brent +0.18%, WTI +0.65%) in early Friday trade. [fact] That divergence is worth flagging rather than smoothing over: crude is trading on the macro backdrop — soft PPI, falling rate-hike odds, demand-side caution explicitly cited in Thursday's oil commentary — even as the physical security picture in the strait deteriorated further. [read] Either the market is treating further escalation as already priced into a persistent high-$80s Brent handle, or it's under-pricing the risk that a second consecutive ADNOC attack is an emerging pattern rather than a one-off. The data available this run can't distinguish between those two reads, and that ambiguity is itself the tradeable fact for the desk this morning. [read]

The Fed angle from Wednesday's CPI carries through. Soft CPI (+0.1% headline MoM, +3.4% YoY) and Thursday's even softer PPI (flat MoM vs. +0.2% consensus, annual rate down to 4.7% from 5.5%) have pulled September hike odds down from the roughly 60% flagged in the last issue — CME FedWatch and Kalshi disagree on the exact level (readings this run ranged roughly 30-40% on CME, 30% on Kalshi) but both now put a hold as the base case. [fact, hedge on precision] A market pricing disinflation and de-risking Hormuz at the same time is either coherent — both signal a Fed that doesn't need to lean harder into what looks like a contained supply shock — or contradictory, since oil's stickiness is exactly the risk that could reverse the disinflation trade. Friday's retail sales, still unreleased as of this writing, is the next data point that tests which read holds. [DEVELOPING] For a desk positioned around energy and rates simultaneously, the trade this morning isn't "pick a side" — it's recognizing that both markets are pricing genuinely different information sets (crude on macro-and-inflation data, the strait's physical risk on incident-by-incident escalation) that happen to be moving in opposite directions this week, and that the two will only reconverge once one set of data stops updating faster than the other. [read]

Politics

The House-Senate funding standoff sharpened this week rather than resolving. [NEW] The Senate passed its own continuing resolution 90-6 before recessing, funding the government through Dec. 11 and including negotiated riders — blocking a proposed rule expanding political appointees' role in federal grant review, and barring fund transfers to Border Patrol. But the House, before its own recess, had already passed a narrower version funding only through Dec. 4 with none of the Senate's carve-outs, opposed by most House Democrats. The chambers now hold two incompatible bills with both out of session into September, leaving reconciliation undone heading toward the Sept. 30 fiscal year-end — a wider gap than the "House still has to act" framing carried last issue suggested. The practical read for the desk: neither chamber can amend or conference the other's bill until members are back in Washington, which pushes any resolution mechanism into a compressed post-Labor-Day window rather than the calmer run-up investors had been pricing, and raises (without yet confirming) the odds of a stopgap-on-a-stopgap rather than a clean reconciliation. [read] Tuesday's Florida, Alaska and Wyoming primaries are the next election-calendar item inside the 10-day window, unrelated to the funding fight.

Economics

July's CPI (BLS, Wed 8:30a ET) rose 0.1% MoM, 3.4% YoY headline; core rose 0.2% MoM, 2.5% YoY — a second straight month of cooling, with shelter (+0.1%, roughly two-thirds of the monthly gain) still the primary driver and energy down 1.5% MoM despite being up 14.7% YoY on the year's earlier gasoline spike. [fact] Thursday's PPI came in even softer: final demand flat MoM against a 0.2% consensus, with the annual rate falling to 4.7% from 5.5% in June as energy costs eased. [fact] Jobless claims ticked up to 209,000 for the week of 8/8 (vs. 202,000 consensus), but continuing claims fell to 1.777 million and the four-week moving average has held below 200,000 for a second straight week — a historically tight labor market cooling at the margin, not deteriorating. [fact] Together, the two soft inflation prints are why September hike odds have fallen from the roughly 60% flagged last issue (see Lead); today's July retail sales and preliminary UMich sentiment — both unreleased as of press time — are the next test of whether consumer spending is cracking under gas and grocery pass-through even as headline inflation cools. Consensus for retail sales ex-auto is +0.3% MoM (Dow Jones survey); a miss on the downside alongside soft CPI/PPI would strengthen the disinflation-plus-slowing-demand case, while a beat would reopen the "sticky services, resilient consumer" argument that's kept the Fed cautious about cutting further. [read] Both prints land two weeks ahead of Warsh's Aug. 28 Jackson Hole keynote — his first as chair — and will shape how much room he has to sound dovish without the bond market calling it premature, the same skepticism flagged after his hawkish-shock July meeting. [DEVELOPING]

Geopolitics

The UAE's response to Thursday's second ADNOC attack was more formal than after the first: Abu Dhabi's foreign ministry invoked UN Security Council Resolution 2817 by name, casting the strike as a violation of an international freedom-of-navigation guarantee rather than a bilateral shipping incident. [NEW] That's a shift in register from last issue's UAE response to the Kurdistan consular drone strike (material damage, no injuries, no resolution invoked) — Abu Dhabi appears to be building a documented, resolution-referenced case rather than reacting incident by incident, which will matter if the Saudi-Pakistan-Turkey defense pact signed last week is ever tested. No further movement this run on that pact, the Joint Chiefs off-ramp report, or the Houthi Jazan-refinery claim — all remain as previously reported and single-sourced where flagged. [continuity] The practical read for the region: a UAE that's now citing UN resolutions by number is laying groundwork for a multilateral response track distinct from whatever bilateral U.S.-Iran talks produce, which matters for how quickly (or slowly) Gulf shipping risk could normalize even if Washington and Tehran eventually strike a deal — insurers underwrite the physical risk, not the diplomacy, and a resolution-referenced dispute is a slower one to close than a bilateral spat. [read]

Iran & Hormuz

The insurance market's structural shift — six P&I clubs now refusing Hormuz war-risk cover outright, reported last issue — hasn't reversed; straits.live's 30.0x multiple and ~$10.0M per-VLCC figure are unchanged since Tuesday despite zero new strikes being logged between issues, consistent with the standing read that premiums fall only after sustained calm, not merely from an absence of new incidents. [read, continuity] Thursday's two attacks reset that clock rather than testing it — with a drone strike and a second ADNOC incident inside roughly 24 hours, the "sustained calm" window insurers would need to re-underwrite risk hasn't opened at all this week. [read] Dark-tanker activity ticked up to 58 vessels in the last 24 hours against a 40.4 seven-day average (straits.live) — one channel operators use to keep cargo moving without visible war-risk exposure, though by definition it isn't captured in any of the transit counts on the Board. [fact] The gap between that dark-fleet count and the single-digit visible-transit figures IMF PortWatch and straits.live both report is itself informative: more vessels appear willing to move cargo off-AIS than are willing to transit openly under the current risk pricing, which suggests the bottleneck is specifically insurable, visible passage rather than physical capacity or shipper demand. [read] None of this bears on the political dispute over who controls the strait (see Lead); it's a separate, slower-moving track that will likely lag any political resolution by weeks even if one arrives.
