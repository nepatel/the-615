The 6:15, for Saturday, September the twenty-sixth. Here's what moved overnight: the Wall Street Journal is reporting that President Trump has rejected Iran's plan to reopen the Strait of Hormuz, and has told his own aides he expects American bombing to resume once the midterms are over.

Quick sweep of the board before we get into it. Oil eased into the weekend, with Brent down around two percent to just above a hundred and four dollars and WTI down a similar amount to the low nineties, even as the ten-year Treasury held near five and a fifth percent, a level we haven't sustained since before the two thousand eight financial crisis; stocks actually closed out the week higher, with the Dow, the S&P, and the Nasdaq all up on Friday; gold slipped back under forty-three hundred; gas ticked up a couple pennies to about four-forty-nine a gallon nationally; and on Hormuz, the numbers everybody's watching — ship transits, the dark tanker fleet, war-risk insurance — are basically unchanged from earlier this week, which itself is telling given what just happened diplomatically. Looking ahead, the calendar gets busy fast: August's PCE inflation reading lands Wednesday, the first one since the Fed's rate hike two weeks ago; Nike reports earnings Thursday; and the September jobs report is out a week from today, next Friday.

So, the lead. Iran's foreign minister, Abbas Araghchi, stood up on the sidelines of the UN General Assembly Thursday and put a real offer on the table: a seven-day sequence where the US lifts its naval blockade, waives sanctions on Iranian oil, and agrees to a ceasefire that covers Lebanon too — and in exchange, the strait reopens to shipping on day seven, followed immediately by real nuclear talks. This wasn't a vague gesture. It tracked closely to the fourteen-point deal the two sides actually signed back in June and then let lapse, just compressed way down, from sixty days to seven. Araghchi told reporters Friday, in his words, everything is now prepared if the American side is serious.

According to the Journal, Trump said no. And more than that, he told people around him privately that he expects the bombing campaign to start back up once the midterm elections are behind him, on November third. Nobody in the administration has confirmed or denied this on the record, so treat it as a single, well-sourced report rather than settled fact. But if it holds up, here's why it matters: this was the most complete off-ramp anyone has put forward since this war started back in February. Taking it off the table means the market has no real reason to unwind the risk it's been pricing in — that seven-and-a-half to ten percent war-risk insurance premium on tanker hulls, the fact that most ships still crossing are running dark. It also means whatever oil-price relief we saw this week didn't come from diplomacy at all — it came from Saudi Arabia getting its East-West pipeline partly running again after those drone strikes, a physical workaround, not a political breakthrough.

And there's a timing question worth just saying out loud instead of dancing around: if this reporting is right, whether American planes go back into the sky over Iran is now, apparently, on an election calendar rather than a battlefield one. That's happening the same week Trump hosted Xi Jinping for a state visit that produced a lot of pageantry and very little else — pandas for the Atlanta Zoo, scholarships for a hundred thousand American students, but no deal on Taiwan, no deal on AI, nothing new on Iran beyond the trade truce extension we already told you about earlier this week. And it's happening against a Federal Reserve backdrop that's already hawkish — that five-plus percent ten-year, a rate-setting meeting at the end of October, an inflation reading due this Wednesday — which means if strikes do resume and oil spikes, it lands on a central bank with very little room to shrug it off.

A couple of other threads. France, with Washington's backing, is now drafting a new UN Security Council resolution to authorize an international naval mission to protect shipping through the strait — that's attempt number two, after Russia and China vetoed a similar plan back in April. Separately, something like eighty countries signed onto a joint statement at the UN this week demanding the strait reopen and condemning the attacks on shipping, so the diplomatic pressure is building even as the bilateral track just collapsed. And on the tech side, Trump and Xi reportedly could not find common ground on artificial intelligence during the summit either — no word on exactly what the sticking point was, but no agreement means the existing chip export rules just stay put for now, which itself is the story heading into next week's big earnings names.

Quickly to sports. The Yankees split a doubleheader with Baltimore Friday to close the regular season, taking the opener in a rout of a loss and coming back to win the nightcap — by our own count that puts them at ninety-two and sixty-eight, and they'll host the wild card series opener Tuesday, opponent still not locked down as of this taping. The Knicks made no moves overnight; their preseason schedule is official now, opening October fifth down in Philadelphia. And Northwestern lost a tight one at fifth-ranked Indiana, twenty-nine to twenty-three, falling to two and one on the young season.
