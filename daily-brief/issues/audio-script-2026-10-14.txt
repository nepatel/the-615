Quick sweep of the board. Now the lead. A Houthi missile hit Riyadh's King Khalid International Airport this past Saturday, killing at least a dozen people and wounding more than three hundred. The airport's been closed ever since — Saudi aviation authorities have it shut through at least the seventeenth — and the attacks didn't stop. Tuesday brought more strikes on that airport and on Abha's, further south, with missile debris also hitting residential parts of Riyadh, damaging homes and, reportedly, a kindergarten and a medical complex. And today, a Houthi strike reportedly hit an Aramco building in the kingdom's eastern province, with Saudi authorities reporting several wounded. The Houthis say they're not done — they're promising to keep hitting Saudi airports and oil facilities in the days ahead. The airline fallout is real: Lufthansa, Air France, KLM, flydubai and others have all cancelled Riyadh flights into next week or beyond, Delta's pushed back a planned new Atlanta-to-Riyadh route by months, and over a third of Saudi Arabia's scheduled flights were cancelled in a single day earlier this week.

This didn't come out of nowhere. The Saudi-led coalition struck Houthi positions in Sanaa, Yemen's capital, earlier this month, and the Houthis — through their own state media, unverified — claim the Saudis hit them more than a hundred times in a single day. The Saudis call the Houthis' version of events misleading. What's clear either way: Saudi Arabia is investigating why its air defenses failed to stop Saturday's barrage, and it's promising to hit back hard. The Houthis, meanwhile, are threatening to go after Aramco's headquarters directly if the Sanaa strikes continue, and they're warning the US to stay out of it.

That threat is landing inside an already tense standoff with Iran. The US has a third aircraft carrier headed to the region, on top of two already on station. Trump told a rally crowd this week the US may have, quote, gone too far in killing Iranian leaders earlier in this war — his first real second-guessing of that approach reported here. He's also said he doesn't think China or Russia are helping Tehran, despite reports the two have been sharing intelligence for attacks on US troops. None of that changes his pledge from last week to hold off on new Iran strikes until after the midterms on November third — a pledge that itself came just a day after reports he'd told aides he didn't want a deal at all, even as the Pentagon was said to be preparing for a much larger bombing campaign if talks collapsed.

Here's the read for the trade: the usual Hormuz numbers — the tanker counts, the transit data, the war-risk insurance estimates — are measuring a chokepoint story that's days, sometimes weeks, old by the time you see it. What's actually moving oil this week is something more immediate: a direct hit on Saudi aviation and now on Aramco's own property, even though the physical damage so far has reportedly been limited and nobody's confirmed an actual production outage. Brent's climbed from Friday's settle into the one-oh-six range, and WTI's up nearly four percent in five sessions — that's a market starting to price supply risk at the source, not just at the strait, and it shows up in the spread too: Brent and WTI have moved almost two dollars closer together since Friday. And worth noting: Trump said a week ago that the war could end soon and that prices would come tumbling down as a result. Oil's gone the other way since — that's a real gap between the stated expectation and what the tape's actually doing. Watch today's CPI print for competing attention, watch whether Saudi Arabia's promised firm retaliation widens the Yemen war further, and watch whether a Houthi strike on American assets or personnel forces Washington's hand before its own election-day deadline.

Both sides' casualty and strike-count claims are coming from state or proxy media and haven't been independently verified by a neutral outlet.
