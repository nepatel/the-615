## Lead: Trump claims "100%" Navy control of Hormuz; Tehran says it's still closed — and oil is jumping anyway

President Trump told reporters in the Oval Office Monday that the U.S. Navy had swept the Strait of Hormuz clear of mines and now holds full operational control of the waterway — "the only one that has control of the Strait of Hormuz right now is the United States Navy," he said, adding the U.S. controls it "100%." Iran's position, restated the same day by Foreign Ministry spokesman Esmail Baghaei, is the opposite: the strait stays closed to commercial shipping until Washington lifts its naval blockade of Iranian ports, and Tehran has piled on more conditions — sanctions relief, a U.S. troop withdrawal from the region, release of frozen Iranian assets, and war-damage reparations. Two governments are now making flatly contradictory public claims about who controls a chokepoint that carried roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne oil before the Feb. 28 closure. The mismatch itself is the story: it signals negotiators are further from a shared floor than the "close" Oman-deal reporting from last week suggested. [fact: both governments' positions confirmed Monday; read: the contradiction is the tell]

Trump escalated separately on reparations. After Iran's Supreme National Security Council reiterated its compensation demand, Trump posted on Truth Social that Tehran's own request gave him "an interesting idea" — have U.S. negotiators instead demand Iran pay for Americans "killed and gravely wounded" in Iran-linked attacks going back "50 years," citing roadside bombings and, in his account, the 2000 USS Cole bombing, an attack U.S. investigators attributed to al-Qaeda at the time, not Iran. Treat the reframing as a negotiating maneuver to reset who owes whom rather than a serious legal claim [read] — the substantive fact is that both sides are now trading reparations demands rather than narrowing them, which is itself evidence the technical Oman shipping-lane pact from 8/5 (60-day, fee-free, split northern/southern lanes) hasn't translated into political momentum toward reopening.

Oil is pricing the standoff as unresolved, not improving. Brent added 2.53% to $89.94 and WTI 2.70% to $84.36 (both ~4:30a ET, CNBC), extending a three-plus-day gain per Bloomberg's overnight wrap. That the "control" claim from the side whose navy would benefit from cheaper, freer-flowing crude is what's driving the price up rather than down tells you which claim the market is discounting — traders are reading Trump's statement as posture rather than fact on the ground, and continuing to price disruption risk over supply normalization. [read] Prediction markets agree: straits.live puts the odds of reopening by 8/31 at 4%, unchanged from Monday, with year-end odds last tracked near 48%.

Equity markets are registering the same divergence, just more quietly. Energy majors are outperforming again — Exxon Mobil and Chevron trading near multi-year highs — while airline stocks are giving back ground as jet-fuel costs climb with crude, a split that's recurred through this war. The S&P and Nasdaq are essentially flat to up overnight (see Board), which means index-level calm is masking a real rotation underneath it, not an absence of reaction.

Second-order: this is the first real test of new Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei's authority since a full military reshuffle he announced Sunday/Monday — six new commanders, including Hossein Taeb, a Khamenei loyalist and former intelligence chief, installed atop the Basij paramilitary. The decrees called for stronger deterrence, readiness for "powerful offensive operations," and an expanded domestic intelligence footprint. Putting a loyalist atop the Basij — the internal-security-facing militia, not the naval apparatus that actually controls Hormuz — alongside offensive-operations language points toward consolidating control at home as much as posturing abroad: a leadership team built for a long war. [read] That's the frame markets are implicitly using to discount Trump's "100%" claim.

The Fed angle carries over. The crude spike lands roughly 36 hours before Wednesday's CPI print, the next real test of whether Fed Chair Kevin Warsh's hawkish framing survives contact with the data. Warsh held rates steady at his first meeting but delivered a hawkish shock — nine of 18 participants penciling in a 2026 hike, the statement's easing bias stripped out — and September hike odds have moved to roughly 60.5% on CME FedWatch, up from 38% a week ago. A sustained run-up in gasoline prices feeding through July CPI's transportation-fuel component would only add to that case, though the pass-through typically shows with a lag rather than immediately. ## Iran & Hormuz

The insurance market is where the crisis is becoming structural rather than episodic: six protection-and-indemnity (P&I) clubs have now withdrawn war-risk cover for Hormuz transits entirely, per straits.live's tracking, rather than merely repricing it — insurers exiting a market outright is a different signal than premiums moving within it. [NEW] That's consistent with Kpler's standing observation (carried in prior issues) that war-risk premiums jump fast on attacks but decline slowly through calm stretches; full normalization requires sustained calm, not just an absence of new strikes, which is why yesterday's zero-new-strikes-in-24h reading hasn't moved the 30x premium multiple at all. Dark-tanker activity — 36 vessels AIS-dark in the past 24 hours per straits.live — is one channel operators are using to keep moving cargo without visible war-risk exposure, though by definition that traffic isn't captured in the transit counts above and carries its own sanctions and safety risk. None of this bears on the political dispute over who controls the strait (see Lead); it's a separate, slower-moving track that will likely lag any political resolution by weeks even if one arrives. For chartering desks, the practical read is that the $10.0M-per-VLCC-passage cost isn't going to fall just because Washington declares the mines cleared — it falls only when insurers see enough consecutive calm days to re-underwrite the risk, and today's methodology block shows none of the three trackers is yet counting that streak.
