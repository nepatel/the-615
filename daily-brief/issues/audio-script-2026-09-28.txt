The 6:15, for Monday, September 28th. Here's what moved overnight: Iran's foreign minister just told NBC that Tehran has no reason to go back to the table with Trump, even if it comes to, quote, a doomsday war — a real hardening from Saturday's story, and it sent Brent crude jumping more than three percent, back up into the low hundreds.

Quickly, the rest of the board: Treasury yields are up again, with the ten-year a touch above five and a fifth percent and the thirty-year near five and a half, both still near two-thousand-seven highs; the dollar's near a two-month high; gold's down almost three percent to a seven-week low as real yields and the dollar outweigh the usual safe-haven bid; stock futures are actually a bit higher despite all that; Asia closed mixed overnight; gas prices are basically flat around four dollars and forty-eight cents a gallon, still near the record for this time of year; and on Hormuz, the tracked ship traffic through the strait is still essentially zero, though the count of vessels just sitting there waiting swung wildly overnight in a way nobody's explained, so don't read too much into that swing either way. Coming up this week: Wednesday brings the first inflation report since the Fed's rate hike earlier this month, and futures markets now see better than two-in-three odds the Fed hikes again in late October. Nike reports earnings Thursday. And the Yankees open their playoff series at home tomorrow night.

Here's the lead. Iran's foreign minister, Abbas Araghchi, went on Meet the Press and said Tehran sees no reason to re-engage diplomatically with this administration, given repeated American strikes — and he used that striking phrase, that Iran will stand firm, quote, even if it comes to a doomsday war. He also said Trump's public comments have been contradictory, that Iran hasn't actually gotten anything formal through the Qatari or Pakistani mediators. That's a real shift in tone from Saturday, when this was just described as a stalled channel. Now it's Iran's own foreign minister disclaiming any need to use that channel at all.

Trump, for his part, is sending mixed signals. In an Axios interview Sunday, he said Iran's current offer is something Washington might have taken a year ago, and that Tehran, quote, overplayed its hand — pretty dismissive. But in that same interview, he said he still expects talks to resume this week. Those two things don't obviously fit together, and nobody's sure which one is the real position.

Meanwhile, the human cost of this conflict came into sharper focus this morning. The Pentagon confirmed that eight Marines were hurt — smoke inhalation, possible concussions — when an Iranian missile hit a vessel they were on back on September fourteenth. That's two weeks old, and it wasn't disclosed until today. Separately, Iran's Revolutionary Guard says they captured a second American underwater drone, and the US is denying that too, same as they denied a similar claim a few weeks back.

None of this shows up yet in the actual Hormuz numbers — the ship traffic and count of stranded vessels move on a lag and haven't budged. What moved was the price of oil, on words alone. And that's really the story here: the market is pricing rhetoric, not physical reality, because right now rhetoric is the leading indicator. Oman is still quietly offering to mediate, and nobody has formally slammed that door shut, but nobody's picking it up this morning either.

Layer on top of that a Fed that already hiked once this month and might hike again in October, a record-adjacent gas price, and a war that just got rhetorically hotter on two separate fronts — that's a tougher setup than the "stalled but not dead" diplomacy story we had over the weekend. And yet stocks aren't really flinching — futures are actually green this morning right alongside the oil and yield moves, which says less about confidence the Iran story is contained and more about a market that already spent last week pricing in a war economy. We'll learn more once the actual trading session gets going.

On politics: China confirmed Monday that the trade truce with the US runs through January tenth, which Beijing's calling a stable, predictable window for more talks. Since Friday's summit wrap, we've learned some of the actual terms — tariff cuts on thirty billion dollars of goods, a new hotline for AI-related incidents between the two countries, and China agreeing to review American financial firms for branch licenses. None of that touches the Iran situation, which came up in the meetings but produced nothing concrete.

On economics: everything this week points to Wednesday morning's inflation report — the first since the Fed's hike — and futures markets have moved fast on Fed-speak alone, now pricing better than two-thirds odds of another hike in late October, up from roughly one-in-three odds before September's meeting. One forecaster has core prices up about half a percent for the month, but that number hasn't been double-checked against a second source, so hold it loosely until Wednesday's actual print.

On business: Nike reports Thursday after the close, expected to post a pretty soft quarter — analysts see earnings down double digits year over year on a mid-single-digit revenue decline — though that's a low bar the company beat easily last time out. Worth watching for any tariff commentary given the still-unsettled trade truce with China. Otherwise it's a quiet earnings week — a few dozen smaller names reporting today, thinning out through Friday — ahead of the real earnings season, which doesn't kick off until the second full week of October.

On technology: the wave of new AI model releases from the big labs continues, but nothing new broke overnight, so we're not re-covering ground here. The one hard date this morning is SpaceX's first orbital Starship flight, carrying an actual batch of Starlink satellites for the first time — that launch window opens around eight fifteen Eastern.

Geopolitics: the Houthis hit Saudi Arabia again over the weekend — missiles toward Khamis Mushait, drones toward Riyadh — the second such attack in just four days, a faster pace than we'd been seeing. The Gulf states' own regional bloc called it a serious violation of Saudi sovereignty. In response, Saudi Arabia, Pakistan and Turkey signed a new mutual-defense pact — an attack on one treated as an attack on all. That's a new structural wrinkle, separate from the US relationship with Riyadh, and worth watching alongside the Hormuz story even though the two conflicts aren't confirmed to be coordinated.

And sports. The Yankees haven't played since Friday's loss to Baltimore, and there's still that unresolved question about their exact final record from last week; they open their Wild Card series at home against the Red Sox tomorrow night, though sources can't even agree if it's an early or late start. The Knicks, the defending champions, signed a two-way rookie over the weekend and open their five-game preseason a week from today in Philadelphia, with the full slate now locked in through mid-October. And Northwestern, now two and one after losing at Indiana Friday night, hosts Penn State at home Friday.
