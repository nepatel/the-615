## Lead: A Qatari-brokered truce proposal sends Brent to its lowest close since August and stocks to their best day since April — even as a Jensen Huang espionage scandal knocks $190B off Nvidia alone

Monday's settlement data confirms what Friday's figures could only hint at: oil, not equities, is this week's story. ICE Brent settled at $92.57, down $5.75 or 5.85% on the day and its lowest close since late August, extending a slide that had already taken the benchmark down 16% the prior week — its steepest weekly drop since the pandemic began. NYMEX WTI settled at $86.85, down $3.98 or 4.38%. Both benchmarks kept falling after the bell; Brent's post-settlement losses widened past $7 a barrel. [fact] The trigger was diplomatic: President Trump said Sunday he was "highly unlikely" to order further strikes on Iran while talks progressed, and Monday, Qatar's prime minister carried an Iranian proposal to the White House that two diplomatic sources described to Israeli media as approved by Tehran — Iran would reopen the Strait of Hormuz and stop attacking Gulf tankers, in exchange for the US lifting its naval and economic blockade and releasing $8 billion in Iranian funds frozen in Qatar. Neither government has confirmed those terms, and Secretary of State Marco Rubio said the same day that Iran's demands are "outrageous and ridiculous" and that the sides are "not close" to a breakthrough; Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick and Secretary of War Pete Hegseth are all reported to oppose a deal on these lines. [fact, single-sourced on the deal's specific terms; flag]

Equities read the same news as unambiguous relief. The S&P 500 closed up 2.35% to 7,549.94, its best single session since April 8 and enough to claw back most of last week's worst-since-April-2025 selloff; the Dow added 1,104.02 points (+2.15%) to 52,389.75, and the Nasdaq Composite gained 2.74% to 25,253.14. Every S&P sector finished green, led by industrials (+4.59%) and basic materials. [fact] The mechanism is straightforward even if the deal itself is not: a cheaper, more secure oil supply lowers a direct input cost across the economy and defuses the geopolitical tail risk that has been the single largest source of headline volatility for two months, and Monday's rally reads as dip-buyers acting on that logic a session after October's blowout jobs report had already done most of the work of calming the AI-capex panic that drove last Thursday's rout. [read] It is also, on the numbers, a fragile relief rally rather than a resolved one: a nearly identical US-Iran memorandum collapsed in Switzerland in May after an Iranian tanker attack in the strait, shipping was attacked again over the weekend on both the US and Iranian telling, and the Houthis claimed a strike on Saudi Aramco's Riyadh offices — none of which stopped Monday's rally, which is itself informative about how thin the market's conviction in the "escalation" case had become even before Monday's news. [read] Supply-side data reinforces that the selling is not purely diplomatic: a Reuters survey put OPEC's 11 members at 22.87 million barrels a day in October, up 1.38 million from September and led by Saudi Arabia, Kuwait and Iraq; JPMorgan estimated an October global surplus of roughly 1.4 million barrels a day; and the US Strategic Petroleum Reserve fell to 279.5 million barrels, its lowest since October 1982, per Energy Department data. [fact] Gelber & Associates' read — that "shipping and regional security risks remain unresolved" even as traders price a less disruptive outcome — is the single best one-line summary of Monday's gap between the physical risk and the price action.

The session's other big mover ran on an entirely separate track. Nvidia closed down 4.27% at $178.67, erasing roughly $190–$192 billion in market value, after the company confirmed its board has opened an independent investigation into CEO Jensen Huang following reports that Alice Huang — a businesswoman US intelligence reportedly assesses as a Chinese asset — had an affair with him and may have been using it to target him for foreign intelligence purposes. Huang admitted the affair in a staff memo and apologized; the board says it has no reason to believe trade secrets or national security were compromised but will examine all available information, and that Huang will remain CEO throughout. [fact, multi-sourced: CNBC, Bloomberg, WaPo, NBC, CBS] That Nvidia's drop barely dented the broader rally — AMD closed up 13.26%, Broadcom up 8.78% — is itself a tell: Monday's buying was a macro, not a stock-specific, story. See Technology for the Huang investigation's particulars and political fallout.

## Iran & Hormuz

Beyond the Lead and Board: the proposed terms — Iran reopening the strait and halting tanker attacks in exchange for a US blockade lift and $8 billion in released funds — echo a US-Iran memorandum reached in Switzerland in May that collapsed within weeks after an Iranian attack on oil tankers in the strait, a precedent none of this run's reporting mentioned but one worth weighing against the current optimism. [read] Iran's Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi said Iran's offer to reopen Hormuz "within two days" still stands, conditioned on the war ending "on all fronts" — a broader demand than the narrower blockade-for-reopening trade reported elsewhere, suggesting the two sides may not yet be negotiating from the same term sheet. [fact, single-sourced] Market pricing itself offers a data point: Dennis Kissler of BOK Financial attributed Monday's selling specifically to oil "continuing strong through the Strait of Hormuz" even before any deal closes, and Morgan Stanley noted physical Dated Brent has fallen below the nearby futures contract — a structural signal of physical-market weakness distinct from the diplomatic news. Iraq's foreign minister separately said Baghdad reached understandings with Washington allowing Iraqi exports through a Hormuz shipping lane near Oman, adding to supply-side pressure on price independent of any ceasefire. [fact] None of this shows up yet in this brief's lagging trackers: PortWatch's transit count and straits.live's dark-tanker screen are both unchanged from last issue (see Board), underscoring that Monday's price move is a bet on the diplomacy holding, not yet a confirmed change in flows.
