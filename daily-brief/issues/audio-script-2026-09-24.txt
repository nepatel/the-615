The 6:15, for Thursday, September 24th. Here's what moved overnight: the ten-year Treasury yield closed at just over five point one percent Wednesday, the highest since July of 2007, after a scorching hot business-activity survey reignited fears the Fed isn't done hiking.

Here's the lead. Three weeks ago the Fed, under its new chair Kevin Warsh, raised rates a quarter point — the first hike since 2023. This week, the data caught up to that decision in a big way. Wednesday's flash survey of business activity came in red hot: the composite reading hit its best level since mid-2021, manufacturing jumped to its best since 2022, and services had their fastest growth in more than five years. New orders grew at the fastest clip in over four years, and hiring picked up to its best pace since early 2021. Then this morning, jobless claims came in well below what economists expected, with the four-week average also easing — another sign the labor market just isn't cooling the way a lot of people expected it to by now.

The bond market did not like any of this. The ten-year jumped more than a dozen basis points intraday Wednesday to close just above five point one percent, the highest since 2007, and the five-year crossed five percent for the first time since then too. Traders are now pricing roughly seventy percent odds of another quarter-point hike at the end-of-October Fed meeting — that would be back-to-back hikes, a sharp turnaround from where things stood just a few months ago, when cuts were still the expectation. And this lines up with what the Fed itself signaled in its own projections: one more hike this year, a pause through next year, and no cuts until 2028 or so. The next real test is the Fed's favorite inflation gauge, due next Wednesday — the first inflation data since the September hike.

Stocks did not take the yield spike well. The S&P fell three quarters of a percent Wednesday, the Nasdaq dropped over one percent, and the Dow lost more than three hundred points. Futures are pointing lower again this morning too, with today's Trump-Xi summit adding another variable for traders to digest before the bell. Here's the thing worth sitting with: this hawkish repricing is hitting hardest at the long end of the yield curve, not the short end — that's a different dynamic than the last hiking cycle. If growth keeps running hot into an economy that's already near full employment, there's room for long-term rates to keep climbing before next week's inflation print gives everyone a cleaner read on what's actually driving this.

One important nuance, though: dig into that business survey and it's at least partly an inflation story, not just a growth story. Input costs — fuel, transport, wages — rose at their fastest pace in about four years. So some of this "hot" reading is really a cost-push story, prices rising through supply chains, not purely stronger demand. Worth remembering that distinction before assuming the bond market's move this week is simply "the economy's on fire."

On politics and the China summit: Xi Jinping landed outside Washington Wednesday evening for his first state visit to the U.S. in eleven years, and Trump met him right at the plane — apparently the first time in about six decades a U.S. president has greeted a foreign leader at the airport like that, popes aside. Today brings the formal arrival ceremony at the White House late morning, then a state dinner tonight with some tech CEOs reportedly on the guest list, plus a military flyover and a joint visit to see the Declaration of Independence and the Constitution at the National Archives. And we already have one concrete outcome out of this summit: the Treasury Secretary announced this morning that the U.S. and China have agreed to extend their trade truce by two months, out to January tenth. That takes a November tariff cliff off the table for now, though it's a delay, not a resolution — the underlying fights over tariffs, rare earths, and tech controls are still unresolved. Taiwan, critical minerals, and technology are expected to be the big topics in today's actual meeting, with Iran and Ukraine likely to come up too, though nobody's expecting a breakthrough on either of those today specifically.

On the business side, that Saudi pipeline restart is the more concrete story behind some of this week's early relief in oil prices, before the yield spike took over as the bigger market story. It's a four-million-barrel-a-day pipeline that lets Saudi crude skip the Strait of Hormuz entirely, and it's back online after being knocked out by drone attacks earlier this month — though it's running at reduced capacity and could take weeks to get back to full strength. Also worth flagging: earnings season picks back up next week, with Nike reporting Thursday and a whole cluster of big names — Broadcom, Adobe, Oracle, Micron, Costco, and others — all reporting around the same window.

On Iran and the Strait of Hormuz: the most direct U.S.-Iran contact since the war started happened Tuesday in New York — a three-hour meeting, mediated by Qatar, between U.S. envoys and Iran's foreign minister. Iranian state TV framed it as Iran laying out its conditions to reopen the strait: lift the naval blockade, unfreeze Iranian assets, end the war on every front. That's an opening position, not an agreement, and Trump himself said there's no breakthrough yet, just another meeting coming soon. None of our actual traffic numbers through the strait have moved yet to back up any real progress — transit counts are still scraping along near record lows, and war-risk insurance is still elevated. So for now, treat this as a conversation, not a resolution — the real relief in oil markets this week came from that Saudi pipeline, not from Hormuz itself reopening.
