## Lead: Warsh's first Jackson Hole keynote turns hawkish; markets reprice for a September hike in a single session

Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh delivered his first Jackson Hole keynote Friday, and the market's read was unambiguous: hawkish. "The responsibility for 65 months of sustained, elevated inflation sits squarely with the central bank," Warsh told the Economic Policy Symposium, adding that while "this summer's readings were better than expected, they do not tell me that underlying trends have meaningfully improved." He said the Fed's "predominant focus right now should be on prices," with inflation — by the Fed's preferred gauge, running near 3.7% over the past year — still "above our 2% target," even as he judged the economy at "full employment" and said he was "impressed" by its overall performance. [NEW]

Mechanism: Warsh again declined to offer forward guidance, framing his approach as "a discipline, not a decision" — but traders didn't need a decision to reprice. CME FedWatch odds of a 25bp hike at the Sept. 16 FOMC meeting jumped to 57-59% from 34-35% Thursday, the sharpest single-day repricing of Fed expectations since Warsh took the chair in May. The 2-year Treasury yield jumped roughly 12bp on the session to 4.38%, a move Yahoo Finance's markets desk called the largest one-day, post-Jackson-Hole-speech move in the yield this century. The 10-year rose a smaller 4bp to 4.72%, and the 30-year barely moved, to 5.21% — a bear-flattening pattern consistent with markets pricing near-term tightening without revising up long-run inflation or growth expectations. [NEW]

Equities gave back Thursday's Nvidia-driven gains: the S&P 500 fell 0.25% to 7,711.76, the Nasdaq dropped 0.52% to 26,402.42, and the Dow eased 0.02% to 53,559.99 — a session Sevens Report's Tom Essaye had flagged in advance as a "solid pullback" risk if Warsh failed to offer clarity on the rate path. He didn't, deliberately: Warsh's framework rejects the forward-guidance regime his predecessors used, on the theory that it constrains the Fed's flexibility to respond to incoming data. The VIX barely moved (14.43, -0.55%), and the dollar index firmed to 99.47 (+0.31%) — a muted-vol, stronger-dollar reaction consistent with a hawkish repricing rather than a growth scare. [read: the market isn't pricing a policy mistake, it's pricing a policy fight — Warsh is the first Fed chair of this cycle to treat "full employment" as license to hike rather than a reason for caution, and traders are taking him at his word rather than assuming he's bluffing into a still-soft labor market.]

The second-order consequence is where this collides with Thursday's jobless-claims data and Bessent's Treasury buybacks. Initial claims for the week ended Aug. 22 came in at 203,000, below the roughly 208,000 consensus and the prior week's upwardly revised 207,000 — a labor market still showing no cracks, which is exactly the data point Warsh cited as clearing the way to treat inflation, not employment, as the binding constraint. [fact] That leaves the Sept. 4 jobs report and the Sept. 11 CPI print as the two data points standing between here and the Sept. 16 decision — either could reverse Friday's repricing as easily as it happened. [read] Treasury Secretary Bessent's expanded long-bond buybacks, which helped pull long yields down through much of August, look increasingly at odds with a Fed chair now actively talking up the short end — a policy mismatch between the Fed and Treasury that FX desks flagged before Friday and that Friday's bear-flattener makes harder to ignore. [continuity: same tension flagged in Thursday's issue, now visible in the yield curve itself rather than just in commentary]

## Politics

Federal Reserve Governor Lisa Cook's attorney met the White House's Wednesday deadline with a reply that went on offense: rather than simply denying Trump's revived mortgage-fraud allegations, the letter cited reporting that Trump himself, Treasury Secretary Bessent and Attorney General Todd Blanche have each also claimed multiple primary residences on mortgage paperwork at the same time. [NEW] The tactic doesn't change the legal posture — Cook still needs the allegations found meritless or the removal effort blocked again, as the Supreme Court did once already this year, with argument now set for January — but it raises the political cost of a second removal attempt built on conduct the White House's own officials share. No response from the White House had surfaced as of press time. The timing lands the same week as Warsh's Jackson Hole debut, crowding two separate Fed-credibility stories — one about who sets rates, one about who gets to stay on the board — into the same news cycle without either resolving.
