Okay, the lead. Sometime before dawn Gulf time today, Yemen's Houthis flew a drone into Saudi Aramco's Jazan refinery. It caught fire, the fire's out, nobody was hurt — it's a four-hundred-thousand-barrel-a-day plant, so this wasn't nothing, but it also wasn't catastrophic. A separate Houthi strike on the Yemeni port of Mokha, earlier the same day, killed at least seven people. The Houthis say both were payback for Saudi strikes on two Yemeni provinces; the Saudis deny they're besieging anybody. Neither of these strikes actually hit the Strait of Hormuz — they're a second front, over in the Red Sea and the Bab el-Mandeb approach, and that front now has its own separate insurance and risk pricing from the Gulf side.

Quick recap of how we got here, for anyone tuning in fresh: this whole thing traces back to February 28th, when the U.S. and Israel opened an air campaign against Iran and killed its supreme leader. Iran responded by effectively shutting Hormuz to normal commercial traffic, and it's stayed shut — today's day one-sixty-two of that closure. That's the backdrop every one of these numbers sits on top of.

Here's why that matters right now specifically. Treasury Secretary Bessent told CNBC this weekend there's a real chance of a deal to reopen Hormuz in the next day or two. But Iran's messaging cuts the other way — their security establishment says traffic doesn't normalize until the U.S. pulls troops out, ends the war, and pays compensation, full stop. And there's a separate, harder sticking point: Iran wants to negotiate a per-ship toll with Oman for transit, and the White House has called that a flat no. So you've got Washington saying imminent, and Tehran saying nothing's even really being negotiated. That gap has been open for over a week now without anybody producing a signed piece of paper.

My read: the Houthi escalation is pressure, not a new war. Widening the Red Sea front raises the cost of any outcome that isn't favorable to Iran's side of the table, right as the Hormuz talk turns more constructive — and it's telling that war-risk insurance hasn't come down even during the more optimistic negotiating windows. If insurers aren't repricing risk downward, that's the market telling you not to get ahead of the diplomacy. Friday's oil settle — Brent down almost two percent to right around eighty-two — mostly reflected the weak U.S. jobs number, not the Gulf. The number that'll actually tell you something is Monday's open, once traders can price in both the refinery strike and the Bessent comments together.

Worth a word on why you're hearing two different transit counts in different places, because it's not sloppiness, it's two trackers measuring two different things. One satellite-tracking outfit puts actual crossings at two ships on a single day earlier this month, against a normal baseline in the seventies — but that data lags and only updates weekly. A separate commercial tracker, Kpler, counted five tankers in a single twenty-four hour stretch, against a pre-war average north of twenty a day. Neither one is wrong; one's counting all vessel traffic, the other's counting tankers specifically, on different publishing schedules. Both point the same direction — call it a ninety percent-plus collapse in throughput either way — but you shouldn't average them or treat either as the definitive number. And on top of that, a big chunk of the ships that are still moving — something like six in ten since a renewed blockade started in mid-July — are doing it with their transponders switched off, so-called dark transits, which means even those counts are probably a floor, not a ceiling.

And there's a second-order story underneath all this: gold's rally to a two-month high and the dollar's slide were mostly about Friday's ugly payrolls report, but if Hormuz reopening stalls or reverses, you'll get safe-haven buying and inflation-hedge buying stacking right on top of that already-dovish Fed trade. That's exactly the tension Wednesday's CPI print is going to have to referee.

Unemployment ticked down, but that's because fewer people are looking for work, not because hiring picked up. That's the setup heading into Wednesday's inflation print.

On business and tech: Amazon is reportedly committing something like two hundred billion dollars to AI infrastructure this year alone — that capex number keeps climbing, not leveling off, and Bezos has been framing it as the bet that defines the company's next decade. AMD's data center business more than doubled last quarter, revenue up fifty percent year over year. A smaller connectivity chipmaker, Astera Labs, more than doubled its revenue too, which tells you the AI buildout is still lifting the whole supply chain, not just the headline GPU makers. And Anthropic is said to be building out its own custom-silicon team instead of relying purely on outside chip suppliers — one more sign the frontier labs want to own more of their own hardware stack rather than rent all of it.

On geopolitics beyond the lead: Saudi Arabia, Turkey, and Pakistan signed a mutual-defense pact in Mecca this past week — an attack on one is treated as an attack on all three. That's a real new alliance, not just a photo op, given it pairs the top oil exporter with NATO's second-biggest military and a nuclear power. And separately, Lebanon is accusing Israeli troops of moving into a village that was supposed to have been handed back to the Lebanese army last month.
