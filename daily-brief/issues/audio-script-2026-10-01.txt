The 6:15, for Thursday, October 1st. Here's what moved overnight: the Fed's favorite inflation gauge came in a lot cooler than expected, and traders slashed the odds of an October rate hike by half in a single day — but oil went up anyway, because the Strait of Hormuz just had its worst night of tanker attacks in weeks. Those two stories are fighting each other for control of the market this morning, and nobody's won yet.

Quickly, the rest of the board: crude's up around two percent on both benchmarks with Brent near a hundred and one and WTI near ninety-two, no settle yet; the ten-year and thirty-year are basically flat around five and a third and five and two-thirds percent; the dollar index firmed a bit; gold's pushing toward forty-two hundred; Tokyo jumped more than three percent overnight while London, Frankfurt and Paris are all lower in early trading; the Hormuz vessel count that's been stuck around two hundred and forty ships suddenly dropped to around one-thirty-five overnight, which is probably noise, not signal; and tonight the Yankees play a win-or-go-home Game Three against the Red Sox.

Now the lead. Yesterday morning, the Fed's preferred inflation measure — the PCE price index for August — came in soft across the board. Headline inflation ran three and four tenths percent year over year, well under the three point seven expected. The core number, which strips out food and energy, came in at three percent, also under forecast and down from three point three the month before. On the surface, that's unambiguously good news for anyone hoping the Fed is done raising rates.

Two caveats worth knowing, though. Part of that improvement comes from a methodology change the government made in how it measures certain prices — a change that got applied retroactively back to twenty twenty-one, which muddies any clean read on the underlying trend. And separately, the savings rate fell to its lowest level in almost four years, meaning households are spending more than they're earning even as prices cool. That's not really what a healthy soft landing looks like.

Markets reacted fast. The probability of a quarter-point hike at the Fed's late-October meeting, which traders had priced at somewhere around two-thirds to three-quarters just two days ago, collapsed to roughly a third. That's one of the biggest one-day swings in rate expectations we've seen all year.

Here's the thing, though — oil didn't go along with the story. If this were a clean, dovish inflation surprise, you'd expect crude to ease along with bond yields. Instead, both Brent and West Texas Intermediate are up more than one and a half percent this morning. Why? Because the Strait of Hormuz had its most violent night in weeks. The UK's maritime monitoring agency reported three separate tankers hit by unidentified projectiles — a crude carrier struck on its side, another tanker hit while heading in, and an LNG tanker also hit. Nobody was hurt in any of the three incidents, and nobody's officially claimed responsibility, though there are unverified claims online pointing at Iran. Take those with real skepticism — they're not confirmed.

What this tells you is that oil right now isn't trading on the Fed's inflation data at all — it's trading on Hormuz risk. And that's why bond yields barely budged and the dollar actually firmed a little, even after a inflation report that should have pulled both lower. Energy-linked inflation fears are offsetting the good news almost in real time. Think of it as two separate markets pricing two separate stories simultaneously: the rates market is pricing the Fed getting more dovish, while the oil market is pricing a shipping lane that just had its most violent night in weeks. Normally those two markets talk to each other. This morning, they're barely on speaking terms.

Why does this matter for the end of October? A Fed that was leaning toward hiking again on stubborn, oil-driven inflation now has real cover to stand pat if this cooling trend holds through the mid-October CPI report and the next PCE report in late October. But if Hormuz attacks keep pushing oil higher between now and then, the case for another hike could come right back — just through a different channel than the one that just eased. Tomorrow's jobs report, tonight's Nike earnings, and tonight's decisive Yankees game are all smaller stories layered on top of this same forty-eight-hour window. The thing to actually watch: whether a counter-proposal the Trump administration sent to Iran yesterday through Qatari mediators produces any real sign of de-escalation before the weekend. Until it does, it's the Strait of Hormuz, not the Federal Reserve, setting the tone for markets right now.

On economics: today brings the September manufacturing survey at ten eastern, which the Fed will read as a secondary check on whether tariff costs are feeding into prices. Tomorrow's jobs report is the bigger one — the Fed will be watching whether a cooling labor market is backing up the cooling inflation story, or whether hiring stays strong enough to keep a hike alive for late October. September CPI lands October fourteenth, producer prices the day after — both carrying more weight now given how far yesterday's single data point moved the odds.

On business: Nike reports fiscal first-quarter results after the close today. Consensus sits around forty-four cents a share on eleven and a third billion in revenue, down double digits and down slightly from a year ago. The number to watch isn't the headline — it's the tariff guidance. Roughly half of Nike's footwear comes out of Vietnam, which faces a forty-six percent tariff rate, versus eighteen percent for China-sourced goods. The company already said tariffs would cost it a billion dollars gross this year and raised its margin-hit estimate once already, from around seventy-five basis points to about a hundred twenty. A second increase tonight would be the real story — and it would land in a market that's already nervous about where consumer spending power stands heading into the holidays.

On geopolitics: Russia hit Ukraine overnight with around fifty attack drones and five missiles. Most were shot down, but strikes still caused damage and injuries in several regions, including Kharkiv. Nothing new to report on the Saudi-Houthi front — that situation remains stable since late September.

On Iran and the Strait of Hormuz: beyond last night's three tanker strikes, the big diplomatic news is that Washington sent Tehran a formal counter-proposal yesterday through Qatari mediators. Iran's foreign minister took it to his own cabinet. Neither side has said what's in it. This follows Trump's public rejection last week of Iran's own plan to reopen the strait within seven days, and Iran's insistence this week that it won't back down from its conditions while still wanting real negotiations rather than renewed war. Whether this new counter-proposal actually addresses the core disagreement — who makes concessions first — isn't known yet. We'll know more as this develops.

The Knicks have no game today; their preseason opens Monday in Philadelphia. And Northwestern, sitting at two and one, hosts Penn State Friday night in the home debut of their new stadium.
