## Lead: AI-capex panic, then a jobs-data rescue — Thursday's selloff erases roughly $3 trillion in market cap, worst session since April 2025, before Friday's blowout jobs report helps claw back a third of the damage

Thursday was a crowded-trade unwind with a macro label attached. The Dow fell more than 1,800 points (-3.4%), the S&P 500 dropped 4.4% and the Nasdaq Composite fell 5.5% — the worst single session for US equities since April 2025 — erasing roughly $3 trillion in market capitalization in one day. [fact, NEW] The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index fell 12%; Nvidia dropped about 9%; Micron and AMD both fell more than 15% intraday before paring losses; AI-infrastructure plays Nebius, Iren and CoreWeave each fell double digits. [fact, NEW] The VIX spiked as much as 70% intraday before settling. [fact, NEW] The proximate trigger, as reported across multiple outlets, was a sudden repricing of AI-capex payback risk — JPMorgan's estimate of roughly $900 billion in global 2026 AI capital spending was widely cited as the number investors are now discounting against actual revenue, though no single confirmed catalyst (a specific downgrade, a specific disclosure) anchors the day [flag, single catalyst unconfirmed]. This brief has tracked the AI-capex-discipline debate since Alphabet's capex guide several issues back; Thursday is that debate's first full-blown equity-market event rather than a sector rotation.

Friday supplied the circuit breaker: data, not sentiment. October's jobs report, delayed by the shutdown earlier this year and finally released Friday at 8:30a ET, showed nonfarm payrolls up 165,000 against a consensus clustered between roughly 59,000 and 70,000 depending on the source (Dow Jones 59,000; Bloomberg 65,000; other estimates near 70,000) — a clean beat by any of the three. [fact, NEW] Unemployment fell to 4.1% versus 4.2% expected; August and September payrolls were revised up a combined 84,000, with September's print specifically revised to 60,000 from an earlier 29,000 estimate; average hourly earnings rose 0.4% month over month, putting wage growth near 3.1% year over year, roughly in line with inflation. [fact, NEW] Equities rallied on the print and on a one-day stabilization in the AI trade: the S&P 500 closed Friday at 7,376.86 (+0.31%), the Nasdaq Composite at 24,579.42 (+1.09%), while the Dow lagged at 51,285.73 (-0.46%) on continued chip-sector drag; Nvidia, AMD and Intel each rebounded Friday, reported in the high single to high single-digit percentage range depending on the name. [fact, NEW] VIX fell 9.01% to 25.64, still well above its pre-Thursday level. [fact, NEW] Even with Friday's bounce, the week closed net negative: the S&P 500 fell about 2.7%, the Nasdaq fell somewhere between 3.4% and 5.5% depending on which weekly window a given source used, and the Dow fell 0.9%. [fact, NEW]

The mechanism worth sitting with: this was a positioning shock, not a growth scare. The jobs data that helped stocks recover Friday describes an economy adding jobs faster than expected with unemployment falling and wages tracking inflation — the opposite of what would normally justify a 1,800-point Dow selloff two days earlier. [read] What moved Thursday was crowded, AI-capex-adjacent positioning in chips, memory and neocloud names re-rating on capex-payback anxiety that had been building across several of this brief's prior issues (Alphabet, Microsoft, Intel, Amazon's capex guides); Friday's data didn't resolve that anxiety, it just gave index-level buyers a reason to step back in a day after the crowded trade had already been flushed once. [read] The second-order consequence for the investor reader: Tuesday's CPI print (8:30a ET, Nov. 10) is now the next test of whether this stays a one-week, positioning-driven flush or becomes the opening leg of a larger AI-capex de-rating that spreads beyond the handful of names that led Thursday's drop. Watch Monday's open for whether Friday's stabilization holds or the selloff resumes; watch whether Qualcomm's stock settles the contested direction of its own earnings reaction (see Business); and watch the chip/neocloud names specifically, since Thursday's drop was concentrated there rather than broad-based.
