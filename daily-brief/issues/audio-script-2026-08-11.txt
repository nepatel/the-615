The 6:15, for Tuesday, August 11th. Here's what moved overnight: President Trump says the U.S. Navy has swept the Strait of Hormuz for mines and now controls it completely. Iran says that's not true, the strait is still closed. And oil is jumping anyway, which tells you who the market believes.

Quickly, where everything else sits this morning: Brent crude is up near ninety dollars a barrel, WTI in the mid eighties, both up around two and a half percent overnight; the ten year Treasury is a little under four and three quarters percent, and the two year's around four and a quarter; the dollar is basically flat; stock futures are a touch higher, nothing dramatic; the VIX is sitting in the mid teens; gold's just under forty-four hundred an ounce; Asian markets closed higher overnight, Europe was roughly flat yesterday; gas at the pump is just above four dollars a gallon nationally; and on Hormuz, we're on day one sixty-three of the closure, war-risk insurance is still running about thirty times normal, roughly ten million dollars to cover one supertanker for a single passage, and there were zero new confirmed strikes in the last twenty-four hours.

Now the lead. Trump told reporters in the Oval Office yesterday that the Navy has cleared the Strait of Hormuz of mines and that the United States controls it, in his words, one hundred percent. Iran's foreign ministry said the same day that's simply false — the strait stays closed to commercial shipping until Washington lifts its naval blockade of Iranian ports, and Tehran's actually added more conditions on top of that: sanctions relief, a full U.S. troop withdrawal from the region, its frozen assets released, and compensation for war damage. So you've got two governments making completely contradictory claims about who controls one of the most important chokepoints in the world for oil. And that contradiction is really the story here — it tells you the two sides are further apart than last week's reporting on a technical shipping deal with Oman made it seem.

Trump also escalated the money fight. After Iran's national security council repeated its demand for compensation, Trump posted that Iran's own request gave him, quote, an interesting idea — he wants Iran to instead pay the U.S. for Americans killed or hurt in Iran-linked attacks going back fifty years, and he even brought up the bombing of the USS Cole from the year 2000, which was actually attributed to Al-Qaeda, not Iran, at the time. Best read here: treat that as a negotiating tactic to flip the reparations conversation around, not a serious legal claim. But the bigger point stands — both sides are trading demands instead of narrowing them, which is more evidence that Oman shipping-lane deal hasn't actually built any political momentum toward reopening.

And oil is pricing this as unresolved. Brent added about two and a half percent, WTI almost three percent, both around four thirty this morning Eastern time, extending a gain that's now run three straight days. Think about that for a second — the claim that would normally be bullish for supply, the U.S. saying it controls the strait, is instead coinciding with oil going up. That means traders are calling Trump's claim posture, not fact on the ground, and they're still pricing continued disruption. Prediction markets back that up too — odds of the strait actually reopening by the end of this month are sitting at just four percent, unchanged from yesterday. Underneath the surface, energy stocks like Exxon and Chevron are near multi-year highs while airlines are getting hit by rising jet fuel costs — so even though the major indexes look calm on the surface, there's real rotation happening underneath.

One more layer: Iran's new supreme leader, Mojtaba Khamenei, just did his first major military reshuffle since taking over after his father was killed in the February strike that started this war. Six new commanders, including a close loyalist put in charge of the Basij militia, and the orders that came with it talked about readiness for, quote, powerful offensive operations. Read that as Iran's leadership settling in for a long war, both to project strength abroad and to lock down control at home — which is exactly why the market isn't buying Trump's control claim at face value.

And this lands right before Wednesday's inflation report, which is the real test this week. Odds of a September hike are now around sixty percent, up from under forty a week ago. If oil keeps climbing into Wednesday's number, that only strengthens the hawkish case, though it usually takes a month or two to show up fully in the data.
