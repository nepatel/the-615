## Lead: Oil breaks back into triple digits at its highest since May, the 10-year closes in on 5% despite a Treasury intervention meant to stop it, and both land hours before the CPI print that was already this cycle's biggest

Thursday delivered the clearest inflation scare of the war so far, and it arrived on two fronts at once. ICE Brent settled at $107.63, up 5.9% on the day — its highest close since May 19, when the benchmark stood at $111.28 — while NYMEX WTI settled at $102.48, up 6.7%, also a since-May-19 high. [NEW] [fact] The move extends the escalation this issue has tracked since Tuesday-Wednesday's multi-front strikes (five Iranian tankers destroyed, Jordan's Al Azraq air base hit, Houthi strikes on Saudi refining country, a drone hit on the CPC's Black Sea terminal) working through the market with a lag, not a new flashpoint on its own. [read]

The same morning, August producer prices came in exactly at the 0.4% m/m consensus headline — "in line," on the number the market watches first — but the composition underneath was the part that moved yields. [fact] Final-demand energy rose 4.2%; diesel fuel alone jumped 24.1%, more than a third of the increase in final-demand goods; core prices (ex food, energy, and trade services) ran 4.7% higher year-over-year; stage-1 intermediate demand rose 11.3% y/y. [NEW] [fact] That is a profile of cost-push pressure building in the pipeline behind an in-line headline, and bond traders read it that way: the 10-year yield pushed to roughly 4.95-4.96%, its highest level since November 2023 and within a hair of the 5% mark that has held since then. [fact]

What makes this week's yield move different from prior inflation scares is that it happened despite an active countermeasure. The Treasury ran a $6 billion buyback of long-dated debt this week aimed explicitly at capping long yields; Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent told Steve Bannon's podcast that any suggestion the operation isn't working is "nonsense." [NEW] [fact] The 10-year kept climbing after the buyback regardless. [fact] [read] That sequencing is the more important story for a bond investor than the CPI print due at 8:30 this morning: a policy tool built to suppress term premium visibly failing to do so, in real time, suggests the market is pricing something the Fed and Treasury can't easily talk down — a mix of oil-driven cost-push inflation and fiscal-credibility concern that a benign core CPI wouldn't directly resolve, because CPI measures consumer prices, not the term-premium and supply-driven dynamics currently pushing yields. A soft print this morning would help sentiment, but it doesn't address the mechanism actually in play this week. [read]

Equities absorbed both moves badly: Thursday was the fourth consecutive losing session for all three major averages, with the Dow down 0.77% to 52,381.08, the S&P 500 off 0.45% to 7,638.95, and the Nasdaq down 0.64% to 26,253.34 — the Dow alone is down roughly 2.5% on the week entering Friday. [fact] Futures were modestly higher pre-market Friday (Dow +130 to +151 points, S&P +0.17-0.19%, Nasdaq roughly flat) as traders positioned into the CPI release, a small bounce that reads more like positioning than conviction given the size of Thursday's move. [fact] [read]

## Iran & Hormuz

The most consequential Hormuz-adjacent development this run isn't a strike but a treaty clause getting tested. Pakistan's defense minister said Sept. 9 that an Iranian attack on Saudi Arabia could activate the Mecca Joint Defence Agreement — the mutual-defense pact Pakistan, Saudi Arabia and Turkey signed Aug. 7, explicitly compared by Turkey's foreign minister to NATO's Article 5, though officials maintain it isn't directed at Iran. [NEW] [fact] That statement, on its own, doesn't change anything operationally, but it puts a three-country mutual-defense mechanism explicitly into play for the first time since the pact was signed — a genuine escalation-risk widening if Saudi Arabia is struck again after last week's Jazan-area attacks. [read] Separately, a damage assessment of the Jordan missile barrage — an A-10 with wing damage, roughly eight F-15E jets lightly damaged but serviceable — is circulating in regional and aggregator reporting; it has not been confirmed by CENTCOM or Jordan's military and should be treated cautiously.
