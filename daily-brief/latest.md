## Lead: Houthis strike Saudi Aramco's southern facilities, wounding 73 and pushing Brent to a seven-week high, as Iran moves in parallel to declare a Hormuz "exclusion zone"

Houthi forces fired drones and ballistic missiles at Saudi energy facilities in the kingdom's south early Tuesday, wounding at least 73 people and forcing operators to halt some operations, Saudi authorities said. [NEW] [fact] The Saudi-led coalition reported strikes and resulting fires across Abha, Khamis Mushait, Jazan and Najran, with the heaviest damage concentrated at Aramco installations in the Jazan area; Royal Saudi Air Force spokesman Col. Turki al-Malki pledged the coalition would "take all necessary operational measures to deter this terrorist militia," while a Houthi deputy media chief said a statement on a "large-scale military operation" inside Saudi Arabia was forthcoming. [fact] Brent crude jumped as much as 2.2% intraday to a seven-week high near $99 a barrel before easing toward the high-$97s by mid-morning Asia hours; WTI touched $94.46 before pulling back toward the low $90s. [fact] Goldman Sachs, reacting to the strike, raised its December 2026 Brent forecast by $5 to $85 and its WTI forecast to $80, citing expected Middle East shipping disruptions extending into 2027. [NEW] [fact]

The attack landed the same day Iran moved to formalize a second front in the Hormuz standoff. Maj. Gen. Mohsen Rezaei, secretary of Iran's Supreme National Security Council, posted Tuesday that Washington had "received a clear warning from Iran's new missiles" and that "economic warfare will be met by a maritime exclusion zone" running from the US Navy's blockade line into the Persian Gulf, with the "operational posture toward US warships and bases... fundamentally recalibrated." [NEW] [fact] The zone, first previewed Monday, would let Iran warn off — or sanction-list — vessels approaching the strait without Iranian cooperation; the US State Department told Al Jazeera that Washington would take "decisive measures" against any attempt to restrict navigation and was coordinating with regional partners. [fact]

[read] Mechanically, the two events are separate: a Yemen-based, Iran-aligned Houthi strike on Saudi soil, and a direct Iranian government policy shift inside the Gulf. But markets are pricing them as compounding, because both raise the odds that energy-supply disruption spreads beyond the strait itself rather than staying contained to the six-month-old tanker war. The Houthi strike hit processing and storage infrastructure, not export capacity directly — Saudi Arabia's playbook, used after the March 2026 Ras Tanura drone strike, is to reroute exports through the Red Sea port of Yanbu when eastern or southern facilities are compromised, which cushions the immediate physical-supply hit and is a likely reason Tuesday's spike retraced from its overnight highs by the time European trading opened. What doesn't retrace as easily is the war-risk calculus: a second active front layered on top of the Hormuz confrontation makes the "temporary spike, self-limiting conflict" framing markets have leaned on since February harder to sustain. It is that repricing of persistence — not the barrel-for-barrel supply loss, which so far is modest — that is moving the curve and Goldman's forecast revision alike. [read]

[read] The more immediate second-order consequence lands on Thursday's — and Friday's — inflation math. Retail gasoline was already at $4.15 a gallon nationally before this week's move, up from the low-$3 range that prevailed before the Hormuz closure; a sustained push toward $99 Brent widens the pass-through window into Friday's CPI print and, by extension, into the Sept. 15-16 FOMC decision. That print was already the pivot point the two sides of the Fed's internal hike debate were waiting on: Gov. Waller's dovish dissent is conditioned explicitly on continued disinflation, while a hawkish majority has been treating Friday's blowout jobs report as sufficient grounds on its own. An energy-driven CPI beat would hand the hawks a second, independent argument without needing core services prices to cooperate — precisely the scenario that would be hardest for Waller's framework to absorb. [read] Equity futures reflected that read straight through the Asia session: the Nikkei fell 1.7% on a stronger yen and Mideast risk-off — a reversal of Monday's 2.1% gain — while the Kospi and Hang Seng each slipped and US index futures pointed lower into Tuesday's cash open, the first full-liquidity US session since Friday's jobs report and Saturday's Iran-US naval clash, now absorbing a third shock in four trading sessions. [fact] The insurance math underneath both stories is the same: private war-risk cover for the Gulf has been gone since March, replaced by the roughly $20B US DFC reinsurance facility that keeps VLCC premia at a finite, quotable ~40x-normal rather than unavailable at any price — a backstop built for a Hormuz-only crisis that traders are now, for the first time, pricing against a second, Saudi-soil front it was not explicitly designed to cover. [read]

## Iran & Hormuz
