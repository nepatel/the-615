## Lead: August payrolls blow past consensus; markets read the beat as bad news for the Fed's dovish wing just as Waller's own decisive data point comes into view

Friday's August jobs report landed far above expectations: nonfarm payrolls rose 162,000 against a Dow Jones consensus of roughly 53,000-55,000, the strongest monthly gain since March, while the unemployment rate held at 4.1% and average hourly earnings rose $0.10 (0.3%) to $37.75. [NEW] [fact] One wire account put July's payroll figure — originally reported as a 23,000 decline — revised up to a 21,000 gain, though that specific revision number could not be independently corroborated this run and should be treated as provisional. [flag: single-sourced]

The market's reaction was the more consequential fact. Stocks fell across the board Friday: the S&P 500 dropped 0.38% to 7,718.60, the Dow lost 271.86 points (0.51%) to 53,414.25, and the Nasdaq Composite eased 0.29% to 26,506.99 — each index giving back all or most of Thursday's Waller-driven rally in a single session. [fact] Treasury yields and the dollar moved the opposite way: the 10-year rose to roughly 4.79% from Thursday's 4.74-4.75% close, and the DXY firmed 0.21% to 99.17, extending off Thursday's low rather than continuing its Waller-triggered slide. [fact] Gold fell, though quote services disagree sharply on the size of the move — spot near $4,392/oz against December futures near $4,522, a roughly $130 gap this run could not reconcile. [fact, flag: divergent sources] Fed-funds futures did the real repricing: CME FedWatch-style odds of a hike at the Sept. 15-16 FOMC meeting rose from Thursday's near-coin-flip level to a majority favoring a hike, with individual readings this run ranging from roughly 58% to 63% depending on the source and moment sampled. [fact, flag: exact probability varies by source]

The mechanism is the same Fed split that led Thursday's issue, now running into its own logic. Governor Waller's case for holding rates in September rested on two legs: a labor market that had already shown one outright monthly decline (July's since-revised -23,000 shock) and a disinflation trend he wanted two more weeks of data to confirm before deciding. He was explicit that the Sept. 11 CPI print, not Friday's payrolls, would be his decisive input. [continuity] Friday's number doesn't touch the CPI leg of that argument directly, but it knocks out the labor-market leg that made a "cooling economy, no urgency to hike" narrative easy to sell — a 162,000 print with earnings still rising 0.3% month-over-month is not the picture Waller was implicitly leaning on when he invoked Lennon on Thursday. [read] Markets, in other words, aren't pricing a policy mistake or a recession scare; they're pricing a stronger hand for Chair Warsh's hawkish framing heading into the meeting that matters. The VIX actually fell to 14.32 on the session <span class="flag">single-sourced</span>, consistent with a repricing read rather than a growth scare.

That leaves the Sept. 10 PPI-and-claims cluster and, especially, the Sept. 11 CPI print as the two remaining inputs before the Sept. 15-16 decision — and CPI now carries more weight than it did Thursday, precisely because the jobs report took away the argument that a soft labor market alone justifies a hold. [read] A hot core CPI print a week from Friday would leave Waller with neither leg of his Thursday case standing; a soft one would still leave him needing to explain away Friday's beat to the rest of the committee. Either way, the coin flip Thursday's Waller remarks created didn't survive the weekend intact.
