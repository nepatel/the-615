## Lead: Two days from the midterms, a fake-polling scandal is complicating the only story left to price

The data underneath Tuesday's midterms took a hit this cycle that has nothing to do with Washington: on Oct. 20, a firm calling itself Wedgewood Polls admitted that nearly all of the polls it had published were fabricated, after the polling aggregator FiftyPlusOne flagged statistical irregularities and contacted the firm on Oct. 19. [fact, NEW] Wedgewood's own account framed the episode as a deliberate test of how easily a wholly invented polling operation could gain traction with the press and campaign trackers; the New York Times, which had folded 15 Wedgewood polls into its own tracking page, pulled all of them once the admission surfaced. [fact, NEW] It's the second such case this cycle — an earlier fake firm, Median Strategies, ran a similar stunt on primary-season polling, including a phony read on this year's Wisconsin Democratic gubernatorial primary. [fact, continuity] Michigan state Sen. Mallory McMorrow has said the fabricated numbers affected her own campaign; a separate former Democratic Senate candidate has made the same claim about her race. [fact/flag, attributed, not independently verified]

None of that changes what the real, surviving polling average says, which is why the scandal matters as a credibility problem rather than a data one: PollCheck's aggregate, last updated Oct. 27, has the generic ballot at Democrats 51.1%, Republicans 42.4% — a D+8.7 margin — with Reuters/Ipsos's own Oct. 26 poll of 1,164 likely voters separately putting it at 53%-44%. [fact, NEW] Trump's approval sits in record-low territory across the cycle, with PollCheck's Oct. 27 average at 34.7% approve, 62.5% disapprove. [fact, NEW] Both figures are broadly consistent with, not a reversal of, the sub-35% approval readings and high-single-digit Democratic generic-ballot edge this brief has carried since issue No. 26 — the news this week is that the edge survived a deliberate attempt to pollute the record, not that it moved.

Early voting adds a second, harder-to-fake data point: more than 30 million ballots had been cast nationally as of Oct. 26, running ahead of 2018 and 2022 turnout at the same point in the cycle, per AP and NPR-sourced reporting. [fact, NEW] The state-level pattern argues against reading that as a uniform signal, however: Texas was running about 74% ahead of its 2022 pace, Ohio roughly 30% ahead, while Iowa was down about 6% — turnout enthusiasm is geographically lumpy, not a single national wave. [fact/flag, NEW]

Read: none of this closes the gap this brief flagged in issue No. 32 between model-based and market-based election odds — one forecasting model still gives Democrats a 94.7% chance at the House and 62.1% at the Senate, while prediction-market pricing runs closer to 85% House and a near-coin-flip Senate. [read, continuity] The fabricated-polling episode gives both camps a reason to distrust the other's inputs going into Tuesday, which is exactly the kind of uncertainty a market pricing a binary outcome does not like two days out. For the investor reader, the mechanism to watch isn't who wins so much as what combination produces divided government: a Democratic House against any Senate outcome revives 2027 fiscal brinkmanship — shutdown and debt-ceiling leverage — as the default negotiating posture, while a Senate flip specifically slows the pace of Fed and judicial confirmations the administration has been pushing through on narrow margins. [read] Watch: Tuesday's results; whether any further trackers claw back polls connected to Wedgewood or Median Strategies before voters go to the polls; and Monday's market open for the first sign of pre-election positioning.
