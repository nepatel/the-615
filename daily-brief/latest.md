## Lead: Crude heads for a ~5% weekly loss as transit data undercuts the strait's "closed" framing

Brent and WTI both slid through the week into Friday's close, with crude wraps putting the weekly loss at "over 5%" and "near 5%" respectively — a bigger move than the headline Hormuz closure narrative would predict on its own. Brent traded as low as $87.98 by one quote and as high as $89.30 by another Friday, with straits.live's own snapshot (1017Z Saturday) showing $88.10; WTI's last clean print was Thursday's $83.53 official settle, with Friday trade in the $83.00-83.28 range. No single clean ICE Brent or NYMEX WTI settle for Friday was located this run [flag], so this brief is reporting the range rather than picking one. [NEW]

Mechanism: EnergyNow's Friday wrap attributed the slide explicitly to a shift in how traders are reading the crisis — "increasingly view[ing] the Iran situation as an economic/sanctions confrontation rather than an imminent threat to physical supply." [read, single-sourced] That read has actual transit data behind it, but the data itself splits by vendor in a way this brief has flagged before and now has a sharper example of. Lloyd's List Intelligence counted 114 transits through the strait Aug. 17-23 — more than 30% above the prior week — with 42 westbound crossings versus 29 the week before: a real, counted pickup in traffic. Sitting right next to it: the World Bank-backed PortWatch project's snapshot for Aug. 23 alone, just 3 vessels against an ~85/day pre-crisis baseline, or roughly 4% of normal — the same figure straits.live's live tracker was still citing as of this morning. Both numbers are real; they are not measuring the same thing. Lloyd's List is a weekly aggregate of AIS-visible transits across the whole strait; PortWatch is a baseline-indexed read of a single day's throughput. A "busier week" (Lloyd's List) and "still historically closed" (PortWatch) can both be true simultaneously, and Friday's oil market spent the session trading the direction implied by the former rather than the level implied by the latter. [NEW, fact + read]

Second-order consequence: a genuine loosening at the strait, even a partial one, matters more to the Fed right now than it does to shippers. The Sept. 11 CPI print is the last inflation read before the Sept. 16 FOMC decision that Fed Chair Warsh's Friday Jackson Hole speech repriced to 57-59% hike odds (CME FedWatch), up from roughly a third the day before. Retail gasoline was already flat on the week at $4.081/gal national average (AAA, Aug. 29) even before Friday's crude slide had time to pass through pump prices. If the Lloyd's List read holds up rather than proving a one-week blip — and this brief has no basis yet to say which it is — a further leg down in crude would be one of the few genuinely disinflationary inputs available to a Fed chair who spent Friday building the hawkish case. [read, continuity: ties directly into Saturday's Warsh lead]

The strait itself was otherwise quiet. The only attributed strike since the last issue was Wednesday's hit on the Kuwaiti-flagged tanker Al Salam II (IMO 9328168), struck above the waterline while transiting eastbound near Jazirat Tawakkul; crew safe, fire extinguished, no group has claimed responsibility (JMIC Advisory Update 090). [NEW, fact] Friday-into-Saturday again passed with no new strikes logged, extending the quiet window flagged in the last issue. Dark-tanker activity held at 65 of 255 screened vessels, and war-risk insurance was unchanged at roughly $10.0 million per VLCC transit, about 40x pre-crisis — underwriters, in other words, are not yet pricing this week's transit uptick as durable. [fact]
