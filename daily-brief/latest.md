## Lead: A hot PCE print and a new round of tariff proposals on 14 countries triggered Wall Street's sharpest intraday swing of the week — and most of it reversed by the close

Wednesday's session was the week's most violent, and its outcome is more ambiguous than Monday's reinstatement-rally headline suggested the market had settled. The Bureau of Economic Analysis's October Personal Income and Outlays report, out at 8:30 a.m. ET, showed headline PCE inflation at 3.8% year-over-year and core PCE — the Fed's preferred gauge — at 3.3%, a tenth above the roughly 3.2% consensus and up from September's reading. [fact, BEA] Personal income rose 0.7% on the month and spending rose 0.3%, both in line with recent trend. [fact, BEA] The inflation beat, however modest, was enough on its own to push December rate-hike odds to roughly 98% by one afternoon reading, up from issue No. 42's "better than 90%" snapshot five days earlier. [fact/read, single-sourced on the exact probability]

The inflation print landed the same morning the U.S. Trade Representative's office, under Jamieson Greer, proposed new Section 301 tariffs on 14 trading partners — China, the EU, Japan, India, South Korea, Taiwan and Vietnam among them — following an eight-month investigation into what USTR called unreasonable trade restrictions. [fact, USTR] The proposed caps range from 15% on the EU and Japan to 35% on Vietnam, with China facing an additional 20% levy that could double to 40% if Beijing is found to be breaching its existing trade truce with Washington; these are ceiling rates subject to a comment period closing Jan. 5, 2027 and hearings starting Jan. 25, meaning nothing is in effect yet. [fact, USTR] The proposals are explicitly designed to replace the IEEPA-based tariffs the Supreme Court struck down in February — a reminder that the administration's post-ruling tariff architecture is still being rebuilt in real time, not resolved. [fact, context]

Mechanism: the combined shock — a Fed now effectively locked into a December hike, layered onto a fresh multi-year tariff overhang on top economies — sent the S&P 500 down as much as 3.2% intraday and the Dow down more than 1,100 points (-2.2%) at its low; estimates of the Nasdaq's intraday drawdown ranged from roughly 1% to 4.6% across outlets and were not reconciled this run. [fact; divergent] Chip and AI names, which had powered Monday's Huang-reinstatement rally, led the reversal on the way down and lagged on the recovery: Nvidia closed down another 3.91%, its third straight weak session since Monday's peak. [fact] By the close, the Dow had erased nearly all of its loss to finish +0.33% at 50,182.95 — its second consecutive close above 50,000 — while the S&P (-0.25% to 7,182.78) and Nasdaq (-0.62% to 23,585.80) posted smaller net losses than the intraday swing implied. [fact]

Second-order consequence: the day's price action argues that two distinct risks are now priced separately rather than netted against each other — a tightening Fed (inflation-positive data pushes hikes, not cuts, because this cycle's inflation is running above target) and a tariff regime whose final rates, timing and retaliation risk won't be known for two more months. That combination is classically stagflationary on paper — inflationary from tariffs, growth-negative from both the tariffs and higher rates — yet the index-level recovery suggests investors are for now treating Wednesday's news as noise to buy rather than a regime change, a read this brief treats as interpretation, not settled fact. [read] The reinstated Nvidia trade, barely a week old, is the most exposed position to that read being wrong: another leg down in chip names on anything less than optimistic — holiday retail data, OPEC+'s Sunday decision, or a hot session once markets reopen Monday — would reopen the question Monday's rally was supposed to close.
