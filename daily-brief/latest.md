## Lead: Crude's fourth down day meets a missile on Riyadh

Brent is down for a fourth straight session — its longest losing streak since June — to $101.50 as of Monday morning trade, with WTI off a similar 2.4% to $97.90. [NEW] The mechanism, per Bloomberg's and Reuters' overnight wraps, is two-sided: signs that Saudi crude supply is recovering after weeks of Houthi strikes on Saudi energy infrastructure, plus investor hope for a diplomatic opening in the US-Iran standoff. [read] "Recovering" here means output restoration at Saudi facilities damaged or threatened by the Houthi campaign that ran through much of September, not a claim that the underlying conflict has cooled — a distinction the rest of this Lead argues the tape is eliding. [read] Layered on top is a lighter, faster-moving trade: equity futures firming into Thursday's Trump-Xi summit, with S&P futures up roughly 0.5% and Nasdaq futures up closer to 0.7% on preparatory US-China talks in New York over the weekend, where Treasury Secretary Bessent and Vice Premier He Lifeng discussed a new bilateral AI dialogue built around national-security guardrails, tariff cuts on select non-sensitive goods, and the trade-truce extension that is the summit's actual point — the existing truce expires in November. [NEW] Gold's pullback off Friday's record and a flat-to-slightly-lower VIX are consistent with the same risk-on read; so is a third straight advance in Asia-ex-Japan equities overnight. [read]

The complication arrived Saturday, when Saudi Arabia said Houthi forces fired a ballistic missile at Riyadh itself — the first time the Yemeni group has targeted the Saudi capital directly since this phase of fighting intensified — before it was intercepted. [NEW, multi-sourced: Al Jazeera, Houston Public Media/NPR] Saudi air defenses also thwarted attempted strikes on Bisha, Taif, Farasan and Yanbu the same day; the Houthis' Yahya Saree claimed cruise missiles, ballistic missiles and drones were all used. [fact] That is a Yemen-theater escalation, distinct from the Strait of Hormuz itself, but it runs on the same Iran-aligned axis the oil market is trying to price — and it argues against reading "Saudi supply recovering" as "the region has de-risked." [read] Iran's own position on Hormuz hardened in the same window: officials now say the strait will not fully reopen until Washington meets its commitments under an interim peace deal that has since lapsed — a more conditional, more skeptical stance than the Oman-brokered reopening claim this issue flagged as unconfirmed on Sunday. [DEVELOPING] The temporary Iran-Oman transit corridor agreed in August is a narrower, separate arrangement — reportedly beginning and ending in Iranian territorial waters, with Tehran empowered to bar American and Israeli vessels and fine violators — and was never a general reopening; conflating the two overstated how close the strait actually was to normal traffic. [continuity]

Put together, the overnight tape is pricing an easier supply picture and a friendlier US-China trade backdrop against a Gulf security picture that, on Saturday's evidence, got harder rather than easier. [read] That is the same gap this issue has flagged in prior weeks — markets and the missile count moving in opposite directions — with a new data point on each side: a fourth day of oil losses on the "supply recovering" leg, and a first-ever direct hit on the Saudi capital on the "nothing is actually settled" leg. [read] The summit itself carries its own asymmetry: US reporting frames Xi as arriving with "China's trade engine roaring," which reads as leverage into a negotiation over a truce the US side also has an incentive to extend before November, and CNBC's own preview flags Taiwan as a live topic even though it isn't the summit's stated focus — a wildcard that could move markets Thursday independent of anything oil-related. [read] For a book with Gulf-linked energy or shipping exposure, the read is that this week's real catalyst is Thursday in Washington, not this morning's crude print — but Saturday's missile is a reminder that the tail risk the market is discounting hasn't gotten any smaller. [flag]
