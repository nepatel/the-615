## Lead: Crude gives back Friday's war premium as Trump signals more patience with Iran, two days before voters decide how much patience he gets

Oil did Friday's move in reverse. Brent front-month traded at $108.45 as of 5:10a ET Monday, down 4.39% on Yahoo Finance's real-time quote — other overnight snapshots from FXStreet and Investing.com-sourced wires put the retreat anywhere from 1.4% to 2.5% depending on the minute sampled, a spread wide enough that this brief won't pick one, but every feed agrees on direction. [fact, NEW] WTI fell in step, to $99.61 (-4.33%, Yahoo). Both grades are unwinding Friday's rebound — Brent $113.43, WTI $104.12 — which itself had been a flight-to-safety bid as equities took their worst week since June. [fact, continuity] The mechanism: a Reuters-reported signal that President Trump wants to give Iran diplomacy more room before any resumption of strikes, now explicitly framed around not acting before Tuesday's midterms. [fact, NEW] That's a continuation, not a reversal, of the position he's held since an Oct. 8 social-media post ruling out strikes before Election Day — but markets had spent Friday pricing in the opposite tail risk, that a hard post-election turn was coming, and Monday's move unwinds some of that premium. [read]

OPEC+ gave the market a second, calmer data point Sunday: the seven core members holding spare capacity — Saudi Arabia, Russia, Iraq, Kuwait, Algeria, Kazakhstan and Oman — kept December targets unchanged at roughly 31 million bpd, a third straight monthly pause, with the group's next and more consequential decision (full 2027 policy) pushed to a Nov. 29 ministerial. [fact, NEW] That resolves this brief's Sunday [DEVELOPING] flag with the least newsworthy outcome available: no change. The country-level targets, per OPEC's own table: Saudi Arabia 10.478 million bpd, Russia 9.949 million, Iraq 4.431 million, Kuwait 2.676 million, Kazakhstan 1.628 million, Algeria 1.007 million, Oman 841,000 — a joint target of roughly 31 million bpd, unchanged for a third straight month, after the group finished unwinding 1.65 million bpd of 2023-vintage voluntary cuts back in September. [fact, NEW] The pause itself is a tell — Gulf members with notionally higher quotas aren't using them, which several analysts interviewed by wire services attribute to output still constrained by the Iran war rather than voluntary restraint. [read, attributed] A capacity review the group says is still underway, covering roughly 2 million bpd of cuts that run through year-end, is the thing to watch into the Nov. 29 meeting.

Second-order consequences split two ways. For the Fed, cheaper crude Monday morning works against the inflation-upside case hawks were building after Friday's selloff, at the margin making a December cut easier to justify — though this brief isn't pricing that from one morning's futures move. For the election itself, the read is narrower: a $4-plus oil move two days out doesn't change any race, but it does strip away one low-information signal (rising pump prices as a drag on incumbent-party sentiment) that campaigns on both sides had been citing. AAA's national average has in fact been falling for a week — $4.1694/gal today, down from $4.1837 Friday — which undercuts the gas-price-as-election-issue framing more than Monday's crude futures move does on its own. [fact, NEW]

Equities read the same morning differently: S&P futures +0.51%, Nasdaq futures +0.86%, VIX down to 18.89 from Friday's 19.42 — a risk-on tape that looks like relief at both the Iran de-escalation signal and OPEC+'s non-event, layered on top of a market that sold off hard enough Friday (Nasdaq's worst month since March) to have room to bounce. [fact, NEW] That bounce is landing on top of, not instead of, this week's other catalysts: Palantir reports hours from now, Pfizer and (probably) AMD report Tuesday, JOLTS prints Tuesday morning, and polls close Tuesday night — four potential volatility events stacked inside 36 hours, which is its own reason a reflexive Monday-morning rally shouldn't be read as a settled verdict on any of them. [read] Watch: whether Monday's cash open confirms the futures move or fades it; whether any Trump administration figure confirms or walks back the Reuters diplomacy signal before polls open Tuesday; and whether Friday's $113 Brent print was the cycle high or just the latest one, given Iran's war-risk premium (last priced 26 days ago at 32x normal) hasn't actually been repriced by anyone this week.

## Politics

Congress defused its own pre-election risk last week: Trump signed a stopgap funding bill — passed by the House and already cleared by the Senate — that keeps the government open through Dec. 11, 2026, averting a shutdown two days before voters go to the polls. [fact, NEW] That punts, rather than resolves, the fight over all twelve FY2027 appropriations bills, none of which Congress has finished; the real negotiation starts in a lame-duck session after Tuesday. [fact, NEW] Separately, early voting has broken the modern midterm record: the University of Florida's Election Lab counted 48.5 million ballots cast as of Sunday afternoon, above 2022's 45.9 million full-cycle mark with a day of early voting still to go in some states; a Reuters-sourced count ran about 600,000 lower at a slightly earlier timestamp, which this brief reads as a sampling-time gap, not a real disagreement. [fact, NEW] Texas (6.86M) and California (5.06M) lead state totals; in states that record party registration, Democrats are again over-represented relative to their share of registered voters, continuing the pattern this brief flagged Sunday. Georgia, Florida, Arizona and Illinois also posted unusually high totals; Georgia's first early-voting day alone ran more than 55,000 ballots ahead of the state's first day in the 2022 midterm, and Dallas County surpassed its own presidential-year early turnout on day one. [fact, NEW] The generic-ballot and approval numbers themselves are unchanged from issue No. 34 and aren't re-run here. One more thing the funding bill sets up: none of the twelve individual FY2027 appropriations bills has cleared Congress, so the Dec. 11 deadline hands whoever wins Tuesday's elections a lame-duck session in which the outgoing Congress, not the incoming one, writes the next funding fight — a dynamic that applies whether government divides or doesn't.
