## Lead: A new Fed chair delivers the first hike since 2023 and the 10-year clears 5% for the first time since 2007 — but it's a damaged Saudi pipeline, not the collapsed Hormuz diplomacy, that's actually easing oil

Wednesday closed a loop this issue has been tracking since hike odds first started climbing three weeks ago: the FOMC raised its target range 25bp, to 3.75%-4.00%, on a 12-0 vote — the first increase since 2023 — effective Sept. 17. [fact, federalreserve.gov] The committee's statement cited resilient domestic spending, strong productivity growth, robust capital investment, and job gains keeping pace with the workforce, while flagging that "uncertainty remains elevated owing, in part, to geopolitical developments." [fact] It was also a first: this was Kevin Warsh's inaugural meeting running the press conference as Fed chair, not Jerome Powell's. Warsh was confirmed 54-45 in May and sworn in May 22; Powell remains a sitting governor through 2028 but no longer chairs the Committee — a standing fact worth flagging plainly, since this issue has been imprecise about it. [fact]

Warsh's framing in the presser was pointedly narrow: "We cannot affect any individual price," he said, naming oil and groceries specifically, arguing the Fed's job is to stop a relative-price shock from broadening into "second and third order effects" through the rest of the economy. [fact] He said three things had changed since the last meeting, and led with stronger-than-expected economic data — a reference, most plausibly, to Wednesday morning's August retail sales, which beat sharply: headline +1.2% m/m against an +0.8% consensus, the largest monthly gain in five months, with the control group up 1.4% against a downwardly revised -0.4% in July. [fact] Reporting citing the dot plot has 16 of 18 participants projecting at least one more hike before year-end, with year-end fed-funds projections clustering 4.1%-4.4%. [fact, sourced to CNBC/Raisin coverage of the SEP — recommend a direct SEP-document check before treating as final] Put together: a hawkish committee, under a new chair explicitly trying to draw a line against second-round inflation effects, hiking into data that gave it cover rather than into weakness. [read]

Markets took the hawkishness at face value. All three major averages had traded higher ahead of 2p Wednesday and gave it back on the decision and presser: the Dow fell 1.21% (-631.21pts) to 51,461.90, the S&P 500 lost 0.45% to 7,551.81, and the Nasdaq closed essentially flat, -0.01%, at 25,978.42. [fact] The bond market's reaction was sharper and more durable: the 10-year yield pushed through 5% and closed around 5.02%, its highest level since July 2007 — a level that had held since November 2023 through every prior scare this issue has tracked. [fact] The dollar index cleared 100 for the first time in weeks, trading around 100.07 just after midnight ET, with one technical read flagging the move as overbought (RSI 83.54) and noting a hawkish dot plot has now removed previously priced 2027 rate cuts entirely. [fact, single-source technical read — directional confidence, not the RSI figure specifically]

The part that complicates the simple "hawkish Fed, stronger dollar, weaker risk assets" story is oil, and it's moving for a reason that has nothing to do with Wednesday's decision. This issue's last edition flagged a reported Monday meeting in Salalah, Oman, where GCC and Iranian diplomats were expected to discuss a temporary Hormuz shipping arrangement; that meeting never happened. Oman's foreign minister announced the day before that it was "postponed... in the interests of consensus," after Saudi Arabia submitted amendments over concerns about the framework's lasting implications for Hormuz, blocking consensus; Iran said it would proceed bilaterally with Oman regardless. [fact, corrects last issue's expectation] So the diplomatic route to easing Gulf oil-supply risk collapsed entirely last weekend — and yet crude has eased anyway, on a different mechanism. Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline, the 1,200km, 4-5mn bbl/day route that lets the kingdom move crude to the Red Sea port of Yanbu without transiting Hormuz, was knocked offline after a drone strike damaged two pumping stations; sources dispute whether the strike originated from Iraq (per initial CNBC reporting) or was a Houthi operation (per subsequent trade-press coverage), and that attribution question is unresolved here. [flag: sourcing conflict on the strike's origin] What moved the market Wednesday was Bloomberg's report that Aramco can restore roughly half the pipeline's capacity within days, with full capability targeted in about six weeks — pushing WTI below $103 for the first time in several sessions. [fact] The mechanism, then, is a supply-side repair to a Hormuz workaround, not a de-escalation of the underlying Iran conflict, which is worth distinguishing clearly: Hormuz itself remains constrained (see Iran & Hormuz), and this is Saudi Arabia fixing its own bypass, not Tehran opening the strait.

The setup into Friday and next week is genuinely two-sided. A Fed that just hiked, with 16 of 18 participants penciling in more, sits against a 10-year at an 18-year high and a dollar breaking a multi-week range — a combination that tightens financial conditions well beyond the 25bp move itself. [read] Whether oil keeps easing depends on execution risk in the Aramco repair timeline that a six-week full-restoration target doesn't remove, not on any actual reduction in the number of ships willing to run the strait itself. [read]
