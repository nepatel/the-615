## Lead: Basij commander claims Hormuz is "under Iran's control" as attacks intensify — and oil fell anyway

Iran escalated its pushback against Trump's "total control" claim on Thursday, and for the first time did so through the newly reshuffled chain of command rather than only the foreign ministry. Hossein Taeb — the Khamenei loyalist installed atop the Basij paramilitary in Sunday's reshuffle — said the strait is "under Iran's control and management," while Iran's joint military command separately declared that no vessel can transit without Tehran's permission, calling U.S. claims of normal passage "lies and falsehoods." [fact: statements confirmed Thursday] Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi went further, saying Washington has "long miscalculated due to intelligence failures" and calling the war itself "an even bigger miscalculation." [fact] This is the first time the command structure installed after February's succession has been invoked in an actual public dispute rather than described in the abstract — read as evidence that stacking loyalists atop the Basij and IRGC was about building a coherent line of authority for exactly this kind of confrontation, not just internal security. [read]

The rhetoric coincided with the worst 24-48 hours for vessel safety in weeks. A commercial tanker was struck by a drone on an outbound transit overnight (UKMTO-confirmed, minor damage, no injuries), and separately ADNOC said two of its vessels were attacked Thursday evening — the UAE's second such incident in under a week, prompting Abu Dhabi to formally invoke UN Security Council Resolution 2817 on freedom of navigation. [fact] Two attacks in roughly a day, layered onto Trump's Wednesday claim that the U.S. "own[s]" the strait and "might keep it," is a sharper contradiction than the general disagreement carried in prior issues — a live claim of control is now being answered with live attacks on shipping. [read]

Shipping data is unusually unanimous on direction even as vendors disagree on magnitude and timing. IMF PortWatch's lagged AIS count showed just 1 vessel transiting Aug. 9 against a ~73/day pre-crisis baseline; straits.live puts last-24h throughput at roughly 1% of normal; and Lloyd's List's slower weekly aggregate shows both total transits (78, week of 8/3-8/9, down from a revised 95) and the non-Iranian-linked westbound subset (23 in the week to ~8/12, down from 28) falling for a second straight week. [fact, three sources, one direction]

Yet Brent and WTI both fell Thursday — WTI settled down more than 2% near $81, its first drop after five straight sessions of gains — and are only fractionally higher (Brent +0.18%, WTI +0.65%) in early Friday trade. [fact] That divergence is worth flagging rather than smoothing over: crude is trading on the macro backdrop — soft PPI, falling rate-hike odds, demand-side caution explicitly cited in Thursday's oil commentary — even as the physical security picture in the strait deteriorated further. [read] Either the market is treating further escalation as already priced into a persistent high-$80s Brent handle, or it's under-pricing the risk that a second consecutive ADNOC attack is an emerging pattern rather than a one-off. The data available this run can't distinguish between those two reads, and that ambiguity is itself the tradeable fact for the desk this morning. [read]

The Fed angle from Wednesday's CPI carries through. Soft CPI (+0.1% headline MoM, +3.4% YoY) and Thursday's even softer PPI (flat MoM vs. +0.2% consensus, annual rate down to 4.7% from 5.5%) have pulled September hike odds down from the roughly 60% flagged in the last issue — CME FedWatch and Kalshi disagree on the exact level (readings this run ranged roughly 30-40% on CME, 30% on Kalshi) but both now put a hold as the base case. [fact, hedge on precision] A market pricing disinflation and de-risking Hormuz at the same time is either coherent — both signal a Fed that doesn't need to lean harder into what looks like a contained supply shock — or contradictory, since oil's stickiness is exactly the risk that could reverse the disinflation trade. Friday's retail sales, still unreleased as of this writing, is the next data point that tests which read holds. [DEVELOPING] For a desk positioned around energy and rates simultaneously, the trade this morning isn't "pick a side" — it's recognizing that both markets are pricing genuinely different information sets (crude on macro-and-inflation data, the strait's physical risk on incident-by-incident escalation) that happen to be moving in opposite directions this week, and that the two will only reconverge once one set of data stops updating faster than the other. ## Politics

The House-Senate funding standoff sharpened this week rather than resolving. [NEW] The Senate passed its own continuing resolution 90-6 before recessing, funding the government through Dec. 11 and including negotiated riders — blocking a proposed rule expanding political appointees' role in federal grant review, and barring fund transfers to Border Patrol. But the House, before its own recess, had already passed a narrower version funding only through Dec. 4 with none of the Senate's carve-outs, opposed by most House Democrats. The chambers now hold two incompatible bills with both out of session into September, leaving reconciliation undone heading toward the Sept. 30 fiscal year-end — a wider gap than the "House still has to act" framing carried last issue suggested. The practical read for the desk: neither chamber can amend or conference the other's bill until members are back in Washington, which pushes any resolution mechanism into a compressed post-Labor-Day window rather than the calmer run-up investors had been pricing, and raises (without yet confirming) the odds of a stopgap-on-a-stopgap rather than a clean reconciliation.
