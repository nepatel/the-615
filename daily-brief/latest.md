## Lead: Iran's ceasefire lapses into a seizure and a fatal strike; Bessent's "Economic D-Day" lands Monday

The 60-day US-Iran memorandum of understanding, signed June 17 to buy time for a "final deal," expired Wednesday with no agreement — and the five days since have shown what that lapse actually means on the water. [NEW] Tehran declared the MoU nullified by US violations and announced mandatory transit permits and new "maritime environmental tolls" for the strait; Washington rejected the claim and kept its convoy escorts and blockade enforcement in place. Within hours, Iran made its first tanker seizure since June 22 — boarding the Liberia-flagged, UAE-linked tanker Amara near Qeshm Island — and, overnight Aug. 17-18, an unidentified projectile struck the bulk carrier Minoan Dignity's engine room as it exited the strait's southern corridor, killing the chief engineer. It was the second strike this month on a vessel managed by the same Greek operator, Modion Maritime, after the Minoan Pioneer on Aug. 4. [read: the seizure and the strike, arriving within a day of each other and of the MoU's expiry, look less like coincidence than Iran demonstrating what enforcement of its new transit rules will cost anyone who doesn't pay.]

Transit data confirms the chilling effect. Kpler counted just three vessels crossing Sunday Aug. 16, five the day before, 12 the day before that — traffic that Lloyd's List Intelligence's slower weekly count put at 73 transits for Aug. 10-16, down from 91 the prior week. [NEW] An estimated 520 vessels are now backed up in the Arabian Gulf, with an up-to-eight-week clearing time even in an optimistic reopening scenario [flag: single-sourced this run]. The UAE, which had stayed rhetorically firm through five months of attacks without directly retaliating, went further this week: it cut all economic and financial ties with Iran, accusing Tehran of firing ballistic missiles at Emirati territory — a charge Iran denies. [NEW] The UAE is Iran's largest source of imports and third-largest export market, worth more than $7 billion; severing it removes one of the last commercial arteries Tehran had left running.

Into that backdrop, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent holds a press conference Monday to detail what Trump has branded "Economic D-Day" — what Bessent calls a "one-two punch" pairing the existing naval blockade with what he's promising will be the "toughest sanctions in history," explicitly aimed at collapsing the regime rather than merely constraining it. [NEW] Iran's Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi got out ahead of it, dismissing the campaign on social media as "a diversion from America's own crisis: unprecedented debt and surging interest costs" and running through a list of past US pressure campaigns he says all failed — "crippling sanctions," "maximum pressure," "unconditional surrender." [read: bravado aside, Araghchi's framing — that Washington is the one under fiscal strain — reads as a play to the domestic audience that matters most to Tehran right now, a public increasingly squeezed by an economy it "cannot sell oil" out of, per President Pezeshkian's comment two weeks ago.]

Oil is the cleanest read on how markets are pricing the sequence. Brent, which settled at $88.52 a week ago Friday, closed Friday in a $93.40-93.87 range across aggregators — a roughly $5, or 6%, jump that tracks the MoU's collapse and Bessent's escalation more than any single headline. [flag: no clean single ICE settle located this run; range reflects genuine cross-source divergence] WTI moved similarly, into an $86.20-86.64 range. National retail gasoline followed, to $4.102 a gallon Saturday — up four cents on the week and roughly a dollar above year-ago levels. The mechanical read for Monday: Bessent's press conference is now a two-way risk event for crude, capable of moving the complex either on the specifics of what gets sanctioned or on Iran's next retaliatory move if the response is severe enough to threaten the regime's remaining revenue base.
