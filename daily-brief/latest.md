## Lead: Trump says he'll make Hormuz "a territory of the United States" — Tehran calls it delusion, and says there's no negotiation to make it real anyway

Speaking Friday at a law-enforcement event in Garden City, New York, President Trump told the crowd that "after we finish defeating Iran, which is being very badly defeated, pretty soon I'll be declaring the Hormuz Strait a territory of the United States." A White House official later told reporters the president was joking and that no formal policy process on any territorial claim exists. [fact: remarks and later clarification both confirmed] Iran did not treat it as a joke. Deputy Foreign Minister Kazem Gharibabadi posted on X that "the Strait of Hormuz cannot be seized by tweet, nor by aircraft carrier, nor by issuing an order, nor by an election speech," adding it "has been Iranian, is Iranian, and will remain Iranian." Tehran's state media separately quoted Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi saying no negotiations with the U.S. are underway at all right now. [fact: both quoted comments confirmed 8/15]

That last line is the one worth sitting with. Every issue since early August has carried the same throughline — Trump claims control, Iran calls it false, but a technical Oman-brokered shipping-lane framework was reportedly close to done underneath the noise. Araghchi's "no negotiations underway" is either a hardening of Iran's position, a bargaining posture, or simply refers to a narrower channel than the Oman framework — this run could not independently confirm which. [read] Either way, the gap between "close to a deal" reporting from early August and "no talks happening" now is itself the data point: whatever momentum existed toward reopening has stalled at the same moment the rhetoric on both sides has escalated from "who controls it" to "who owns it."

The tape isn't panicking, but it isn't shrugging either. Brent settled Friday at $88.52 (ICE front, Trading Economics), up 1.67% on the session and extending a multi-day climb; a second tracker (Oilprice.com) showed a smaller $87.47 print, a divergence neither source resolves. [unverified: no single settle confirmed] The renewed attacks on shipping — UAE's ADNOC said two of its tankers were struck by drones transiting the strait the night of 8/13, confirmed by the UAE Foreign Ministry the next day with no casualties — are consistent with a market pricing continued disruption over any near-term reopening, and Lloyd's List's own transit count backs that reading: 78 vessels crossed in the week of 8/3-8/9, down from 95 the week before, the first weekly decline since the late-July reopening push that had briefly lifted traffic. [read: the "reopening momentum" story from two weeks ago has reversed]

Equities are telling a calmer, separate story. The S&P 500 closed Thursday at a fresh record — its 27th of 2026 — before slipping 0.17% Friday to 7,785.76, still a third straight weekly gain; the Nasdaq Composite eased 0.28% to 26,729.16. Neither move traces to Hormuz. Friday's dip followed a weaker-than-expected July retail sales report (Census: sales down 0.6% month-over-month to $763.6B, dragged by a 1.8% drop in auto dealers and a 2.2% drop in nonstore/online retail), arriving two days after Wednesday's CPI print showed headline inflation cooling to 3.4% year-over-year and core to 2.5%, both down a tenth from June. The read-through is a Fed story, not a Hormuz story, and it's the same one that's been building all week: rate-hike odds for the September 16 FOMC meeting have been sliding since late July, when CME FedWatch trackers put a hike near two-in-three; a weak July jobs report cut that to roughly 44% by 8/7, and this week's soft CPI and softer retail sales pushed most trackers toward a 40%-hike/60%-hold split by Friday — the exact number varies by snapshot and source, but every reading available this run points the same direction, down. ## Iran & Hormuz

The transit data is the clearest signal that the "reopening momentum" narrative from two weeks ago has stalled: Lloyd's List counted 78 commercial transits in the week of 8/3-8/9, down from 95 the week before and the first weekly decline since traffic began recovering in late July, with non-Iranian-linked transits down to 45 from 63 over the same span. Dark-tanker activity moved the other way — 58 vessels ran AIS-dark in the 24 hours before this run's straits.live snapshot, well above the platform's own 44.1-vessel seven-day average — consistent with more operators choosing to move cargo invisibly rather than accept war-risk pricing or transit risk openly. The war-risk premium itself hasn't moved: still 30x pre-crisis levels and roughly $10 million to insure a single VLCC passage per straits.live, unchanged since at least 8/11, which tracks with Kpler's standing observation (carried in prior issues) that premiums jump fast on new attacks but decline only slowly through calm stretches — and this week didn't offer enough calm to test that. None of this bears directly on the Trump/Iran rhetorical exchange in the Lead; it's the slower-moving commercial track that will lag whatever happens politically by weeks either way.
