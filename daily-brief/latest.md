[fact, stockanalysis.com] Together the two sessions have erased more than half of Monday's gain. The broader market followed it down Tuesday: the S&P 500 slipped roughly 0.5% to about 7,201 and the Nasdaq Composite dropped 1.48% to 23,733.09, while the Dow rose 0.85% to a first-ever close above 50,000 — a rotation into defensive value names rather than a vote of confidence in the rally's durability, as chip and AI-infrastructure stocks gave back Monday's leadership. Asia absorbed the reversal harder than Wall Street did. South Korea's Kospi, which had jumped 6.32% Monday on the chip-rally spillover, sank 6.16% Wednesday — arithmetically erasing almost exactly Monday's gain in a single session. The reinstatement is also drawing direct Congressional pushback rather than just scrutiny. Sen. Kevin Cramer (R-N.D.), a Senate Banking Committee member who had called on Huang to resign before the board reversed itself, told CNBC Tuesday that the board — not Huang — now owns the reputational and security risk: "They can no longer say it was Jensen's fault," adding that Huang might need "a babysitter" and urging Congress to "immediately pass legislation to limit AI chip sales to China." [fact/read, attributed; NEW] That call lands alongside a concrete enforcement action: Commerce's Bureau of Industry and Security confirmed this week it added Singapore's Megaspeed International and six affiliates — including Alibaba's Singapore subsidiary, Apex Enterprise Solutions — to the Entity List over the risk that advanced Nvidia chips were diverted to China, resolving yesterday's flag that no primary BIS release had been located. [fact; DEVELOPING, resolved] But multiple former officials quoted across outlets say the listing omits the foreign-direct-product rule that would reach Taiwan-manufactured Nvidia chips routed through Megaspeed's affiliates, making the designation largely symbolic rather than a binding chokepoint — and Megaspeed itself denies wrongdoing. [read, attributed]
