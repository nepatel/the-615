## Lead: Trump rejects Iran's seven-day plan to reopen Hormuz, tells aides to expect strikes to resume after the midterms

Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi used the sidelines of the UN General Assembly on Thursday to lay out Tehran's most concrete offer yet on the Strait of Hormuz: a seven-day sequence in which Washington would lift its naval blockade of Iranian ports, waive sanctions on Iranian oil sales and observe a ceasefire extending to Lebanon, in exchange for the strait reopening to commercial shipping on the last of the seven days, followed immediately by comprehensive talks on Iran's nuclear program. [fact] The terms track closely to the 14-point memorandum of understanding the two sides signed in June and later let lapse, compressed from that deal's 60-day runway to seven days — a real acceleration if genuine. [read] Araghchi told reporters Friday that "if there is seriousness on the US side to come to a deal and reopen the Strait of Hormuz, everything is now prepared." [fact]

According to the Wall Street Journal, President Trump has rejected the offer and told aides privately that he expects a renewed US bombing campaign against Iran once the Nov. 3 midterms are past. [NEW, single-sourced to WSJ via secondary outlets — no on-record White House confirmation or denial located this run] No formal US response to Araghchi's proposal has been made public. [flag]

The mechanism worth tracing: this was the most complete diplomatic offramp on the table since the war began in February, and its rejection removes the near-term probability of a negotiated Hormuz reopening rather than the physical-workaround relief this week's oil market actually traded on — Saudi Aramco's East-West pipeline restart, not any Hormuz breakthrough, is what pushed Brent and WTI lower into Friday's close. [read] With that bilateral track now closed off, the market's own pricing of the risk — a war-risk premium still running 7.5%-10% of hull value, a dark-fleet share still dominating the limited transits that do occur — has no reason to unwind before the midterms, and Trump's reported timeline gives it a specific window to hold through. [read] A second diplomatic track, France's US-backed push for a new UN Security Council resolution authorizing an international naval mission through the strait, continues in parallel, but multilateral naval-escort authority moves on a much slower clock than a bilateral phased deal, and Russia and China already vetoed a similar Bahrain-led draft in April. [read]

There is a domestic-politics dimension worth stating plainly rather than inferring quietly: if the Journal's account is accurate, the timing of a potential resumption of US strikes is being calibrated against the midterm election calendar rather than against conditions on the ground in the strait. [read] That sits awkwardly next to the diplomatic optics of this week's Trump-Xi summit, where both leaders discussed Iran without any stated outcome, and against a domestic backdrop where the Fed's own hawkish repricing — a 10-year yield still at 5.17%, a hike-odds-heavy Oct. 27-28 FOMC meeting, an Aug. PCE print due Wednesday — leaves little room to absorb a fresh oil supply shock without it showing up quickly in inflation expectations. [read] For a portfolio pricing in gradually falling geopolitical risk premia into year-end, Friday's reporting is the opposite signal: the most promising exit ramp from this crisis was just closed, on a timeline tied to an election rather than a ceasefire.
