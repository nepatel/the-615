## Lead: Nvidia blows past its own guidance again, handing Warsh a stronger tape and a stickier inflation problem on the eve of Jackson Hole

Nvidia reported fiscal second-quarter revenue of $96.221 billion Wednesday after the close, beating the roughly $92.1 billion Visible Alpha consensus by about $4 billion, with Data Center revenue of $89.0 billion — up 117% year-over-year and now 92% of total sales. Non-GAAP EPS of $2.22 topped the $2.09 estimate; GAAP EPS came in at $2.46. Gross margin held at 75.0% on both a GAAP and non-GAAP basis, matching guidance despite what the company has flagged as rising memory and wafer costs. The mechanism that actually moved markets: Q3 revenue guidance of $108.0 billion, plus or minus 2%, cleared Wall Street's roughly $104.2 billion consensus by nearly $4 billion — a bigger beat-and-raise than the deceleration narrative around Nvidia's cooling year-over-year growth rate (106% this quarter, down from triple digits a year ago) had priced in. CFO Colette Kress told analysts she expects roughly 70% revenue growth in fiscal 2028, an unusually explicit multi-year figure for a company that has mostly declined to guide beyond a single quarter. [NEW]

The market's reaction confirms the scale of the beat. NVDA rose as much as 6% in Wednesday's after-hours session and was still up roughly 6% in Thursday's premarket, dragging S&P 500 futures up 0.7% and Nasdaq 100 futures up 0.9% ahead of the bell — a sharper premarket move than Monday's Iran-sanctions announcement produced in either direction. Salesforce and CrowdStrike, which also reported Wednesday, reinforced the tape: Salesforce posted $11.3 billion in revenue (+11% Y/Y) and raised FY27 guidance by $200-300 million; CrowdStrike beat on both lines ($1.47B revenue vs. $1.44B expected, EPS $0.31 vs. $0.29) and lifted its full-year outlook. [read: three consecutive AI-adjacent beats in one evening is the clearest signal yet that this week's "high-beta earnings risk" framing had it backwards — Monday's pre-print chip selloff was defensive positioning, not a read on the fundamentals.]

The second-order consequence lands at the same moment. Wednesday's other release was the Q2 GDP second estimate: unrevised at 1.5% annualized growth, but with the report's embedded PCE price index revised up two-tenths to 5.3% headline and 3.6% core — both hotter than the advance reading. [fact] That is a different series from the Fed's preferred July PCE index, also out Wednesday, which showed core PCE up 0.2% month-on-month and 3.3% year-on-year — but both move the same direction: inflation readings ticking up, not down, the day before Jackson Hole opens and two days before Warsh's first keynote as chair. [read: a resilient, AI-capex-driven equity tape colliding with stickier-than-expected inflation data is close to the worst setup for a new Fed chair trying to use Friday's speech to signal a data-dependent, market-anchored policy path — strong growth and firm inflation together argue against the rate cuts markets have been pricing, right as his own credibility is freshly contested.]

That contest is explicit. Treasury Secretary Bessent raised the ceiling on the department's long-bond buybacks from $2 billion to at least $4 billion this month, an intervention that has helped pull the 10-year to 4.66% and the 30-year to 5.18% (Treasury.gov, 8/26) — a genuine yield decline, but one FX desks including Bank of America say is muddying the market-rate signal Warsh needs to calibrate Friday's message, with the dollar (DXY 99.15) still pinned near a three-month low on fiscal-risk concerns. [read: Nvidia's beat buys Warsh nothing on the inflation side of his mandate and complicates the growth side too — a Fed chair who wants an unfiltered bond market is instead getting artificially suppressed yields from his own Treasury Secretary at the exact moment equities are telling him the economy doesn't need rescuing.] Marvell reports after Thursday's close as the week's last major AI-capex data point before Warsh speaks Friday morning — and after Wednesday's Dick's Sporting Goods collapse (see Business), a reminder that not every earnings print this week is landing on the same side of the beat-and-raise story.

## Politics

[NEW: formal response filed] Scavino's original letter had argued there is "sufficient reason to believe" Cook made false statements on mortgage agreements; Lowell's response frames every disputed entry as either a lender-side error or immaterial to loan terms, the same defense his client has maintained publicly since the removal fight began roughly a year ago.
