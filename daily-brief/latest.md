## Lead: US strikes Iran's Larak Island, Tehran hits back at Jordan, and Tuesday brings two more tanker attacks — reopening a war-risk trade the market had started to look past

The Strait of Hormuz crisis, quiet enough Friday-into-Saturday that the prior issue flagged a rare 24-hour lull, is escalating again. US forces struck two Iranian rocket launchers on Larak Island Saturday after observing IRGC personnel preparing to fire sea mines into the strait; CENTCOM described it as "limited, precise action against IRGC minelaying forces posing an imminent threat," and Iran says three IRGC personnel were killed. [NEW] Iran retaliated Sunday with what the Guards called "Punishment of the Aggressor" — a combined missile-and-drone strike on the King Hussein and Al Azraq bases in Jordan; Jordan said it intercepted eight missiles and reported no US casualties. It was the first direct US-Iran military exchange in over a month. Tuesday brought a second wave: two tankers, the Liberian-flagged Senegal Prosperity and the Bahri-operated VLCC Sidr, were both struck by unidentified projectiles roughly 17 nautical miles east of Khasab, Oman, per UKMTO and Marisks reporting — no casualties or pollution on either vessel, but a clear signal that whatever degraded Saturday, Iran or its proxies retain the capacity to hit shipping. [NEW]

Mechanism: the market reaction inverted the usual playbook. Oil moved as expected — WTI up as much as 2.44% to $87.85 in Tuesday premarket trade, Brent indicative in the $90-91 range on different feeds, both benchmarks still lacking a clean settle as cash markets open. [fact] Equity futures fell in sympathy (S&P -0.58%, Nasdaq 100 -1.02% premarket) and the VIX jumped 6.3% intraday to 15.86. But gold — the asset that should catch a safe-haven bid from a hot-war escalation at the world's most important oil chokepoint — instead extended Friday's selloff, sitting $150-200 below last week's highs. [read: the read here is that positioning is more levered to the Sept. 16 FOMC than to the tanker war. Friday's Warsh-driven repricing (CME FedWatch September hike odds now 66%, up from 57% at Friday's close) has become the dominant cross-asset driver, strong enough that even a live escalation in Hormuz isn't pulling gold higher — a genuine divergence from how this crisis has traded since February, and worth watching for whether it holds once cash markets are fully open.]

The second-order consequence is the one that matters most for the next two weeks of Fed positioning. Axios reported Sunday that a CENTCOM-drafted plan for broader "limited strikes" against Iran — aimed at reducing the risk to tankers, Navy vessels and Air Force aircraft — had not been approved by Trump ahead of the weekend's exchange, but could now be greenlit. [fact, single-sourced to Axios] Trump himself posted an AI-generated video Monday depicting a strike on Kharg Island, Iran's main oil export terminal, and told reporters "their navy's gone… their air force is gone… their leaders are gone… all of their anti-aircraft — I mean, it's all wiped out." [fact: direct quote] Tuesday's tanker strikes complicate that framing considerably — a degraded Iranian military is still landing rockets on commercial shipping — and raise the odds that whatever CENTCOM plan Trump is weighing gets used. [read] Any sustained oil move from here lands directly on Warsh's desk nine days before the Aug. CPI print (Sept. 11) and fifteen before the Sept. 16 decision: a supply-side price shock is exactly the wrong input for a Fed chair already arguing inflation, not employment, is the binding constraint — it hands the hawks a second data point without requiring the economy to show any underlying strength. [read] Friday's jobs report remains the more proximate swing factor, but a war-driven oil spike into that print would make an already-live September hike debate harder to talk back down.
