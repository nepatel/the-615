## Lead: Iran hits a US-linked base in Jordan hours after CENTCOM destroys five more of its tankers — the tanker war and the base war have converged into one continuous risk event

Overnight, the escalation cycle that has defined the Strait of Hormuz crisis since Saturday's naval clash took its sharpest turn yet inside about 36 hours. Tuesday, CENTCOM said it had destroyed five Iranian oil tankers — four in the Gulf of Oman (M/T Kaviz, Charminar, Horizon 1 and Riesco) and one near Kharg Island (M/T Derya) — after the IRGC twice targeted a US Navy warship with ballistic missiles over the prior two days; American forces reportedly ordered the tankers' crews to abandon ship before striking, and CENTCOM reported no US casualties. [fact] Then, in the early hours of Wednesday ET, Iran retaliated directly against a US-linked land target for the first time this week: the IRGC claimed ballistic missiles struck a hangar housing American RQ-4 and MQ-9 drones at Prince Hassan Airbase in Jordan. Jordan's military said it intercepted 10 of 13 missiles fired, with the rest landing on open ground and no casualties reported, while a US official characterized the attack as "ineffective." [NEW] [fact, single-sourced (ABC News, 3:02a ET) and still developing; not yet corroborated by CENTCOM]

[read] The mechanism connecting the two episodes is now explicit and mutually reinforcing in a way it wasn't a week ago: US strikes destroy Iranian tankers, Iran retaliates against a US-linked land target in a third country rather than at sea, and markets treat both halves of the exchange as one continuous risk event rather than two separate incidents. That's a meaningful shift from last week's naval clash, where the retaliatory logic ran ship-to-ship. A strike on a Jordanian airbase pulls a US treaty-adjacent partner directly into the crossfire and raises the odds of an incident producing American or allied casualties — which would be a materially different market and political event than anything since the Feb. 28 closure declaration, given Jordan has otherwise stayed at arm's length from direct combat. [read]

Brent has now traded through $99 for the first time since the crisis began accelerating in late August, up from the high-$80s three weeks ago; WTI sits at a three-month high near $94. [fact] Tuesday's equity session already reflected the compounding pressure: the Dow's 628-point, 1.18% drop was its worst close in weeks, layering oil-driven inflation anxiety on top of Monday's trade-war escalation with Canada and a Fed decision now two weeks out. [fact] [read] With futures little changed early Wednesday (S&P +0.10%, Nasdaq-100 +0.22%), the market hasn't yet fully processed the overnight Jordan strike; whether Tuesday's selloff resumes at the open or the "ineffective" framing lets equities shrug it off is the first thing this reader should watch at 9:30. [read]

Two threads compound the picture. Qatar's Foreign Ministry warned Tuesday that continued disruption to the Strait risks an "industrial catastrophe" — a notable escalation in tone from a Gulf state that had largely avoided direct commentary on the crisis — as Kpler data put average daily transits over the past 10 days at their lowest since May. [NEW] [fact] Separately, the new head of Iran's Supreme National Security Council, Mohsen Rezaei, said over the weekend that Tehran plans to formalize an "exclusion zone" outside the Strait, under which any vessel identified attempting to transit would be added to Iran's sanctions list. [fact] No implementation date has been set, and the move would functionally codify what is already a near-total shutdown rather than materially change current transit volumes — but it would remove any ambiguity about Iran's claimed legal authority to interdict shipping beyond its own territorial waters. [read]

For the Fed, oil at $99 complicates Friday's CPI regardless of what the print itself shows: a committee already split roughly down the middle on a September hike (CME FedWatch odds sit at 57% this morning, down from a post-Jackson Hole peak near 66%) now has to weigh a fresh energy-price impulse against President Trump's public campaign to push the Fed toward cuts ahead of the midterms — a tension this issue's Economics and Politics sections pick up below. [read]

CENTCOM's own public framing of Tuesday's strikes is itself worth flagging: commanders have described the response doctrine in deliberately disproportionate terms since Saturday's clash — destroying multiple Iranian assets for every attempted Iranian strike on a US vessel — and the five-tanker response to two missile attempts fits that pattern exactly. [fact] [read] That doctrine is precisely what a Jordan-based retaliation is designed to route around: if the cost of hitting a US ship is a multiple of Iranian tankers, hitting a US-linked asset on Jordanian soil at least changes the venue, even if this particular attempt caused no reported damage. Whether Iran escalates again with a more effective strike, or whether Tuesday night proves to be the high-water mark of this cycle, is the single most consequential open question hanging over both the oil market and Friday's CPI reaction.
