## Lead: Iran and Oman float an interim Hormuz corridor — and Tehran immediately says that isn't the same as reopening

Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi and his Omani counterpart, Badr al-Busaidi, used a joint statement Tuesday to disclose an "interim framework" aimed at resuming shipping through the Strait of Hormuz — the most concrete diplomatic movement of the five-month crisis. [NEW] The mechanism: a temporary joint maritime corridor roughly seven nautical miles wide, paired with a mine-clearance project to restore safe navigation. Under the proposal, inbound traffic entering the Gulf from the Gulf of Oman would run entirely through Iranian waters, while outbound traffic would cross both Iranian and Omani waters — a routing choice that keeps Tehran, not a neutral third party, in physical control of the corridor Muscat is brokering. The two sides agreed to continue technical talks over the next 30 to 60 days toward a permanent corridor, a framework for administering the strait, and a mechanism for information exchange, traffic management and maritime security services, and said they would also pursue parallel talks with other Gulf states bordering the waterway.

Oil's reaction was immediate and unambiguous: Brent fell for a third consecutive session, down 2.68% to $86.20 and WTI down 2.71% to $80.13 as of Wednesday's pre-open, extending Monday's and Tuesday's declines as the crisis-premium unwind that began with Monday's Bessent sanctions rollout (see prior issue) continued on top of Tuesday's Iran-Oman news. [read: markets are pricing this as the first credible step toward de-escalating the physical blockade, not just another round of the rhetorical sparring that has characterized most of the past five months.]

But the read and the fact diverge here, and the gap is the story. Iran's deputy foreign minister said Tuesday, in the same news cycle as the joint statement, that the Oman understanding does not mean the strait is reopening — it remains, in his words, "militarily closed." [NEW] That is a direct, same-day hedge from Tehran's own government against the interpretation oil markets are trading on. Nothing in the transit data supports a reopening yet either: single-day counts remain in the low single digits against an 85-vessel-per-day pre-crisis baseline (see Hormuz counters), and Lloyd's List's slower monthly aggregation still has August running near 12 transits a day versus a roughly 130-per-day pre-war norm. What actually changed Tuesday is narrower than the oil-price move suggests — Iran agreed to talk about a corridor under its own control, with a 30-to-60-day runway before any of the substantive questions (permanent administration, security services, who polices compliance) get resolved.

The second-order read is about sequencing, not substance. This diplomatic opening lands the same week as Monday's Treasury sanctions rollout ("Operation Economic Outcast," detailed in the prior issue) and Bessent's explicit refusal to set an enforcement deadline for the countries most exposed to secondary sanctions. Read together, Washington's coercive track and Oman's diplomatic channel are now running in parallel rather than in sequence — and Oman, notably, is the one Gulf state that has maintained a functioning line to Tehran throughout the crisis, which is precisely why it is the one able to produce a document Washington could not extract through sanctions alone. Whether that channel produces something durable in the next 30-60 days, or simply gives Iran a talking point to manage the sanctions pressure while the "militarily closed" status persists on the water, is the question the next several issues will need to track — not this week's oil-price pop, which is already reversing gains built on a geopolitical premium that predates any actual change in enforcement.
