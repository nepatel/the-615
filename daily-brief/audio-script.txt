The 6:15, for Tuesday, November 24th. Here's what moved overnight: Nvidia's board reversed itself in six days, bringing Jensen Huang back as CEO with help from the White House, and Wall Street had its best day since last spring.

Quick lay of the land before we get into it. Oil's sitting in the high eighties for Brent and around eighty bucks for U.S. crude, the ten-year's hovering just under five percent, gas is down to about three sixty a gallon nationally, and in the Gulf, attacks on tankers near the Strait of Hormuz kept going through at least the middle of last week, with diplomacy still going nowhere. On the calendar: Best Buy reports earnings this morning, the Fed's favorite inflation number comes out tomorrow, markets are closed Thursday for Thanksgiving, and OPEC-plus meets this coming Sunday to set next year's oil output.

Now, the big story. Nvidia's board voted, unanimously, on Sunday, to put Jensen Huang back in as CEO — just six days after the chairman had told investors flat-out that the board had no plans to bring him back. Huang had resigned on November twelfth after reports surfaced about extramarital affairs, one of them involving a woman suspected of being tied to Chinese intelligence. The board says it got input from, quote, senior levels of the U.S. government, and is now confident there's no national security problem. President Trump told reporters, in his words, "I convinced them, I made the deal" — he says he personally promised the board that Huang hadn't broken any national security laws. The chief financial officer, Colette Kress, who'd been running things as interim CEO, goes back to being CFO. And there's still an independent investigation underway into the foreign intelligence allegations — the board says that one's going to run its course regardless.

Here's the mechanism: Nvidia's stock closed up almost thirty-two percent on Monday — more than one hundred sixty-five dollars a share — and it dragged the whole market with it. The S&P 500 had its best day since last April, up almost five percent. The Nasdaq jumped more than six and a half percent, also its best day in over a year. The Dow added nearly fifteen hundred points. That one session wiped out most of a correction that had knocked the S&P below seven thousand just the Friday before. Chip stocks led the charge — Micron up nearly twenty percent, AMD up seven, CoreWeave up over twenty. But here's the catch: by this morning, some of that was already reversing. Nvidia was down more than four percent in premarket trading, and futures were softer too. Asia was a mixed bag overnight — South Korea's market jumped on the chip rally, but Japan's Nikkei actually fell almost four percent.

Why this matters beyond the stock price: you've now got a sitting president publicly taking credit for personally brokering the reinstatement of a public company's CEO, based on a private assurance to the board about national security. That's a corporate governance story independent of whether Nvidia's numbers are good. A Columbia law professor flagged exactly that concern. Senator Elizabeth Warren says this doesn't let Nvidia off the hook with Congress on export control questions. On the other side, tech investors like Elon Musk had been publicly pushing for Huang's return, and Musk posted that the board had, quote, corrected its mistake. And on the same day as the reinstatement, the Commerce Department reportedly added a company called Megaspeed — the one at the center of the China chip-diversion investigation — to its trade blacklist, something the Treasury Secretary had said two weeks earlier was being slow-walked. We haven't been able to confirm that blacklisting from a primary government source yet, so treat it as reported, not confirmed.

The backstory, as best we can tell: administration officials called the board Friday night to say Huang hadn't broken any laws. Huang himself joined the board's own meetings over the weekend. The vote came Sunday, and Trump called Huang afterward and told him, quote, work hard. Worth noting, Nvidia stock had already fallen about twenty-five percent in the week before this happened, and some investors and employees were openly questioning the interim CEO's leadership — so this wasn't purely about national security, it was also about the stock price. What to watch: whether this morning's pullback turns into a longer fade, and Huang now has three separate dates in front of Congress in the first week of December, including his first public testimony.

One more thing worth sitting with here: we've now got a company reversing a unanimous board decision about its own chief executive within less than a week, citing a phone call from the government as part of the reasoning. Whatever you think of the outcome, that's not how this is supposed to work, and it's going to be part of the story for a while, independent of where the stock trades.

On the economy: nothing new on inflation or jobs since last issue, but Wednesday's a big one — the Fed's preferred inflation gauge comes out, along with GDP and jobless claims, right before the Fed's December meeting. Markets are pricing in better than ninety percent odds of a quarter-point rate hike in December. A soft inflation number Wednesday is really the only thing that could change that.

In business: Walmart beat expectations last week, with revenue up about five percent and online sales climbing double digits, and they raised their full-year guidance — though the stock barely moved, since all the attention had already shifted to the Nvidia drama. They did flag a couple of things to watch: their membership club's profit growth is going to be squeezed for a while by a promotional bundle, and a shift in the timing of one of their big India sales events dinged this quarter's international numbers but should help the next one. Best Buy reports this morning; analysts expect around a dollar fifty a share, building on a solid beat last quarter.

In technology: Huang's Congressional schedule has expanded — originally just one closed-door interview, now it's three separate appearances across the first three days of December, ending with public testimony before the Senate Banking Committee. Separately, Blue Origin confirmed its New Glenn rocket won't fly again until January.

Overseas: Iran's Revolutionary Guard launched a drone attack on a U.S. radar site in Bahrain last Tuesday — the third such attack in a few days after a two-month lull. Bahrain's air defenses say they intercepted it; the U.S. military disputes Iran's claim that it hit anything. On the diplomatic front with Iran, nothing has changed — talks remain stalled, with no deadline in sight. And in the Gulf, tanker attacks near the Strait of Hormuz continued through at least the middle of last week, with Iran and the UK's maritime trade authority still disagreeing on whether these are projectile strikes or mines.

And finally, sports. The Yankees added three players to their roster and David Bednar is staying put after accepting his qualifying offer. The Knicks' last confirmed result was a win over Toronto, improving to eight and six — we don't have anything more recent confirmed as of airtime. And Northwestern's season is over: they lost on a last-second field goal at Minnesota, finishing six and five.

That's The 6:15. Trains are running.
