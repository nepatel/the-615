The 6:15, for Monday, November 2nd. Here's what moved overnight: oil is giving back almost all of Friday's spike, down more than four percent in early trading, after word that Trump wants to give Iran diplomacy more room before the midterms, and OPEC+ held its output steady for a third straight month.

Quickly, the board: stock futures are up a little under a point, the ten-year's still sitting at Friday's level with the bond market not open yet, gold's basically flat, gas prices keep drifting down to around four seventeen a gallon, the Strait of Hormuz is still closed for a two hundred forty sixth straight day with almost nothing moving through it, early voting has already smashed the midterm record at something like forty eight and a half million ballots cast, and tonight the Knicks host Brooklyn with their record still a mess nobody can nail down.

Here's the lead. Oil did Friday in reverse this morning. Brent was trading around a hundred eight dollars a barrel in early futures trading, down more than four percent, and WTI fell the same way, to right around a hundred dollars. Other outlets had the drop smaller, somewhere between one and a half and two and a half percent, depending on exactly when they checked — but everybody agrees it's down, not up. Both grades are unwinding the rebound they had Friday, when stocks were getting hammered and oil was catching a safety bid instead.

The reason: a report that President Trump wants to give Iran diplomacy more time before any resumption of strikes, and he's explicitly tying that to not acting before Tuesday's election. That's not really new — he said back on October eighth he wouldn't hit Iran before Election Day — but Friday the market had started pricing in the opposite, that something harsher was coming right after the vote. This morning unwinds some of that fear.

OPEC+ added a second, calmer data point. The seven core members with spare capacity — Saudi Arabia, Russia, Iraq, Kuwait, Algeria, Kazakhstan, and Oman — kept their December output targets exactly where they were, a third month running, with the real decision on next year's policy pushed off to the end of November. Put together, those seven are supposed to be producing around thirty one million barrels a day, with Saudi Arabia and Russia each accounting for roughly ten million of that on their own. Interesting wrinkle: these countries aren't actually using the higher quotas they already have, which tells you their output is still being squeezed by the Iran war itself, not that they're choosing restraint. There's also a capacity review still underway covering about two million barrels a day of cuts that run through the end of the year — that's the thing to watch heading into the late-November meeting.

What this means for the reader: cheaper oil this morning works against the inflation-hawk argument that built up after Friday's selloff, which at the margin makes a December rate cut a little easier to justify — though that's a read on one morning's futures, not a settled call. And gas prices have actually been falling for a week straight now, which quietly undercuts the idea that pump prices are hurting whoever's in power going into Tuesday.

Stocks, meanwhile, are reading the morning as good news outright — futures up, the VIX down from Friday's spike — which looks like relief at both the Iran signal and OPEC's non-event, on top of a market that got beaten up badly enough Friday that it had room to bounce. But remember what's stacked into the next thirty six hours: Palantir reports tonight, Pfizer and probably AMD report tomorrow, a jobs-openings report lands tomorrow morning, and the polls close tomorrow night. That's four separate things that could move markets, all landing before Wednesday. A reflexive Monday morning rally isn't a verdict on any of them yet.

On politics: Congress defused its own shutdown risk last week — Trump signed a funding bill that keeps the government open through December eleventh, so there's no shutdown hanging over Tuesday's vote. That's a punt, not a resolution — none of the twelve actual spending bills for next year are done, so whoever wins Tuesday inherits that fight in a lame-duck session. And early voting, as mentioned, has already blown past the twenty twenty two midterm record, with Texas and California leading the way — Texas alone is nearing seven million early ballots — and Democrats still showing up more than their share in the states that track party registration. Georgia, Florida, Arizona, and Illinois all posted unusually big numbers too; Georgia's first day of early voting alone ran tens of thousands of ballots ahead of where it was at the same point four years ago.

On economics: the data calendar wakes back up this week. Tomorrow brings the job-openings report, Thursday brings weekly unemployment claims, and Friday is the big one — October's jobs report, on schedule, no shutdown delay this time, which actually matters given the government shut down and canceled a jobs report entirely about a year ago. September's report showed the economy adding a modest twenty nine thousand jobs with unemployment at four point two percent — that's the baseline Friday will either confirm or revise.

On business: the earnings calendar firmed up overnight. Palantir reports tonight after the close, with Wall Street penciling in profit growing sharply year over year on around two billion dollars of revenue — options traders have been pricing in a double-digit swing either way. Tomorrow, Pfizer reports before the opening bell, with analysts expecting around sixteen billion dollars of sales, and AMD is expected to report tomorrow evening too, though that date's still not fully locked down — and estimates on AMD's profit are oddly split depending on which accounting method you use, so take any specific number with a grain of salt until the actual release. Worth saying out loud: all three of those prints land before the polls even close Tuesday night, which means the most concrete catalysts this week are coming out of corporate boardrooms, not out of the voting booth, even though the election is what everyone's watching.

On the Strait of Hormuz: it's now been closed to commercial shipping for two hundred and forty six straight days. The tracker's dark-tanker signal — ships going quiet to hide their movements — actually eased somewhat overnight, which lines up with the de-escalation story, though the tracking service keeps flagging that its sample size is smaller than usual, so don't lean on that number too hard. Actual transit counts are still frozen at basically nothing, unchanged for over a week now. And the insurance premium on sailing through the strait hasn't been repriced in almost a month — which this brief reads as the market having already priced in an indefinite closure, not as a sign things have calmed down. If this morning's oil selloff holds, that stale insurance number is probably the next thing due for a re-think.

Sports, quickly: the Yankees' season has been over since early October, nothing new there. The Knicks host Brooklyn tonight at seven thirty — their actual win-loss record is still a mess that nobody's been able to pin down across conflicting trackers. And Northwestern lost badly at Oregon on Saturday, thirty four to fourteen, in the two teams' first-ever meeting.

That's The 6:15. Trains are running.
