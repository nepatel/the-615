The 6:15, for Sunday, August 23rd. Here's what moved overnight: Iran's ceasefire with the US expired this week, and in the days since, Iran seized a tanker, an attack killed a sailor, and the UAE cut off trade with Tehran entirely. Treasury Secretary Bessent goes public tomorrow with what the administration is calling an economic D-Day.

Quickly, the numbers: Brent's sitting in the low nineties, up about five bucks on the week; the ten-year's up near four and three quarters percent; stocks bounced Friday but still closed the week lower; gold's near forty-six hundred an ounce; national gas prices just ticked up to around four ten a gallon; and the Strait of Hormuz has now been effectively closed for a hundred and seventy five days, with traffic through it down to single digits a day.

Here's the lead. The sixty-day deal the US and Iran signed back in June to buy time for a real agreement expired last Wednesday with nothing to show for it. Tehran didn't just let it lapse quietly — they declared the whole thing void, blamed the US for violating it, and announced they're now going to require permits and charge tolls for anyone transiting the strait. Washington said no, that's not how this works, and kept its navy escorts and blockade running exactly as before.

And then Iran backed up the rhetoric with action, fast. Within hours of the deal expiring, Iran seized a tanker called the Amara, boarding it near Qeshm Island — that's their first tanker seizure since late June. And overnight the same week, a bulk carrier called the Minoan Dignity got hit by some kind of projectile in the engine room while it was leaving the strait, and the ship's chief engineer was killed. That's the second attack this month on a vessel from the same shipping company — the first one happened earlier in August, so this isn't a one-off, it's a pattern targeting the same operator specifically.

The shipping numbers tell you how bad it's gotten. Some days last week, only three or five ships crossed the strait, total — compare that to dozens a day before this crisis started. There's reportedly over five hundred vessels just stuck, backed up in the Gulf, and it could take weeks to clear even in a good scenario. And here's a big one: the UAE, which had mostly stayed on the sidelines rhetorically through five months of this, finally cut all economic and financial ties with Iran this week, accusing them of firing missiles at Emirati territory. Iran denies it. But the UAE was one of Iran's biggest trading partners left — losing that is a real blow.

So into all of that, tomorrow morning, Treasury Secretary Bessent holds a press conference to lay out what Trump's calling economic D-Day — basically the toughest sanctions package yet, paired with the existing naval blockade, explicitly aimed at collapsing the Iranian government, not just squeezing it. Iran's foreign minister got out ahead of it, calling it a diversion from America's own debt problems and saying every past pressure campaign against Iran has failed. Whether that bravado holds up depends a lot on what Bessent actually announces tomorrow.

Markets are pricing the deterioration, not the rhetoric. Oil's up about five dollars, roughly six percent, since a week ago Friday, and gas prices at the pump followed it higher, up to just over four dollars a gallon nationally. Tomorrow's press conference is a real two-way risk event for crude — it could move on the specifics of who gets sanctioned, or on how Iran decides to retaliate if the pressure campaign is severe enough to genuinely threaten the regime's money. Worth watching either way, because we've now gone five months without this waterway looking anything like normal, and each of these escalations has landed on top of the last one rather than replacing it.

A few other things worth knowing. Fed Governor Lisa Cook has until Wednesday to respond to Trump's renewed effort to fire her — her lawyers say the allegations are as baseless as they were a year ago. That lands the same day as Nvidia's earnings and a big batch of economic data, including the Fed's preferred inflation gauge and a GDP revision. Separately, the funding fight in Congress is still unresolved — the Senate wants to fund the government into December, the House passed a shorter version, and neither side's in a rush to reconcile them while the House stays out on recess.

Speaking of the Fed, minutes from their July meeting came out more hawkish than expected — three regional bank presidents actually wanted to raise rates that month, and the minutes made clear that hawkish view goes well beyond just those three; some officials think financial conditions still aren't tight enough to get inflation back down. Separately, the new Fed chair, Kevin Warsh, floated cutting the number of rate meetings each year from eight down to six. Betting markets still see basically no chance of a rate cut next month, and a rate hike, which seemed off the table two weeks ago, is back in play — all of which sets up a tense backdrop for Warsh's first big speech as chair, at Jackson Hole next Friday.

On the business side, Anthropic is reportedly getting ready to file for its IPO as soon as the end of this month, targeting a two trillion dollar valuation and an October debut — that's roughly double where its last private funding round valued it, back in May. And Nvidia is backing up to a hundred and five billion dollars in financing for a massive new data center campus in Ohio being built for OpenAI — ten gigawatts of power, though the first chunk of it won't even be online until 2028. Stocks themselves bounced back Friday but still finished the week lower, with the Nasdaq down about two percent on the week. And on the chip side, Microsoft's reportedly getting ready to unveil its own AI chip, the Maia 300, as soon as next month, as it tries to lean less on Nvidia — though neither Microsoft nor its manufacturer has put a number on how many they're actually ordering.

Overseas, Russia and Ukraine traded heavy strikes again this weekend — Russia hit Ukraine near the Moldova border, killing several people, and Ukraine, for the first time, struck Russia's second-biggest online retailer, setting a warehouse on fire and forcing an evacuation. Putin's threatening even more destructive strikes back. And back on Hormuz — maritime security trackers now rate the threat level there as severe, the highest it's been this entire five-month crisis, and that seized tanker, the Amara, was still sitting motionless near Qeshm Island as of a couple days ago, which tells you Iran's not backing off this new permit system anytime soon.

Sports. The Yankees won Friday, their fifth straight, on a two-run homer from rookie Spencer Jones, but dropped Saturday's game to Toronto four to three. They're seventy-three and fifty-five, second in the AL East. The Knicks have no game — they're in the off-season — but word is Josh Hart's extension talks are trending toward a three-year deal worth around sixty to sixty-two million, still not signed as of this morning. And Northwestern football is quiet this weekend too, with their season opener still two weeks out.
