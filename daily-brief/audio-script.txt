The 6:15, for Wednesday, October 21st. Here's what moved overnight: Washington is sending a third aircraft carrier toward the Middle East, confirming this fight is expected to run well past the midterms, and this morning the two oil price feeds this show tracks can't even agree whether Brent crude is going up or down.

Quick numbers, all roughly six a.m. Eastern: Brent's sitting right around a hundred ten, ten eleven, depending which feed you trust, with WTI near a hundred dollars even; the ten-year's at about five-point-one percent, the two-year near four-point-eight; the dollar index is basically flat; S&P futures and Nasdaq futures are both a touch higher; the VIX is in the high teens; gold's just above four thousand one hundred; and national average gas is down again, around four twenty-seven a gallon, even as crude climbs, which still doesn't fully make sense and nobody's reconciled it. Asia closed mixed overnight, Tokyo up, Hong Kong and Shanghai down; Europe's modestly higher at midday. In the Gulf, transit traffic through Hormuz is still running at something like five percent of normal, UKMTO logged at least a dozen ship attacks in the week through last Friday, and the strait's been effectively closed to commercial shipping for well over two hundred days now. Most of the big shipping lines are now routing around Africa instead, and betting markets are only giving about one-in-five odds that traffic gets back to anything normal before next spring. On the calendar: Tesla reports earnings tonight after the close; jobless claims hit tomorrow morning; the World Series opens Friday with the Rays at the Brewers; Northwestern hosts Rutgers Saturday; and the Fed meets a week from today with rate-cut odds still pretty thin.

Here's the lead. A third US carrier, the Theodore Roosevelt, left San Diego back in late September with about ten thousand sailors and Marines aboard, headed to join or relieve the George Washington, which has been on station through most of this fight. If the two carriers overlap once the Roosevelt gets there, the US would have three carrier strike groups in the region again, same as back in April, and there's an amphibious group headed that way too. Nobody's put a date on how long that would last, but the read here is pretty simple: Washington is resourcing for this to keep going past the midterms, not gearing up for something imminent. That lines up with Trump posting over the weekend that the US could, quote, soon finish the job unless Iran changes course. And shipping is already behaving like this is the new normal — most of the big container lines are routing around Africa instead of through the strait, and the ones still making the transit are charging about a thousand bucks a container extra to cover it. Betting markets agree: they're giving it only about one-in-five odds that transit traffic gets back to normal levels before next spring.

Meanwhile the attacks haven't slowed down. A tanker got hit by some kind of projectile off the coast of Oman early this morning, caught fire, but it's anchored now and the crew's fine. That's on top of a tugboat getting hit by two projectiles over the weekend. The US military's line this week is that the vast majority of Iran's attacks are failing, and that something like ninety-seven percent of ships trying to get through are making it — though that's a US military claim, not an independently verified number, and it doesn't really square with how thin the actual transit counts look. The military's also claiming it's disabled four commercial vessels, turned back a hundred thirty-five ships, and destroyed ten Iran-linked tankers since the blockade resumed back in July, and says the blockade's cost Iran twenty-five billion dollars in lost oil revenue. Again, that's one side's numbers, not independently checked.

And here's the part that should worry anyone trying to actually trade this: this morning, Brent crude's two main price feeds don't even agree on which direction it's moving. One's showing it up almost two percent, the other's showing it down about one percent, at basically the same moment. When your market's this thin and this dependent on just one or two data sources for a live price, that's a market where you could easily be reading the tape completely backwards. For what it's worth, Goldman's still calling for Brent to drift back down near ninety dollars by December, treating all of this as a risk premium that eventually unwinds rather than a permanent repricing.

On the corporate side: Netflix beat on earnings per share overnight — eighty-seven cents against expectations of about eighty-two — but cut its ad-revenue guidance for the year down to two point seven billion dollars, from an earlier three billion target, and the stock dropped about six percent after hours. That's one week after the company's biggest round of layoffs since 2022. Margins actually went up, to almost thirty-five percent, so this isn't really a demand story so much as an outlook story. Tesla's up tonight; deliveries already came in ahead of the lowered bar, at something like four hundred eighty-seven thousand vehicles, so the real question on the call will be what happened to automotive margins, not units.

All of this is landing in the same stretch where Trump's approval numbers have fallen to fresh lows across five separate polls — several of them actual record lows for his second term, with one putting him as low as thirty percent and another finding only about one in five independents still approve — while Democrats are favored on the generic ballot by a solid, high-single-digit margin two weeks out from the midterms. That's the political backdrop the carrier buildout and any Iran decision will get argued over, not resolved, in the next two weeks.

On the economy: nothing new printed overnight. Thursday's jobless claims are the next clean data point, with the Fed's preferred inflation gauge due sometime before next week's meeting. Here's the thing worth watching — September's hot inflation number was based on oil in the hundred-to-hundred-six range; crude's been sitting above a hundred ten for four sessions running now, so if that holds, next month's inflation read could come in even hotter, which is probably exactly why rate-cut odds for next week are still sitting in the teens to low twenties.

In sports: the Yankees' season has been over since October 7th, swept by Tampa Bay in three straight. The Knicks lost their season opener at home to Philadelphia, one-oh-six to one hundred, in LeBron James's debut with the Sixers, right after getting their championship rings — Brunson led New York with twenty-one points but shot just six for nineteen from the field. Northwestern improved to five and one with a dramatic last-second field goal to beat Michigan State on the road, erasing an eight-point fourth-quarter deficit, and they host Rutgers Saturday. And the World Series is set: the Rays, fresh off a seven-three win to clinch the pennant, open on the road at the Brewers Friday night — the first Fall Classic since nineteen eighty where neither team has ever won it all.

That's The 6:15. Trains are running.
