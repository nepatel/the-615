The 6:15, for Tuesday, November tenth. Here's what moved overnight: a Qatari-brokered proposal to reopen the Strait of Hormuz sent oil to its lowest close since August, and stocks had their best day since April.

Quick numbers, all rounded: Brent settled around ninety-three dollars, down almost six percent, its weakest close in nearly three months; WTI settled near eighty-seven. The S&P 500 jumped better than two percent to around seventy-five fifty, its best day since early April; the Dow added over eleven hundred points; the Nasdaq gained almost three percent. The ten-year Treasury sits just under four point nine percent. Gold is up to around forty-one thirty-eight an ounce. And Nvidia alone dropped more than four percent on a separate story we'll get to.

Here's the lead. Oil and stocks told two different stories on Monday, and they're actually the same story. President Trump said Sunday he's highly unlikely to order new strikes on Iran while talks are progressing, and Monday, Qatar's prime minister carried an Iranian proposal to the White House. The reported shape of it: Iran reopens Hormuz and stops hitting Gulf tankers, the US lifts its naval and economic blockade and releases eight billion dollars in frozen Iranian funds. Neither government has confirmed those exact terms — this is still single-sourced to Israeli media citing diplomatic sources. And there's real skepticism: Secretary of State Rubio called Iran's demands outrageous and said the two sides aren't close to a deal, and several cabinet officials are reportedly against this kind of arrangement. A nearly identical deal fell apart in Switzerland back in May after Iran attacked tankers in the strait days later. So treat this as a market betting on de-escalation, not a done deal.

Markets bet big anyway. Every sector in the S&P finished higher Monday, led by industrials. The read is pretty simple: cheaper, safer oil lowers a real cost across the whole economy and takes the geopolitical tail risk off the table, at least for now, and that was enough to erase most of last week's ugly selloff in one session. It's also not just the diplomacy — supply is genuinely loosening. OPEC output rose to almost twenty-three million barrels a day in October, up over a million from September. JPMorgan sees a global surplus of around one and a half million barrels a day. And the US Strategic Petroleum Reserve is now at its lowest level since nineteen eighty-two. One analyst put it well: shipping and security risks in the Gulf are still unresolved, even as traders price in a calmer outcome. That's the gap to watch.

Separately — and this one's not about oil at all — Nvidia had its own rough day. The company's board opened an independent investigation into C-E-O Jensen Huang after reports that a woman he had an affair with, identified as Alice Huang, is assessed by US intelligence as having ties to Chinese intelligence. Huang admitted the affair in a memo to staff and apologized. The board says there's no sign company secrets or national security were compromised, but it's looking into everything, and Huang stays on as CEO through the review. China's government called the spy angle unsubstantiated. The stock fell over four percent, wiping out around a hundred ninety billion dollars in value — but tellingly, the rest of the chip sector didn't follow it down; A-M-D actually jumped thirteen percent. That tells you Monday's rally was a macro story, not a Nvidia story.

What to watch: October's CPI print lands at eight-thirty this morning, before most of you are even off the train. The number forecasters are circling is about three-tenths of a percent on headline, two-tenths on core — both a step down from September — but that's still only a couple of estimates lining up, not a full Wall Street survey, so hold it loosely. A cooling number would reinforce this relief rally, a hot one reopens the inflation argument the market had mostly stopped worrying about. Worth remembering, though: Monday's oil crash doesn't even show up in this particular report — it's too late in the month to be captured — so the real test of whether cheaper oil actually helps inflation is next month's data, not today's. And CoreWeave reports earnings tonight, the first real look at AI-infrastructure economics since last week's capex scare, with Wall Street bracing for another sizable loss even as revenue keeps growing fast.

Quickly around the rest of the board. Politics: Kansas's governor's race is still uncalled nearly a week out, with Democrat Cindy Holscher ahead by a few thousand votes out of over a million cast, and a recount is likely given how close that margin is — both sides have already claimed victory, so expect this one to drag. Economics: today's CPI is the headline event, as we said, with a second print on producer prices coming Friday — and because of this year's delayed data calendar, those two reports are landing closer together than usual. Business: Cisco and Applied Materials both report Thursday, not Wednesday as we said in a previous issue — that's a correction — and Applied Materials in particular is a direct read on chip-equipment spending, the same spending Wall Street got nervous about last week; its own guidance and the Street's expectations aren't quite lined up, so Thursday's number matters more than usual. Technology: beyond the Nvidia story, Anthropic has now publicly filed its paperwork to go public, planning to list on the Nasdaq under the ticker A-N-T, though pricing, share count and timing are all still unknown, and we're not going to repeat the financial figures floating around out there since two different reports contradict each other on whether the company made billions or lost billions in the first half of the year.

On Iran and Hormuz, a bit more texture. Iran's foreign minister says Iran's offer to reopen the strait within two days still stands, but only if the war ends on, quote, all fronts — which is a bigger ask than just the narrower tanker-for-blockade trade being reported elsewhere, so the two sides might not even be negotiating over the same deal yet. Meanwhile, Iraq's government says it's reached an understanding with Washington to keep its own oil exports moving through a shipping lane near Oman regardless of how the broader standoff plays out. And the hard tracking numbers — actual ship transits through the strait, how many tankers are going dark — haven't moved yet. Monday's price action is a bet that the diplomacy holds, not proof that anything on the water has actually changed.

And now, sports, quickly. The Yankees' season is over; closer David Bednar has until next Tuesday to decide on a qualifying offer worth just over twenty-three million dollars, and second baseman Jazz Chisholm wasn't given one, so he'll test free agency without the team getting draft compensation either way. The Knicks are five and four, off since a solid road win over Cleveland, and they host Milwaukee tonight at the Garden. And Northwestern football dropped to three and seven after a home loss to a ranked Iowa team, with a trip to Ohio State up next Saturday.
