The 6:15, for Friday, October 2nd. Here's what moved overnight: Nike posted a huge headline earnings beat after Thursday's close, but almost a dollar of it came from a one-time tariff refund — strip that out and it's a pretty ordinary quarter, and the stock actually fell on the news.

Quick board setup. Payrolls for September land at 8:30 this morning, nothing out yet as you listen. Tonight Northwestern hosts Penn State at the brand-new Ryan Field, and the Yankees open their Division Series against Toronto after knocking out the Red Sox last night. Oil's harder to pin down this morning — one read has Brent down sharply from yesterday, but it's a single source with no settlement price behind it, so treat that as unconfirmed for now. Rates, the dollar, and the overseas markets didn't get a fresh read this run, so those numbers are carried over from yesterday. And on Hormuz: shipping traffic through the strait is still running at about three or four percent of normal, insurance markets still pricing war risk in the high single digits to ten percent of a ship's value, and nothing new confirmed on strikes in the last day.

Now the lead. Nike's fiscal first-quarter numbers looked spectacular on the surface — earnings per share of seventy-two cents against a Street estimate of about thirteen cents, one of the biggest beats you'll ever see on paper. But look closer and almost a billion dollars of that profit — about fifty-two cents a share of it — was a one-time benefit tied to tariffs the company expects to get refunded, under the same tariff authority that's currently sitting in front of the Supreme Court. Take that one item out, and adjusted earnings were only around twenty cents a share. Still a beat against the roughly twelve-to-thirteen-cent estimate analysts had penciled in, but a completely ordinary one, not the blowout the headline number suggests. Revenue came in just under eleven billion, modestly ahead of estimates — the cleaner read on how the actual business is doing. The market saw through the headline number immediately: the stock fell about three and a half percent in premarket trading despite "beating" on paper, which tells you the professional money wasn't fooled for a second.

The mechanism here is worth watching more broadly, not just for Nike. Expect other retailers and apparel companies with heavy tariff exposure to post similar one-time "tariff recovery" credits over the next couple of quarters while that Supreme Court case works its way through — and each one of those headline beats is going to deserve the same skepticism Nike's got this morning. Nike's actual underlying tariff exposure hasn't gone away either, one-time credit or not: roughly half its footwear sourcing still runs through Vietnam, which carries a tariff rate in the mid-forties percent, versus under twenty percent for goods sourced from China, and the company had already raised its margin-hit guidance once this year because of it.

The bigger macro story, though, is still Wednesday's inflation data, which knocked down the odds of an October Fed rate hike pretty sharply — markets went from pricing in a hike as the more-likely outcome to seeing it as a long shot, more or less overnight. We haven't been able to find a fresher reading on those odds since then, so that's still the best information we have, not something reconfirmed this morning. What matters most today is the jobs report landing at eight-thirty — a soft number on top of Wednesday's cool inflation print would reinforce the case for the Fed to hold steady at the end of the month; a strong one reopens the hike debate on its own, completely separate from whatever's happening with oil and the Gulf. Worth remembering that crude itself is pricing off Hormuz risk right now more than it's pricing off the Fed's data — so don't expect oil and rates to necessarily move together the way they normally would.

On economics: September's manufacturing survey came in a little softer than expected, at fifty-four point six, continuing an eight-month streak above the growth-contraction line, but new orders and the order backlog both cooled noticeably — a sign the pace of expansion is slowing even if the sector hasn't tipped into contraction. Today's jobs report is the more important number by far, especially coming right after August's surprisingly strong rebound, when the economy added well over a hundred thousand jobs and unemployment held steady a bit above four percent. The Fed is going to read today's number alongside Wednesday's inflation print as a pair — cooling inflation plus cooling jobs makes the case for standing pat easy; cooling inflation plus a still-hot labor market is a much harder call for them at the end of the month.

On business: Nike's the whole story this morning — no other earnings of real size came in overnight. The tariff-refund dynamic behind that one-time benefit is something to keep watching across retail as the court case moves forward.

On geopolitics: Russia hit Ukraine overnight with roughly fifty drones, an anti-ship missile, and several ballistic missiles launched from multiple directions including occupied Crimea; Ukrainian air defenses knocked down the large majority of them, but a guided bomb still got through and hit Kharkiv, injuring six people — five of them hospitalized — and starting fires at a city market and nearby apartment buildings. That's roughly in line with the pattern we've seen most nights this month, not an escalation on its own. No new fighting reported between the Houthis and Saudi Arabia since earlier in the week; the regional defense pact between Saudi Arabia, Pakistan and Turkey is still holding as far as we can confirm.

On Iran and the strait: no confirmed movement on the diplomatic track — Washington sent a counter-proposal to Tehran through Qatari mediators earlier this week, and neither side has said what's actually in it. No new confirmed attacks on shipping since Tuesday night's incidents. The shipping-traffic numbers themselves are still badly lagged — the most recent published count has transits running at something like three or four ships a day, versus a normal pre-crisis pace in the mid-eighties, but that data is already a few weeks stale by the time it gets published, so treat it as a rough scale, not a today number. Both the diplomatic and physical pictures are running on stale information this morning, so don't read quiet as calm — it might just be quiet data.
