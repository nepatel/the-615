The 6:15, for Wednesday, October 7th. Here's what moved overnight: a tanker in the Strait of Hormuz was hit by an unidentified projectile Tuesday, injuring twelve of its nineteen crew — the worst casualty toll of the seven-month war — and oil snapped back hard on the news, erasing Monday's strange split between rising attacks and falling prices.

Quick numbers for the subway, all roughly as of six, six-thirty this morning. Brent crude is up around a point and a quarter, just past a hundred and one dollars. WTI is near ninety, up about half a point. The ten-year Treasury yield is around five and a third percent, pushing toward a one-year high, and the thirty-year is right there with it. The dollar index is up near a half percent, just over a hundred and two. The VIX jumped almost four percent, to about fifteen and a half. Gold is down a little over one percent, around forty-one hundred. S&P and Nasdaq futures are both red by a few tenths of a percent, after record closes yesterday. Tokyo and Hong Kong both closed down around three-quarters of a percent, and London and Frankfurt are lower too. Gas is a touch under four thirty-seven a gallon nationally. Fed rate-hike odds for this month's meeting are stuck at a cycle-low sixteen percent, but December's hike odds jumped to seventy-three. And the Yankees play an elimination game tonight, down two games to none.

Here's the lead. The Hormuz campaign just recorded its worst casualty toll since the war started back in February. A tanker called the On Peace was hit by an unidentified projectile Tuesday — twelve of its nineteen crew were hurt, most of them Indian nationals, and Oman's military airlifted ten of them to the hospital. That came two days after a Greek-flagged LNG carrier, the Maran Gas Mystras, took a hit near the waterline leaving the strait on Sunday — that crew was fine and the ship kept moving under its own power. The UK's maritime authority says there's been at least one attack a day in the strait or the Gulf of Aden since the start of the month, and nobody's claimed responsibility for either strike.

Oil reversed hard on that news, and here's why it's interesting: it's the exact opposite of what happened Monday. Monday, this brief reported crude falling even as four separate tankers got hit, because Middle East exports were actually running above pre-war levels and Saudi Arabia had just deepened its discount to Asian buyers — the market was trading the flow of oil, not the headlines about attacks. That decoupling didn't survive a second day of escalation, especially with the war's first double-digit casualty count. Oil's pricing the headline again. And here's the thing — the flow data hasn't actually been updated; the transit count feeding these trackers is still stuck on a reading from three days ago. So nobody actually knows if exports are still strong. The market's just betting that this attack matters more than the stale shipping numbers.

That oil move dragged interest rates up with it. The ten-year and thirty-year both climbed in the same stretch of the morning that oil did, and that's the real story for markets today: Friday's terrible jobs report had already taken a Fed rate hike this month off the table — those odds are at sixteen percent, barely moved from Monday. But now an oil-driven pop in inflation expectations is pulling long-term rates up anyway, even though the Fed itself looks like it's on hold. That's a steepening move that wasn't happening Monday. Meanwhile the odds of a hike in December actually firmed up to seventy-three percent from around sixty-five just two days ago — the market pricing the tightening case back in for year-end, precisely because oil stopped cooperating with the inflation-is-cooling story. Stocks are feeling it too: after record closes Tuesday, futures are red this morning, following a tech-led selloff across Asia.

None of that resolves the diplomacy. Qatari mediators met Iran's foreign minister in New York Monday to relay a US counter-offer on Iran's plan to reopen the strait. Trump's already called Iran's original proposal unacceptable, and Iran says the strait stays closed until the US meets its conditions. No outcome from New York is confirmed yet. The real question for the rest of the week is whether next Wednesday's inflation report — not Friday's jobs number — ends up being what decides whether the Fed's hold survives an oil market that just stopped playing along.

On politics: the generic ballot still favors Democrats heading into next month's midterms, but the polls are all over the place — Emerson has them up eleven points, Fox News has them up eight, and YouGov's latest has it down to just two points, the smallest Democratic lead since September. The polling averages are still sitting around five or six points either way — so the spread between pollsters is new, but the overall picture hasn't really moved.

On the economy: Friday's weak jobs report is still the main thing driving the Fed's thinking, and next Wednesday's inflation print is the next big data point — no consensus estimate for it yet. This week's bank earnings, JPMorgan, Citigroup and Goldman Sachs on Tuesday, Bank of America and Morgan Stanley the next day, are going to get read closely for what they say about lending margins now that long-term rates are climbing again. Last month's factory-activity report, which missed expectations, has now been the Fed story for three issues running — it's getting dropped here since nothing new happened with it overnight.

On business: OpenAI reportedly closed a six-point-six-billion-dollar funding round at a hundred-fifty-seven-billion-dollar valuation, led by Thrive Capital, with Microsoft, Nvidia, SoftBank and Abu Dhabi's MGX all putting money in. That would make it one of the three largest venture-backed companies ever, alongside SpaceX and TikTok's parent company. Worth flagging, though — that's coming from secondary reporting, not confirmed directly by OpenAI itself as of this morning.

And a bit more on Hormuz: the tracking service that follows all this logged sixty-three separate incidents in the strait over the last twenty-four hours — though that's a single source, so take it with some caution. The southern shipping corridor is technically open for two-way traffic, but the situation on the ground is genuinely contested: Iran says the strait is closed until its conditions are met, while the Financial Times is reporting that ship captains are being paid a hundred thousand dollars a month to transit it anyway. There's also a wrinkle in the dark-tanker numbers — ships that go dark on their transponders to dodge the war-risk premium. That count actually came in below its recent average for the first time in a while, though the tracking service itself says that's more likely a gap in what they're picking up than a real sign that fewer ships are hiding. Bottom line: the attacks are getting worse, the shipping data is too old to tell us whether oil is still flowing like it was, and this morning's price move is really a bet on the casualty headline mattering more than stale numbers.
