The 6:15, for Friday, November 27th. Here's what moved overnight: a hot inflation print and a brand-new round of tariff proposals sent stocks swinging hard on Wednesday, and most of it reversed by the close.

Quick board, before the lead. Sunday brings OPEC-plus's big ministerial, setting next year's production framework. Early December brings Jensen Huang's run through Congress, now with actual requested dates: December third for a public Senate Banking hearing, with Commerce Secretary Lutnick requested for December tenth. The Fed meets December eighth and ninth, and a hike is now priced at nearly certain. On Wednesday, the S&P closed down a quarter percent, the Nasdaq down about six tenths of a percent, and the Dow actually closed higher, up about a third of a percent, above fifty thousand for a second straight day. Nvidia fell again, almost four percent, its third rough session since Monday's big reinstatement rally. Oil, rates, the dollar, gold — all stale, nothing fresher located this run. Gas is holding around three sixty a gallon nationally. In the Gulf, attacks on tankers continued at an elevated pace, and OPEC-plus's Sunday decision is the next big one there. On the home front: the Yankees are quiet, the Knicks are eight and six heading into tonight's game against Miami, and Northwestern's season is over at six and five, waiting on a bowl assignment.

Now the lead. Wednesday was the most violent trading session of the week, and it's worth understanding why, because the headline doesn't match what actually happened underneath it. First, the data: October's inflation report, the Fed's favorite measure, came in a touch hot — the core rate at three point three percent instead of the three point two percent economists expected. That's a small miss, but it was enough, on its own, to push the odds of a December rate hike up to somewhere around ninety-eight percent. Markets had already more or less priced that in, but now it looks closer to certain.

Then, the same morning, the Trade Representative's office dropped a second shock: proposed tariffs on fourteen trading partners — China, the European Union, Japan, India, South Korea, Taiwan, Vietnam, and others. These are proposals, not law yet — there's a public comment period running into January, and hearings after that — but the rates on the table are real: fifteen percent caps on the EU and Japan, up to thirty-five percent on Vietnam, and an extra twenty percent on China that could double to forty percent if Beijing is found breaking its current trade truce. This whole package exists because the Supreme Court struck down the previous round of tariffs back in February, so Washington is effectively rebuilding its tariff architecture from scratch, in public, in real time.

Put those two things together — a Fed that's basically locked into raising rates, plus a brand-new multi-year tariff overhang on the country's biggest trading partners — and you get Wednesday's whipsaw. The S&P fell as much as three percent at its worst point in the day. The Dow was down more than eleven hundred points at one stage. And then, by the close, almost all of it came back. The Dow finished up on the day. The S&P and the Nasdaq finished down, but only slightly. The one group that didn't bounce back was chips and AI stocks — the same stocks that powered Monday's huge rally when Nvidia's board brought Jensen Huang back as CEO. Nvidia fell again Wednesday, its third losing session in a row since that reinstatement news.

Here's the thing to actually watch, because it's genuinely unresolved: markets are, for the moment, treating Wednesday's news as noise rather than a turning point. That's a read, not a fact. In theory, this is a textbook bad combination — tariffs push prices up, higher rates slow the economy down, and you get both problems at once. But the index-level recovery suggests investors aren't pricing that scenario yet. The most exposed trade is the Nvidia reinstatement rally itself, which is barely a week old and already fading. Another weak session in chip names — on bad holiday retail numbers, a disappointing OPEC decision Sunday, or just a rough Monday reopen — would reopen the exact question Monday's rally was supposed to have settled.

Quickly on the other beats. On the political side, that tariff package is already drawing the predictable reaction — congressional Democrats and several of the targeted governments pushing back, while the Trade Representative frames it as overdue. And Elizabeth Warren's argument that Nvidia still owes Congress answers now has actual dates attached: December third for Huang, December tenth for the Commerce Secretary, though neither is locked in yet.

On the economy, there were two other releases Wednesday morning worth a mention. Home prices nationally, by one index, grew about one and a half percent year over year in September — more of the same gradual slowdown we've been tracking. Consumer confidence for November wasn't available this run — not confirmed as of airtime.

On business: Best Buy had a genuinely strong quarter — comparable sales up about six percent, way ahead of expectations, and the company raised its full-year guidance. That's the first earnings report under its brand-new CEO. It lands right alongside Walmart's strong quarter from earlier in the week, just ahead of today's Black Friday half-day session.

On technology: there's an actual correction to flag from our last issue. We'd reported that the Commerce Department added a company called Megaspeed — a Singapore-based Nvidia customer under investigation for possibly funneling chips to China — to its export blacklist. New reporting says that's backwards: Commerce has actually held off on adding Megaspeed for about two months, even as other parts of the government pushed for it. Nvidia says it's found no evidence of diversion and already cut the company off. Also: SpaceX flew its fifteenth Starship test flight two weeks ago and, for the first time, caught the booster back at the tower on an orbital flight — a real milestone worth noting even this late.

On the Gulf: attacks on tankers near the Strait of Hormuz kept coming through late November, with incidents reported near Oman almost daily, including one odd one on the twenty-second that the maritime monitor described only as "involving military forces," without saying whose. Iran keeps blaming mines; the UK's maritime monitor keeps describing projectile strikes. Nobody's reconciling those two stories, and we're not going to pretend to either. Sunday's OPEC decision matters here too — the group is deciding whether to bring back more of the production capacity it took offline years ago, a call that's gotten harder because the Iran war has made it tough to even audit what some member countries can actually produce right now.

Sports, quickly: the Yankees haven't made a move since mid-November — they're sitting on a qualifying-offer acceptance from reliever David Bednar and three low-key roster additions, and that's it so far this winter. The Knicks are eight and six, with tonight's home game against Miami still to be played as of this recording. Northwestern's football season is done at six and five, three and five in the Big Ten, still waiting to find out which bowl game they're headed to — the selection show is early December.
