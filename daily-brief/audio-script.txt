The 6:15, for Sunday, September the 13th. Here's what moved overnight: an Iranian ship just got hit off Qeshm Island, hours before Iran and the Gulf states are set to sign a Hormuz shipping deal in Oman tomorrow — without Bahrain in the room.

Quick lay of the board before we get into it. Markets are closed through tonight, so Friday's numbers still stand: Brent settled around one-oh-four and a half, WTI just over a hundred, the ten-year at just under five percent, the two-year near four and two-thirds, the thirty-year a little above five and a third though that one's still thinly sourced, stocks all up around one percent on the day with the volatility index down sharply, the dollar index basically flat, gas is a touch over four-thirty a gallon nationally, the Hormuz strait is into its sixth month of closure with transits still running at a small fraction of normal and a dark-tanker count that looks elevated, the Yankees dropped one to the Mets Saturday, Delaware holds the last primary of this cycle Tuesday, and the FOMC decision is Wednesday with hike odds — yes, hike, not cut — sitting somewhere in the high eighties percent, up hard from where they were just a week ago.

Now the lead. Early this morning, an Iranian commercial ship was hit by an unidentified projectile off Qeshm Island, not far from Bandar Abbas. One crew member was killed, four wounded, out of a crew of ten. Iranian state media quoted the local governor blaming what he called a terrorist enemy, but nobody's claimed it yet, and nobody's said what actually hit the ship.

That happened less than a day before the biggest diplomatic moment of this whole crisis so far. Tomorrow, Monday, foreign ministers from Iran, the Gulf Cooperation Council, and Iraq are meeting in a town called Salalah, in Oman, to sign an agreement setting up a joint Oman-Iran shipping corridor through the strait, and to notify the International Maritime Organization about it. It would be the first time senior diplomats from Iran and the Gulf have all sat down together since the US and Israel started their campaign against Iran back in February.

But one Gulf state is sitting this one out. Bahrain announced Saturday it won't join any meeting with Iran in the room until the two countries actually restore diplomatic relations — they've been broken since 2016, back when Bahrain cut ties in step with Saudi Arabia, and Saudi Arabia itself patched things up with Tehran a few years later while Bahrain never did. Bahrain also pointed to two fresh attacks this week: one on a big pipeline in Saudi Arabia, and one on an ammonia tank in Bahrain itself, which they said almost caused a disaster. Their foreign ministry's basic argument is: you can't build regional security while ignoring attacks and staying quiet about the consequences, or by appeasing the country doing the attacking. That said, Bahrain was careful to say it's not opposed to the diplomacy itself — it passed its objection along to Oman through normal channels and says it still supports the effort in principle. It's just not showing up to sign anything next to Iran right now.

Here's why investors should care. This is the exact same story that moved oil on Friday, when it was still just a rumor of a meeting — Brent dropped almost three percent, WTI a little less, purely on reports that Iran and the Gulf were about to talk. Now it's not a rumor anymore. There's a date, a location, and an actual document getting signed. So when futures reopen tonight, the real test is whether an actual signature is a bigger deal than the rumor already was — or a smaller one, especially since by Iran's own account, this deal would still keep American and Israeli ships out of the new route. That's a narrow corridor agreement, not a reopening of the whole strait.

And the timing matters for the Fed. This lands two days before Wednesday's rate decision, where hike odds have been climbing mostly because of this same oil-driven inflation scare. If the market decides tomorrow's signing is real de-escalation, even partial, the Fed could end up hiking into a shock that's already started to fade by the time the statement comes out Wednesday afternoon — a central bank one meeting behind the market, essentially, even if nobody would call it an outright policy mistake. Worth watching how the committee's language handles that possibility Wednesday.

One more thing to keep in perspective: none of this is a ceasefire. Transit through the strait is still running at a sliver of normal, the war-risk insurance premium for big tankers hasn't budged in almost a month, and a ship just got hit with an unidentified weapon hours before the talks. This is confidence-building running alongside an active conflict, not the end of one.

Moving to the rest of the world. Overnight into Sunday, Russia launched something like four hundred fifty drones at Ukraine — Ukrainian air defense says they shot down around four hundred nine of them — plus a handful of cruise missiles, some guided missiles, and ballistic missiles, all fired from several directions at once, including occupied Crimea and the Sea of Azov. Strikes hit more than a dozen regions stretching from the north down to the south and east, and missile hits on two metal-working plants killed two people and injured six others. That's roughly triple the drone count from just two nights before, though we don't have a fresh damage assessment to compare apples to apples, so take the trend line loosely rather than precisely.

Back on the Iran side — there's an old loose thread worth flagging. Remember that defense pact between Pakistan, Saudi Arabia, and Turkey — the one modeled loosely on NATO's mutual-defense idea — and the reports that Iran got invited to join it? There's still no fresh response from Tehran to Pakistan's comment earlier this month about activating that pact. The only thing on the record from Iran is a comment from back in August saying they weren't worried about it — but that's old, it predates the newer comment by about a month, so it doesn't actually answer the question everyone's still waiting on. And separately, that claim that the new Oman route would exclude U.S. and Israeli ships — that's still just Iran's own characterization, and nobody else — not Oman, not the U.S., not any Gulf government — has confirmed it one way or the other.
