The 6:15, for Monday, August 17th. Here's what moved overnight: President Trump says he's going to declare the Strait of Hormuz a territory of the United States once Iran is, in his words, defeated — and over the weekend, ship traffic through the strait hit zero for the first time in this entire war.

Quickly, where everything else sits this morning: Brent crude's pushing back up near ninety dollars a barrel, up about a point overnight; WTI's in the low eighties, though the contract just rolled to a new month so that number's a little noisy; the ten-year Treasury's around four and a half percent, the two-year near four percent, though those are Friday's official numbers and live quotes this morning are running a bit higher; the dollar's basically flat, maybe a touch softer; stock futures are up modestly, building on record closes last week; the VIX is in the low teens on Friday's close; gold's just under forty-four hundred an ounce; Asian markets closed higher again overnight, Europe's roughly flat in early trading; gas at the pump is a little above four dollars nationally, up slightly on the week; and on Hormuz, we're on day one hundred sixty-nine of the closure, dark tankers running dark are up sharply, and war-risk insurance is still sitting around thirty times normal.

Now the lead. President Trump said Friday he plans to declare the Strait of Hormuz American territory once Iran is defeated — he says it'll happen pretty soon. That's an escalation from two weeks ago, when he was just claiming the Navy controlled it completely; now he's talking about actually claiming the strait itself. Iran's response was remarkably unified across factions that usually don't agree on much: the deputy foreign minister said the strait can't be seized by a tweet or an aircraft carrier or an executive order; the head of the judiciary called the claim delusional; the parliament speaker said Iran already won this war, both militarily and politically. The read here is that Trump may have accidentally handed Tehran a unifying talking point at exactly the moment its own economy is struggling — the Iranian president himself said this weekend that they used to sell oil and now they can't.

While the rhetoric was escalating, so were the actual attacks. Three tankers linked to the UAE's state oil company were hit Thursday and Friday, and a bulk carrier was struck by an unidentified projectile near the Omani coast over the weekend. The UAE called it a flagrant violation of international law. What's notable is that Iran seems to be concentrating its strikes on one specific company's fleet now, rather than spreading them around — that reads like a deliberate message aimed squarely at the UAE.

And the traffic numbers back up how bad this has gotten. Satellite tracking that usually lags about a week showed just a single vessel transiting back on the ninth. A separate weekly count showed total transits down about a fifth week over week. And then came the number that really stands out: five vessels crossed on Saturday, and zero on Sunday — compared with thirty-one the weekend before. Zero is a number this war hasn't produced before, even at its worst.

Oil is pricing that deterioration, not the rhetoric. Brent settled Friday just under ninety dollars and is bid a little higher this morning, up around five dollars from two Mondays ago. WTI's harder to read cleanly right now because the front-month contract just rolled over. Stocks, meanwhile, are mostly shrugging this off on the surface — futures are higher again this morning, building on back-to-back record closes last week, even though retail sales and consumer confidence both came in weak the same week. That's a pattern we've flagged before: the market's treating soft growth data as good news for the Fed, as long as it's not showing up in earnings yet. Energy stocks are where the strait crisis is actually visible in prices right now.

And that connects straight to the Fed. Remember, July's inflation report already showed energy prices up close to fifteen percent year over year because of this war's effect on oil. If crude keeps pushing back toward ninety, that sets up for an even bigger energy contribution next month. That's landing right as the Fed conversation had been moving the other way — odds of a September rate hike on the futures market roughly cut in half over the past week, from around sixty percent down to the low thirties, after a weak retail sales number and a really ugly consumer sentiment report. So the question now is whether a fresh oil shock outweighs two soft economic reports, and we'll get more clarity when Fed Chair Kevin Warsh gives his first Jackson Hole speech as chair, at the end of next week — a speech that's now also shadowed by a separate fight: Trump's given Fed Governor Lisa Cook until August twenty-sixth to respond to a renewed effort to remove her.

On politics: that government funding fight from a couple weeks ago is still not resolved. The Senate's version funds the government through mid-December; the House's own version, passed in July, only runs to early December — a real gap the House hasn't addressed because they're still in recess until September. Separately, that fight over Fed Governor Lisa Cook is back — the White House sent her a new letter in early August reviving the effort to remove her, with a deadline of August twenty-sixth, landing right before that Jackson Hole speech.

On the economy: last week's data was mixed but leaned dovish overall. Inflation came in right in line with expectations in July, both headline and core. Producer prices were basically flat. But then Friday brought the two numbers that really moved things: retail sales fell more than half a percent, the first drop in nine months, and consumer sentiment cratered well below what economists expected. Together, that's why rate-hike odds got cut roughly in half over the course of the week.

On business: three closely watched earnings reports came back mixed last week. CoreWeave beat estimates and its stock jumped about twenty percent. Cisco also beat, and still fell more than eight percent — investors focused on margin pressure instead of the beat. Applied Materials posted record revenue but missed slightly on the top line and fell about five percent. The pattern across all three: strong results aren't clearing an increasingly high bar. Elsewhere, SpaceX closed its sixty-billion-dollar acquisition of the coding company Cursor, the largest startup acquisition on record, and AMD priced its biggest-ever bond deal to help fund its computing partnership with Anthropic.

On technology: global chip sales hit another record in June, up well over a hundred percent year over year. Google shipped a new mid-tier AI model last week while its flagship model stays delayed. And Microsoft's reported plan to order more than three hundred thousand AI chips from Taiwan Semiconductor is still unconfirmed by either company.

On geopolitics: Russia and Ukraine traded their biggest strikes in weeks — Ukraine sent hundreds of drones at Moscow, Russia hit Kyiv with missiles overnight. A suspected Russian drone also crossed into Romanian airspace and got shot down by a NATO jet, the fourth time that's happened this year. And two U.S. tariff actions — a fifty percent tariff on Canadian goods and new steel and aluminum tariffs — both take effect Tuesday.

Sports: the Yankees salvaged the final game of their series with Toronto Sunday, winning in extra innings on a walk-off homer, after dropping the first two. They're sixty-nine and fifty-five, second in the AL East, five and a half games back of Tampa Bay. The Knicks are still in the off-season, with Josh Hart's contract extension still unsigned. And Northwestern's season opener is confirmed for September fifth against South Dakota State, with the starting quarterback job still up for grabs.
