Here's what moved overnight: volatility cratered and oil kept falling on Friday even as two new tanker strikes hit the Strait of Hormuz and Iran's Revolutionary Guard issued its bluntest threat yet — "destruction" for any ship that tries to pass without authorization.

Now the lead. Friday's close read like a market that's decided to treat the Gulf war as background noise instead of a live conflict. The VIX settled just under fifteen, down hard from Thursday — though the exact percentage move didn't add up cleanly between sources, so take that drop as directionally real but not precisely sourced. The S&P and Nasdaq both nudged higher, the Dow dipped just a touch — basically a shrug, in the same week the Fed delivered its first rate hike since 2023. Brent fell under a percent, WTI eased about a percent and a half, continuing a slide that's now running alongside, not in response to, a harder Iranian posture in the strait.

And that posture did get harder overnight. The UK's maritime trade agency said a tanker was hit by an unidentified projectile exiting the strait Wednesday night. Separately, Iran's Revolutionary Guard claimed Thursday night that it struck a Togo-flagged tanker for trying an unauthorized transit — a claim the Associated Press couldn't independently verify. And the Guard's navy went further, warning on state media that any vessel attempting passage without authorization would face, quote, "destruction" — a blunter line than the "illegal crossing" language they used around the disputed strike earlier this month. None of it moved Friday's oil tape at all.

The mechanism here is the same one flagged in the last issue: crude futures are tracking reported Saudi and Omani workaround supply routes more than the actual security situation inside the strait, and this run couldn't get fresher transit data to test that against — the vessel-count figures are still stuck on an unreconciled split from four days ago. What is new is that the market's other stress gauges are leaning the same direction as oil. Gold hit a fresh high. The dollar's brief move above the hundred-point mark on the dollar index didn't survive the session. The ten-year barely budged. Stocks up, volatility down, oil down, gold up, rates flat — that combination looks less like a read on any one catalyst and more like a broad de-risking heading into quarter-end.

Here's the tension going forward: the Fed hiked this week partly on an energy-driven inflation impulse that oil's own slide is now quietly unwinding, and the committee's dot plot penciled in as many as two more hikes this cycle. With the next meeting confirmed for late October — and no fresh dot plot at that one — the Fed has about five weeks to see whether the supply-workaround story holds, or whether one of this week's two tanker incidents turns into something that reprices everything at once. For positioning, a VIX in the mid-teens heading into a live war in the world's most important oil chokepoint is cheap optionality by the numbers. Whether that's an opportunity or a mispricing depends on which of Friday's two data points — the tanker attacks or the calm equity tape — turns out to be the noise. Either way, this is the kind of gap that tends to resolve suddenly rather than gradually: either the workaround supply keeps flowing and the strait risk fades into the background for good, or one more incident forces the market to reprice all at once, and the five weeks between now and that October meeting is exactly the window where that could happen.

On to geopolitics. Russian guided bombs killed two municipal workers and injured eight people, including a thirteen-year-old, in a nine-story residential building in Sumy on Friday, part of a wave of strikes that also hit Kyiv, Zaporizhzhia, and Odesa this week. Ukraine hit back at an oil refinery northeast of Moscow and struck warplanes and helicopters at a base near Rostov, according to President Zelensky. Separately, a bipartisan sanctions bill targeting Russian energy revenue has reportedly reached President Trump's desk — one senator warned that Putin is betting he can simply outlast Western support before that bill starts to bite. The specific provisions and any signing timeline weren't confirmed this run.

On Iran and Hormuz: the Gulf foreign ministers' meeting with Iran that was postponed earlier this month got a fuller explanation this week, though a confusing one — Iranian officials say Saudi Arabia asked for the postponement, while Oman's foreign minister called it a mutual decision made in the interest of consensus. No new date has been set. And as covered in the lead, this week brought two fresh tanker incidents alongside the Revolutionary Guard's bluntest threat of the conflict so far. The strait has been under partial Iranian closure since the U.S.-Israel air war began back on February twenty-eighth, and that basic picture hasn't shifted either way this run.
