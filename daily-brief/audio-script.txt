The 6:15, for Monday, August 24th. Here's what moved overnight: Treasury Secretary Bessent goes public this afternoon with what the administration is calling economic D-Day against Iran, and oil is actually selling off ahead of it, not pricing it in.

Quickly, the numbers: Brent's sitting in the low nineties, down a bit this morning after last week's big jump; the ten-year's just under four and three quarters percent; gold just hit a fresh high near forty-six hundred an ounce; stock futures are a touch lower; and the Strait of Hormuz is still basically shut, with only a handful of ships crossing on a given day.

Here's the lead. At two o'clock this afternoon, Bessent holds a press conference to lay out what he's calling the single greatest financial offensive ever against Iran — the toughest sanctions package yet, paired with the naval blockade that's already in place. The reported plan is to go after anyone still buying Iranian oil, moving its money, or supporting its shipping — basically secondary sanctions aimed at squeezing the countries and banks still doing business with Tehran. Bessent's specifically calling on China to get on board, which tells you Chinese refiners buying discounted Iranian crude are a real target here, not just a talking point.

Iran didn't wait for the announcement to respond. Their foreign ministry is calling it economic warfare, state media's calling it a sign of American weakness — the usual bravado. But more concretely, Iran's warning it'll seize any ship that doesn't follow its new transit rules in the strait, and it's telling other Gulf countries not to go along with the American pressure campaign. That's aimed squarely at the UAE, which already cut off all economic ties with Iran a few days ago after accusing Tehran of firing missiles at its territory.

Now here's the part that should actually catch your attention this morning: oil is falling, not rising, ahead of this announcement. Brent settled Friday just over ninety-four dollars, and it's down more than a percent this morning, into the low nineties; WTI's following it lower too, into the mid-eighties. Normally you'd expect a toughest-ever sanctions package on a chokepoint that's already nearly shut down to push oil higher, not lower. It's doing the opposite. Two ways to read that. One, this is just plain profit-taking — oil already jumped about five bucks, six percent, last week pricing in exactly this kind of escalation, so a pullback into the actual announcement isn't crazy. Two, and this is the sharper read: after five months of blockade and threats that haven't actually reopened the strait or meaningfully choked off Iranian oil finding buyers through middlemen, the market may just be discounting Bessent's rhetoric at this point — tough talk that's been heard before, from this same podium, without much follow-through on the tape. Either way, this cuts both directions for the rest of the week. If the actual sanctions list disappoints relative to the rhetoric, oil's early fade looks smart in hindsight. If it actually reaches big-volume buyers like Chinese refiners, you could see a sharp reversal, and fast.

Zoom out a little and there's a separate story about the dollar and Treasuries that has nothing to do with Iran. Gold just hit its highest level since mid-May, extending a rally that started after the Treasury surprised markets by upsizing its long-bond buybacks last week. That tells you some of the safe-haven buying this month is about worries over Washington's own finances, not just the Gulf. The ten-year's still up near four and three quarters percent, so if Bessent's own sanctions do end up squeezing oil supply, that shock lands on a bond market that's already jumpy. Stock futures are barely moving on any of this so far, but this week's got Nvidia earnings, a big batch of inflation and growth data, and the Fed's Jackson Hole conference all coming up — so there's not a lot of room for today's sanctions news to be a non-event.

A few other things. Fed Governor Lisa Cook now has just two days left to respond to Trump's push to remove her over old mortgage-fraud allegations — her lawyers have called those allegations baseless — and that deadline lands Wednesday, the same day as Nvidia's earnings and a cluster of inflation and GDP data. Separately, the funding standoff in Congress is still unresolved, with the House still out on recess and no urgency from either side to close the gap between the two chambers' bills.

Speaking of the Fed, betting markets have actually cooled off on the idea of a rate hike next month — odds are down to around thirty percent for a hike, with a hold now the strong favorite, a real shift from the more hawkish mood right after last week's Fed minutes came out, as softer hiring and cooling inflation data reassert themselves. Nobody's pricing in a cut, though. Goldman Sachs is reportedly telling clients not to lean too hard into that hawkish bet ahead of Wednesday's data, which will be the next real test of which read is right.

On the business side, Anthropic's IPO plans are getting more concrete — their revenue reportedly jumped to eleven and a half billion dollars last quarter, way up from under a billion a year earlier, and reports now point to a pricing date in late October on the Nasdaq at a valuation target around two trillion dollars, roughly double where their last private round valued them. Nvidia reports Wednesday, with Wall Street expecting revenue right around ninety-two billion dollars, basically in line with what the company itself guided — which some see as a low bar that sets up for a bigger surprise either way. And Microsoft's reportedly getting closer to unveiling its own AI chip, the Maia 300, as soon as next month, still trying to lean less on Nvidia, though neither Microsoft nor its chip partner has confirmed the numbers being reported.

Overseas, Ukraine hit a radar station and a drone-boat storage site in occupied Crimea overnight — the latest exchange in a war that's shown no sign of slowing down. And back in the Gulf, that tanker Iran seized last week, the Amara, is still sitting motionless near Qeshm Island more than a week later — no sign of it being released, no sign Iran's backing off its new permit system. Shipping through the strait is still running at a tiny fraction of what it was before this crisis started — we're talking single digits of vessels on some days, against dozens a day before the war. The bigger question this week is whether Iran responds to today's sanctions announcement with more seizures or strikes, rather than just talk, and whether the UAE's trade cutoff with Iran, announced a few days ago, actually turns into real restrictions on cargo and money moving between the two countries, or stays mostly symbolic.

Sports. The Yankees beat Toronto eight to three yesterday, capped by a two-run single from Ben Rice — they're seventy-four and fifty-five, second in the AL East. The Knicks are in the off-season, no game, and Josh Hart's contract extension is still unsigned. And Northwestern football is also quiet — though the program did land a thirty-five million dollar commitment from the Uihlein family for the next three years, announced a couple weeks back. Season opener's still two weeks out.
