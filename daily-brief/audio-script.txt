The 6:15, for Sunday, November 15th. Here's what moved overnight — which, since it's the weekend, is less than usual, but the Nvidia story that's dominated this brief for a week just picked up a new wrinkle worth four minutes of your subway ride. Jensen Huang's deadline to answer a congressional request for a closed-door briefing passed quietly on Wednesday, the same day his resignation as CEO officially took effect, and as of this morning, nobody has reported whether he actually responded.

Let's do the board in one breath. Kansas's county-by-county audit of the governor's race is due to wrap up tomorrow, with the official canvass to follow after that. Nvidia reports earnings Tuesday afternoon — its first call ever without Jensen Huang in the room — with Wall Street looking for around two dollars and forty-seven cents a share on roughly a hundred nine billion dollars in revenue, which lines up closely with the company's own guidance. Markets are closed for the weekend after Friday's close, which was down slightly on the day but still capped the best week for stocks since August. The Hormuz numbers are unchanged and still stale — call it one in ten of normal tanker traffic through the strait. And on the home front: the Yankees' closer, David Bednar, has a twenty-three-million-dollar qualifying-offer decision due Tuesday; the Knicks won their fourth straight before Wednesday's result against Cleveland went unconfirmed again, for the second issue running; and Northwestern lost at Ohio State on Saturday to fall to six and four on the season.

Now the lead, and here's the part that's actually new. Nvidia's board put it in writing this week: Jensen Huang is out as president, CEO, and director, effective last Wednesday, the eleventh. Colette Kress, the company's longtime chief financial officer, steps up as interim CEO while keeping her finance job, and Harvey Jones — a Nvidia board member going back to the nineteen-nineties and the former CEO of the chip-design firm Synopsys — becomes the company's first truly independent board chair. No timeline yet for a permanent replacement; the board says it's looking at both internal and external candidates. That part, if you've been following along, you've heard before.

Here's what's new. Back on November fifth, three Republican committee chairs in the House Foreign Affairs Committee asked Huang to sit for a closed-door briefing on November eighteenth — the day after Nvidia's earnings call — about his relationship with a Chinese businesswoman that U.S. intelligence reportedly believes passed information back to Beijing. They gave him until November eleventh to say yes or propose another date. November eleventh came and went — and it was also, as it happens, the exact day his resignation took effect — and nobody has reported whether he ever actually answered that letter. The committee's chair has said he would, quote, absolutely consider a subpoena if Huang stays silent. So you've now got two separate accountability tracks running in parallel and neither one's resolved: a board-level investigation into a man who no longer works at the company doing the investigating, and a congressional inquiry that nobody can even confirm he's responding to.

Layer onto that a slightly jumpier market than the one we had earlier in the week. On Friday, the S&P and the Nasdaq both closed down for a second straight session, even though the week overall turned out to be the best one for stocks since August. One market recap — and we want to be upfront that we could not independently confirm this against the government's own numbers — pointed to three things driving Friday specifically: renewed selling in chip stocks, Nvidia and AMD and Broadcom all included; a producer-price inflation number that reportedly came in a touch cooler than Wall Street expected, up three-tenths of a percent on the month against a four-tenths forecast, though up six and a half percent over the past year; and the ten-year Treasury yield, which reportedly spiked intraday to its highest level since October on worries the Fed might not cut rates again in December. Bitcoin, for what it's worth, dropped below seventy thousand dollars for the first time in nearly three months — a separate risk-off signal moving in the same direction as the chip-stock retreat. None of that is really about Nvidia's leadership vacuum specifically. But put together, it means Tuesday's earnings call now lands on a market that's a little more nervous than it looked just a few days ago — not the clean rebound setup that Wednesday's rally had seemed to promise.

So, worth watching before Tuesday: does Huang ever answer that briefing request; does Nvidia name a permanent CEO before the call; and does that producer-price number actually get confirmed by the government's own release, because right now it's resting on a single source we haven't been able to cross-check.

Quickly around the rest of the board. In politics: Kansas still hasn't called its governor's race. The statewide audit — which involves hand-counting roughly ten percent of precincts in every county, including about a hundred twenty-five thousand ballots in Johnson County alone — wraps up tomorrow, with the county canvasses to follow after that. The Democrat's lead is still around seventy-six hundred votes, essentially unchanged from our last issue; nothing there has moved.

In technology, beyond Nvidia: Anthropic's IPO timing shifted a little over the weekend. One tracker tied to the company's own investor-information site now has pricing on the eighteenth and trading starting the nineteenth — a day later on the trading start than what we'd heard before — though a different outlet says Thanksgiving week is actually more likely, and nobody's reconciled those two competing timelines yet. The deal terms themselves haven't changed: eight hundred twenty-five million shares in a seventy-to-seventy-eight-dollar range, a twenty-six-billion-dollar private placement from Nvidia, Google, and Microsoft, and a Nasdaq listing under the ticker A-N-T.

On Iran and the strait: honestly, not much moved. Secretary of State Rubio said earlier in the week that there's been, quote, progress made in those talks, but not finality yet — talking about Oman-mediated discussions on reopening the strait. That's a notably softer line than what he'd said just a day earlier, when he said the U.S. wasn't close to a breakthrough, and nobody's explained publicly what changed in between. Iran, for its part, says it's only talking to Oman, not directly to Washington. The tanker-traffic numbers are the same stale figures we've had for two weeks running now — still roughly one-tenth of normal transits through the strait, and October's oil flow still around a third below the pre-war baseline.

One quick correction before we get to sports, because we think it matters to say this out loud rather than just quietly fix it. Our last issue reported Northwestern's record heading into Saturday's game as three and seven. That was wrong. Multiple box scores — Fox Sports and CBS Sports both — confirm the Wildcats were actually six and three going in. We're flagging that as an error on our part, not restating it as if it were new information.
