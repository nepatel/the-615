The 6:15, for Saturday, August 29th. Here's what moved overnight: Fed Chair Kevin Warsh gave his first Jackson Hole speech Friday, and it landed hawkish — bond traders now put the odds of a September rate hike at roughly six in ten, up from about a third the day before.

Quick sweep of the board: stocks gave back Thursday's Nvidia-fueled gains, with the S and P off a quarter percent and the Nasdaq down about half a percent; the two-year Treasury yield jumped about twelve basis points, its biggest one-day move after a Jackson Hole speech in a generation, while the ten-year and thirty-year barely budged; Brent's sitting in the high eighties, WTI in the low eighties, though nobody's got a clean settle on either grade this weekend; gold's holding above forty-six hundred an ounce; the dollar firmed up a touch; retail gas is right around four-ten a gallon nationally; and in the Gulf, Friday into Saturday was the first quiet twenty-four hours in recent memory — no strikes logged on shipping through the strait, though tanker traffic is still running a fraction of normal and the trackers still can't agree by how much. Looking ahead, mark your calendar: jobs report a week from Friday, Labor Day the Monday after, Canada's retaliatory tariffs kick in that Tuesday, CPI the Friday after that, and then the Fed decision on September sixteenth — the meeting Friday's speech was all about.

Here's the lead. Kevin Warsh stood up at Jackson Hole Friday and did something Fed chairs usually avoid — he assigned blame. "The responsibility for sixty-five months of sustained, elevated inflation sits squarely with the central bank," he said. Translation: this is on us, and we're not done. He said this summer's inflation readings were better than expected, but they don't tell him the underlying trend has actually improved. Inflation by the Fed's favorite gauge is still running close to four percent over the past year, well above the two percent target, even though he thinks the economy's at full employment and doing well overall.

Now, Warsh has a whole philosophy about not giving forward guidance — he calls it "a discipline, not a decision," meaning he won't tell you what the Fed's going to do next. But he didn't need to say it outright. Traders heard "we have work to do" and immediately started pricing in a hike. Odds of a quarter-point increase at the September meeting jumped to around six in ten, from about a third the day before — the sharpest one-day repricing since Warsh took the job back in May. The two-year yield, which tracks near-term Fed expectations closely, jumped about twelve basis points in a single session — the biggest post-Jackson-Hole move investors have seen this century, according to Yahoo Finance's markets desk. The ten-year moved less, the thirty-year barely moved at all — so short rates jumped and the long end stayed put, which is exactly the pattern you'd expect if the market believes a hike is coming without panicking about the long-run outlook.

Stocks gave back Thursday's rally — the S and P down a quarter percent, the Nasdaq down about half a percent, the Dow basically flat. Nvidia had juiced everything Wednesday night; Warsh un-juiced a chunk of it Friday. And here's the read: this doesn't look like the market pricing in a Fed mistake. It looks like the market pricing in a Fed fight. Warsh is the first chair this cycle willing to treat a strong labor market as a green light to hike, rather than a reason to hold off — and traders are taking him at his word.

There's a second layer here too. Thursday's jobless claims came in at two-oh-three thousand, better than expected, which is exactly the kind of data Warsh is pointing to when he says employment isn't the constraint anymore — inflation is. That leaves two big data points between here and the September sixteenth decision: the September jobs report and the August CPI print in mid-September. Either one could flip this repricing right back — a soft payrolls number or a cool CPI print would undo a lot of what happened Friday, just as fast as it happened. And there's a wrinkle with the Treasury: Secretary Bessent's been buying back long-dated bonds all August, which has been pulling long yields down — but that's now sitting awkwardly next to a Fed chair actively talking up the short end. Two parts of the government pulling the bond market in different directions, and Friday's yield-curve move — short rates up, long rates flat — made that tension a lot harder to ignore than it's been all month.

Quick hits on the rest. In politics, Fed Governor Lisa Cook's lawyer filed a response to the White House's renewed effort to remove her — and instead of just denying the mortgage fraud allegations, the letter pointed out that Trump himself, along with Treasury Secretary Bessent and Attorney General Blanche, have all also claimed multiple primary residences on their own mortgage paperwork. No response from the White House yet.

In business, Marvell's earnings beat Thursday night — revenue up about thirty-six percent year over year, roughly in line on profit — but the number that mattered was the forward guide: next quarter's revenue outlook came in well above what Wall Street expected. Combined with Nvidia's own strong guide earlier in the week, that's a second confirmation that AI chip demand is broadening out, not just concentrated in one company.

In geopolitics, Russia and Ukraine kept hitting each other's economic infrastructure over the weekend — Russia struck a port terminal in Mykolaiv and logistics warehouses in Kyiv; Ukraine's drones killed three people in Russia's Belgorod region. Both sides are now going after fuel and logistics rather than front lines.

And in the Gulf, it was actually a quiet stretch — no strikes on shipping logged in the past day, which hasn't happened in a while. But the tanker that got hit earlier in the week off Oman still hasn't been attributed to anyone, and the transit-count trackers still can't agree on how bad things are — one says traffic's at four percent of normal, another says it's basically flat week over week. Nobody's reconciled that gap yet. War-risk insurance for a single big tanker passage through the strait is still running around ten million dollars a trip, roughly forty times what it cost before all this started, and about a quarter of the tankers being tracked are still going dark for stretches of the day.

Sports, quick: Yankees beat the Red Sox one-nothing Friday, they're seventy-six and fifty-eight, four games back of Tampa Bay in the East, but holding the top Wild Card spot. Knicks' Josh Hart extension talks are still unresolved, still eyeing something in the sixty to sixty-two million dollar range over three years. And Northwestern named its six team captains this week ahead of their season opener against South Dakota State next Saturday.
