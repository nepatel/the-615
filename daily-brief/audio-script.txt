The 6:15, for Sunday, August 30th. Here's what moved overnight: oil is heading for close to a five percent weekly loss, and the reason ties straight back into the Hormuz numbers this show has been tracking for months.

Quick sweep of the board, since it's the weekend and markets are shut: stocks closed out Friday lower, the S and P off a quarter percent, the Nasdaq down about half a percent, both giving back Thursday's rally after Fed Chair Warsh's hawkish Jackson Hole speech; the two-year Treasury yield is sitting in the low fours, the ten-year and thirty-year barely moved; the dollar's a touch firmer; Brent's in the high eighties and WTI's around eighty-three, both down close to five percent on the week, though nobody's got a single clean settle price to point to this weekend; gold's somewhere in the mid-forty-six-hundreds, though the quotes we're seeing disagree by more than a hundred and fifty dollars, so take that one with a grain of salt; retail gas is flat on the week, a little over four dollars a gallon nationally. Looking ahead: the House still has to vote on the Senate's funding bill, which passed ninety to six and would keep the government open through mid-December; jobs report a week from Friday; Labor Day the Monday after that; Canada's retaliatory tariffs kick in that Tuesday; CPI the Friday after; and then the Fed's rate decision on September sixteenth.

Here's the lead. Crude oil fell through the week and into Friday's close, and multiple market wraps put the weekly loss at somewhere around five percent — bigger than you'd expect just from the ongoing Hormuz closure story on its own. So what's going on? One energy-news wrap flagged the real reason Friday: traders are increasingly starting to treat the Iran situation as more of an economic and sanctions standoff than an actual imminent threat to oil supply. And there's real data behind that shift, though it's messier than a clean story.

One tracker, Lloyd's List, counted the crossings through the strait for the week of August seventeenth through the twenty-third, and found over a hundred transits — more than thirty percent above the week before. Westbound crossings alone were up by about a third too. That's a real, measured pickup in traffic. But right next to that number sits a completely different one: PortWatch, the World Bank-backed tracker, looked at just a single day, August twenty-third, and counted three vessels — against a normal pre-crisis baseline of around eighty-five a day. That's about four percent of normal. Same strait, same general window, two very different pictures.

Here's the thing — both of those are real counts. They're just not measuring the same thing. Lloyd's List is looking at a whole week's worth of ship movements across the strait. PortWatch is comparing a single day's throughput against the old baseline. So you can have a "busier week" story and a "still basically closed" story be true at exactly the same time, and what happened Friday is that the oil market started trading the direction — busier — rather than the level — still closed. This show isn't going to pretend those two numbers reconcile into one tidy figure. They don't, and forcing them together would tell you less than reporting them side by side.

Now, why does this matter beyond the oil desk? Because a real loosening at the strait, even a partial one, actually matters more to the Fed right now than it does to shipping companies. Remember, Friday's whole story was Warsh going hawkish at Jackson Hole and the market suddenly pricing in close to sixty percent odds of a September rate hike, up from about a third the day before. The next inflation read before that decision is the August CPI report, out September eleventh. Gas prices were already flat on the week even before Friday's crude drop had time to work through to the pump. So if this transit pickup is real and it holds — and we don't know yet if it will — a further drop in oil would be one of the only genuinely dovish things working against a Fed chair who just spent Friday making the hawkish case as hard as he could.

And the strait itself was actually quiet the last couple of days. The one confirmed strike since our last issue was Wednesday, when a Kuwaiti-flagged tanker called the Al Salam Two got hit above the waterline while crossing eastbound — small fire, put out fast, crew was fine, and nobody's claimed responsibility for it. Nothing new logged in the last twenty-four hours. War-risk insurance for tankers is holding right around forty times what it cost before all this started, so insurers clearly aren't ready to call this transit uptick real yet either.

Quick hits on the rest. In politics: the Senate passed a funding deal Friday, ninety votes to six, that would keep the government open through mid-December — Republican Susan Collins and Democrat Patty Murray put it together specifically to get past the November midterms without a shutdown fight. It still needs a House vote, no date confirmed yet. Separately, the Fed governor Lisa Cook mortgage-fraud dispute with the White House is sitting exactly where it was Wednesday — her lawyers turned the allegations back on Trump and his own cabinet's mortgage paperwork, and the White House hasn't responded.

In geopolitics: Ukraine and Russia kept hitting each other's infrastructure over the weekend, and it's spreading. Ukrainian drones hit an oil refinery deep inside Russia, in the Leningrad region near St. Petersburg — well outside where this campaign usually operates. Russia says it shot down most of the drones Ukraine sent Saturday night, and Russian forces hit a district in Ukraine's Dnipropetrovsk region more than twenty times overnight. President Zelensky spent the weekend pushing for Ukraine to ramp up to a thousand long-range drone strikes a day, up from what he says is around three hundred now — that's a stated goal, not something they're actually doing yet.

And in the Gulf: beyond the transit story already covered, not much moved. That tanker strike from Wednesday is still the most recent one, still unclaimed. Roughly a quarter of the tankers being screened are still going dark for stretches of the day, so however you read the transit numbers, the underlying risk hasn't visibly eased. No fresh word on that other tanker, the Amara, that Iran may have detained near Qeshm Island back on the seventeenth — nobody's officially confirmed that one either way. And Iran's deputy foreign minister was out again this week repeating the line that no ship crosses the strait without Iran's coordination, which tells you where Tehran wants the narrative to sit even as its own trackers show more ships getting through.

Sports, quick. The Yankees split a doubleheader with the Red Sox Saturday — lost the first game six-nothing, won the second nine-two. They're seventy-seven and fifty-nine, still holding the American League's top Wild Card spot. The Knicks' Josh Hart extension is still unsigned — the numbers being floated have narrowed a bit, somewhere in the fifty-four to sixty-two million dollar range over three years. And Northwestern is off this week, opening their season next Saturday at home against South Dakota State.
