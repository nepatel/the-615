The 6:15, for Friday, November 13th. Here's what moved overnight: Nvidia's board has pushed out Jensen Huang as CEO, replacing him with chief financial officer Colette Kress, in the middle of a wild three day swing in stocks driven by a scorching hot inflation report.

Quick scan of the board before we get into it. Today's the day for the October producer price report, though it hadn't landed as of this taping; Nvidia reports earnings next Tuesday the seventeenth, the same day the Yankees have to decide on David Bednar's qualifying offer; there's no Fed meeting in the next ten days, but the next inflation report lands December tenth; crude's last confirmed settle was Monday at Brent in the low nineties and WTI in the mid to high eighties, with nothing confirmed since, which is itself notable; Treasury yields have climbed steadily all week since Tuesday's inflation print; stocks fell about four tenths of a percent Thursday, the session Nvidia's news broke; and the Hormuz diplomacy story has gotten more complicated, not less, since our last issue — more on that in a minute.

Now the lead. Nvidia confirmed Thursday that Jensen Huang has resigned as president, CEO, and director, effective immediately. That's a stunning end to a thirty three year run that made him basically the face of the entire AI buildout. The company's own filing with the SEC names CFO Colette Kress as interim CEO, and board member Harvey Jones becomes the company's first ever independent chair, while they search for a permanent replacement. Nvidia hasn't given an official reason. The reporting ties it to allegations that broke a few days earlier — that Huang's companion, Alice Huang, is assessed by U.S. intelligence as having ties to Chinese intelligence, and may have used the relationship to try to get at him. Our last issue reported that as an open board investigation. It's not anymore — it's a resignation. And the fact that Nvidia won't say why leaves open whether this was negotiated or something closer to forced.

Here's why the mechanism matters more than the personnel move itself. Huang wasn't a figurehead. He was, in a real sense, the emotional collateral behind the entire AI spending trade — the guy investors trusted to tell them hyperscaler spending would keep turning into Nvidia revenue, right as doubts about whether that spending actually pays off have been building since October. Pull that person out suddenly, with no stated cause, under a cloud of espionage allegations, and you strip away a specific kind of reassurance at exactly the moment the broader AI trade needed it least. The stock reportedly fell as much as thirteen percent at one point Thursday before paring losses, though we couldn't independently confirm where it actually closed — which tells you something about how chaotic Thursday's trading was.

The knock-on effects run through two calendars. Nvidia reports earnings this coming Tuesday — Kress's first call as CEO, and the company's first real chance to separate the question of whether the AI spending story is intact from whether Nvidia's leadership is intact, two things the market had been treating as one and the same. Separately, congressional committee chairs have asked for a briefing from Huang about Nvidia's relationship with a company called Megaspeed — that request predates the resignation and now proceeds without him in the CEO seat.

And that shock landed right in the middle of a three day whipsaw. Tuesday's inflation report ran hot — headline prices up four tenths of a percent on the month against expectations closer to three tenths, core prices up three tenths against expectations of two tenths — and it flipped the conversation from the Fed holding steady to live talk of a hike in December. The Dow fell that day even though the report was technically decelerating from September. Wednesday reversed hard: the S&P gained better than one percent, the Nasdaq over one and a half, on a rally led by CoreWeave's results, which calmed fears that AI infrastructure spending doesn't pay off — and Apple touched a record five point one six trillion dollar market cap along the way. Thursday gave almost all of that back, on the day Huang's exit became public. Put together, you've got two things converging, not resolving — a rates story moving toward higher for longer, and a leadership shock at the single most important stock in the whole AI trade. The fact that Thursday's selling was concentrated on that one day, not spread evenly through the week, tells you the market is treating Nvidia's leadership question as its own distinct risk, separate from the spending debate CoreWeave had just calmed.

Watch for Thursday's actual closing price once it's confirmed, whether Kress or Jones say anything publicly before Tuesday, today's producer price number landing on top of an inflation report that already moved the needle, and whether Tuesday's earnings call becomes the moment Nvidia tries to pull its leadership story apart from its spending story.

On politics: Kansas's governor's race is still uncalled. Holscher's lead over Masterson has reportedly widened to around seventy six hundred votes, though that's a single source. There's a statutory audit underway covering six close races including the governor's seat, and Kansas only covers the cost of an actual recount if the margin's inside half a point — which matters for whether Masterson even asks for one.

On economics, beyond the lead: that inflation report's internals ran hotter than the headline alone suggests. Shelter costs were up three tenths of a percent and made up more than thirty percent of the entire increase. Baby formula prices jumped over five percent in a single month, the biggest jump on record. No year over year figure got published this cycle because last year's shutdown broke the index's comparison base. And today's producer price number, again, simply hadn't come out as of this taping.

On business: CoreWeave's quarter was the clean beat of the week — revenue up a hundred and sixty eight percent year over year, well ahead of expectations, even with a wider loss per share than the Street wanted. They raised full year guidance and disclosed backlog north of a hundred thirty billion dollars. Cisco and Applied Materials both reported Thursday and both beat and raised their own guidance — worth noting, because it means Thursday's broad market selling was really about Nvidia specifically, not a reassessment of chipmakers generally.

On technology, beyond the lead: Nvidia's board says it still has no reason to believe trade secrets or national security were compromised — that's a position carried over from before the resignation, and we don't know if it's changed. One sourcing note: a widely shared report misidentified the new board chair as the interim CEO instead of Colette Kress — the company's own filing is clear that Kress has the CEO title. Elsewhere, OpenAI reportedly closed an eleven billion dollar first slice of a funding round valuing the company around one and a quarter trillion dollars, short of what they were targeting — the wording suggests that round isn't even fully closed yet.

On Iran and Hormuz: our framing from the last issue doesn't hold up anymore. We'd reported a single Qatar-brokered proposal that Iran had already approved. That's not quite right — Qatar actually put forward its own competing plan requiring inspections before any reopening, a tougher sequence than what Iran's actually offered, which means Doha is now a party with its own terms, not just a messenger. And President Trump said this week that Gulf allies actually prefer keeping the current blockade in place, which is a real reversal from the truce-is-close read we had two issues back. Worth remembering: a similar deal reached back in June fell apart within weeks after a tanker attack — so any optimism here should be treated carefully until it survives contact with an actual incident in the strait.
