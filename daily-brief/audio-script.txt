Here's the lead. The memorandum of understanding the US and Iran signed back on June eighteenth gave both sides sixty days to turn a ceasefire into an actual settlement over the Strait of Hormuz. That window closed yesterday with nothing signed. Reporters asked Trump point blank if he'd extend it. He said no. Then, in an interview with Fox's Trey Yingst, he said that if Oman gets in the way of reopening the strait, quote, we'll bomb the you-know-what out of them. That's a real break from how this war has gone so far — Trump's threatened Iran directly for months, but Oman has been the one party in this whole mess trying to broker exactly the outcome Washington says it wants. Naming the mediator as the obstacle, not Tehran, tells you where the frustration actually sits right now.

Iran's foreign ministry, for what it's worth, said Tehran and Oman have reached an understanding on the map of the shipping route — basically confirming the technical progress we already knew about from Oman's side. But the Revolutionary Guard flatly denied any backchannel talks with the US, called Trump's claims, quote, fantasies caused by delusions. So you've got the civilian ministry admitting some technical progress while the military wing says there's no contact at all — that's the same split we've been tracking for weeks, not a new rupture.

The shipping numbers tell the real story here. Two separate trackers agree traffic through the strait has basically collapsed — five ships moved through Saturday, zero on Sunday, against a pre-war baseline of something like a hundred thirty ships a day. And that doesn't even include this morning: a vessel got hit by an unidentified projectile just before six a-m Eastern, with a crew member hurt. That's on top of everything else — this is a single-source report so treat it with some caution, but it fits the pattern.

Oil's pricing all of this. Brent settled Monday up almost three percent, and it's holding near ninety-one this morning — within a couple bucks of the year's highs, and up something like eight bucks from three weeks ago. WTI's messier because the contract just rolled from September to October, so different data feeds are showing slightly different levels this morning — take today's exact number with a grain of salt until that settles out.

What's different today versus last week is how equities are reacting. When Trump talked about making the strait quote a territory of the United States a week ago, stocks mostly shrugged. Not today — futures are down meaningfully, and the VIX's jump yesterday was the biggest one-day move since this crisis started ramping up again. Gold actually fell, because rising Treasury yields are competing for the same safe-haven money — the ten-year's creeping toward a nineteen-month high, and nobody's sure yet whether that's inflation fear or just heavy bond supply from all the AI infrastructure spending. We'll get a clearer read tomorrow afternoon when the Fed's July meeting minutes come out.

A few things worth watching from here: does Oman respond publicly to getting threatened by its own security partner, does that transit-map understanding ever turn into an actual reopening date, and does this morning's strike turn out to be part of the same concentrated pattern we saw last week against Emirati tankers, or does it go back to hitting everybody. None of those answer themselves this morning — they're just the questions to keep an eye on.
