The 6:15, for Tuesday, September 22nd. Here's what moved overnight: oil sank, stocks hit records, and yields eased, all in the same session, on a Hormuz peace that the strait itself hasn't confirmed.

Quick sweep of the board: Brent settled around a hundred dollars, down almost four percent, and was still falling before the open; the ten-year eased to just under five percent; the Nasdaq closed at a record, up more than two percent, with the S and P and Dow both higher too; gold and the dollar were mixed and honestly a little contradictory across trackers this morning; jobless claims land Thursday, durable goods Friday; the Strait of Hormuz is on day two-oh-five of its closure, transit counts are still a mess of disagreeing trackers, but everyone agrees war-risk insurance is still running around forty times normal; and on the Yankees, Knicks, and Northwestern: the Yankees dropped their finale in Arizona and lost that series, the Knicks' preseason schedule is set, and Northwestern is idle until Saturday.

Now the lead. Monday's session moved oil, rates, and stocks all in the same direction at once, and it's worth being careful about why. Brent fell almost four percent to settle around a hundred bucks, and kept sliding overnight into the high nineties. W-T-I did much the same, down into the high eighties before the open. The ten-year eased a few basis points to just under five percent, though the thirty-year is still sitting up around five and a third — the long end hasn't gotten the same memo as the short end. And stocks didn't just drift up on cheaper oil and lower yields — the Nasdaq hit a record, up over two percent, with the S and P up about a point and a half and the Dow up a more modest three quarters of a point. That's a classic risk-off unwind, all three asset classes together, and it doesn't happen by accident.

The story behind it: U.S. Central Command said Monday that oil is, quote, moving through the strait, and that momentum is building toward normalization. That's an interested party talking its own book, not an independent count. It's layered on top of a weekend claim from Iran's foreign minister that Tehran and Oman have agreed a plan to reopen the strait — which Oman still has not confirmed.

Here's the problem: the water itself doesn't back up a move this big. The same day CENTCOM was talking up momentum, a tanker called the L.R. Stephanie got hit by an unidentified projectile entering the strait — the fourth tanker strike logged since September seventeenth. Two crew were hurt, but the ship kept moving. The tracker I checked still calls the strait's status contested, not reopened. And the transit numbers — depending which tracker you use, you're looking at roughly nine percent of normal pre-crisis traffic by one count, or in the low teens of vessels per day by another — none of that says shipping is flowing again at anywhere near the scale that would justify Monday's move on its own. War-risk insurance is still running around forty times its pre-crisis level, and six protection and indemnity clubs have pulled coverage entirely. That's a much harder signal to fake than a CENTCOM press line, and it hasn't budged in the direction Monday's oil price implies.

Now, some of Monday's stock rally probably isn't about oil at all. AMD crossed a trillion dollars in market value for the first time, on the back of Meta's new AI agent, called Muse, topping the App Store charts two weeks after launch — that's a real, separate story about chip demand for AI inference, and it's worth not conflating the chip rally with the Hormuz trade, even though they landed on the same tape the same day.

But the setup into a five-week gap before the next Fed meeting, at the end of October, is clear: markets are pricing real de-escalation in the Gulf off one CENTCOM talking point and one unconfirmed diplomatic claim, while a tanker just took a hit and insurers are still charging a war premium. If Oman denies that claim, or another strike lands somewhere that matters more, you could see oil, rates, and stocks all snap back the other way, together — a three-asset round trip that would be unusual outside an actual war.

Quickly through the rest. In politics: CNN, MS NOW, and Politico sued the Trump administration Monday over their White House ban, which took effect over the weekend; all three are still barred, there's no ruling yet, and the press pool has actually suspended its own televised coverage of the President since CNN — which held the pool camera slot — got pulled from that duty.

In economics: not much data until Thursday's jobless claims and Friday's durable goods. The last claims number, for the week ending September twelfth, was a very low one hundred ninety six thousand — still a labor market where employers are holding onto people. Worth watching whether the long end of the curve, the thirty-year around five and a third percent, confirms or contradicts the easing you're seeing at the short end.

In business: the big one is governance, not earnings — Warren Buffett has stepped down as Berkshire Hathaway's chairman, becoming chairman emeritus at ninety-six, with his son Howard taking the chairman's seat eight months after Greg Abel took over as C.E.O. Buffett's own line from the letter: Greg runs the company, Howard will guard its culture and values. Separately, H.P. shares fell more than three percent after declining to give next year's guidance, citing an expected mid-single-digit drop in industry-wide PC volumes. And a handful of Greenland-linked penny stocks spiked hard on the security-deal headline — that's a thin, headline-driven move, not a fundamentals story, so don't read too much into it.

In technology: that AMD trillion-dollar milestone, driven by Meta's Muse AI agent — the fourth U.S. chipmaker to cross that line, after Nvidia, Broadcom, and Micron. Chip stocks broadly had a strong day on the back of it.

In geopolitics: Trump announced a security deal with Denmark giving the U.S., quote, permanent control over Greenland's security, expected to be formally signed at the UN General Assembly this week — it would block American adversaries from bases or investments on the island without Washington's sign-off, but no text of the actual agreement has been published yet, just Trump's own characterization of it. Separately, Zelenskyy says he'll meet Trump at the UN this week to push for an energy ceasefire with Russia, as Russian drone strikes kept hitting Sumy and the Kyiv region; Ukraine's own count puts cumulative Russian losses since the invasion at around one and a half million, though that's Kyiv's number, not an independently verified one.
