The 6:15, for Saturday, November 7th. Here's what moved overnight: a two trillion dollar AI-driven stock rout on Thursday partly reversed on Friday, after a jobs report that blew past every forecast.

Quickly, the board. Northwestern hosts Iowa tonight, both teams six and two; markets are closed through the weekend after Friday's close left the S&P around seventy-three seventy-seven, the Nasdaq around twenty-four six, and the Dow just over fifty-one two, with Brent around ninety-eight and WTI around ninety-one; the ten-year's up near four point eight six percent; gold's in the low four-thousands; Hormuz stays closed, day two-fifty-one, with dark-tanker readings swinging sharply overnight in a way we don't fully trust yet; and the Yankees, Knicks, and Northwestern all have moves on the board tonight. Looking ahead, Monday's Veterans Day with markets open but the bond market closed, Tuesday brings October's inflation print, and Friday brings producer prices — there's no Fed meeting in this stretch, they're on hold until December ninth, and OPEC and its partners just held oil output flat for a third straight month.

Here's the lead. Wednesday's relief rally, after the Democrats swept the midterms, didn't survive its first full trading day. Thursday was the worst session for stocks since the tariff shock back in April of last year. The Nasdaq fell five and a half percent, into correction territory. The S&P dropped around four and a half percent. The Dow lost about three and a half percent, or roughly eighteen hundred points. And this wasn't about fears of AI regulation from the new Democratic Congress, which is what a lot of people were watching for. It was narrower than that: a sudden worry that AI data-center buildout itself might slow down. Tech stocks got hit hardest, the worst day for that sector since the pandemic crash in March of 2020. The VIX, the market's fear gauge, spiked to its highest level since the end of March.

The damage was concentrated at the very top of the market. The seven biggest tech stocks lost more than a trillion dollars combined in a single session. Nvidia alone lost over four hundred forty billion dollars in value, falling more than nine percent, and turned negative for the year for the first time in twenty twenty-six. It wasn't just the big names, either — on the New York Stock Exchange, decliners outnumbered advancers by more than five to one, and more than a third of S&P 500 stocks closed at least twenty percent below their highs for the year.

Then Friday cut the other way. The October jobs report smashed expectations: the economy added a hundred sixty-five thousand jobs, way more than the roughly sixty thousand economists were looking for — one of the biggest beats of the year. Unemployment actually fell, to four point one percent. And the two previous months both got revised up sharply, meaning the labor market cooled a lot less over the summer than we'd thought. Treasury yields rose on that news, with the ten-year pushing back up near four point nine percent, as traders pushed back their bets on an imminent Fed rate cut. Stocks only partly recovered — the Nasdaq bounced about one percent, the S&P a third of a percent, but the Dow actually fell again, even as Nvidia rebounded almost three percent. So the week closes well below Wednesday's highs across the board.

To put some numbers on Friday's bounce: the S&P closed at seventy-three seventy-six, up about a third of a percent; the Nasdaq closed near twenty-four five-eighty, up about one percent; the Dow closed just above fifty-one two, down almost half a percent. The fear gauge came back down to around twenty-five and a half, still elevated, well off Thursday's panic high.

Here's how I read it. These are two separate stories that happened to collide on the same tape. Thursday was a repricing of how long the AI building boom can keep spending at this pace — the same story we've been tracking in the big cloud companies' spending plans, now hitting the chip stocks directly. Friday's jobs number is genuinely good news on its own terms, but buried in that report is a detail worth knowing: the average length of unemployment is now the highest outside the pandemic years since twenty-sixteen, meaning the strong headline is sitting on top of a more uneven jobs market underneath. For Monday, watch whether this AI repricing is done or just paused, whether Nvidia holds its bounce back into positive territory for the year, and Tuesday's inflation report, which markets will now read against a jobs number this strong.

On Iran and Hormuz: the war escalated again Friday night, on its own separate track from the markets story. Trump confirmed new U.S. strikes on Qeshm Island — the same island hit back on November 2nd, just before the midterms — calling them, quote, minor strikes, and not saying much more. Iranian state media reported two missile impacts with no official damage count; Iran's air defense claims it shot down an American surveillance drone over the island, and Iran's Revolutionary Guard says two tankers hit mines and caught fire in the strait — none of that is independently confirmed, it's all coming from Iranian state media right now. The U.S. military called the situation, quote, relatively calm, a few hours later. This follows an earlier round of strikes on the same island on November second, just three days before the midterm vote, which this brief has been tracking as a pattern of the administration striking and then pulling back rather than a sustained campaign. On the diplomatic side, Iran's security deputy said Tehran wants real, practical steps from Washington, not just promises, before any new round of talks — a harder line than Trump's own claim of a lot of progress. One data point worth flagging rather than trusting outright: the tracker we use for dark, untraceable tanker traffic through the strait showed a sharp overnight swing, and we think that's more likely a data glitch than a real one-day change in actual smuggling activity.

Two earnings items worth closing out, since we'd flagged both as pending. Caterpillar's third quarter, it turns out, actually came out back on October twenty-ninth, not this week like we'd thought: revenue up seventeen percent, to twenty point seven billion dollars, with adjusted earnings per share of seven-twenty, well ahead of a year ago. And Qualcomm's fiscal fourth quarter, reported Wednesday, beat estimates too: revenue up ten percent to eleven point three billion, earnings per share of three dollars, with guidance for the current quarter coming in even higher.
