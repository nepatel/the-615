The 6:15, for Tuesday, October 20th. Here's what moved overnight: Brent crude broke above one eleven a barrel this morning, and the Strait of Hormuz war has turned into a direct fight over who actually controls the strait.

Quickly, the board. Brent is trading a little above one eleven, WTI is near one hundred to one oh eight depending on which feed you trust, two very different quotes this morning that we're flagging rather than splitting the difference on; the ten-year is around five and an eighth percent, the two-year near four and three quarters; S and P futures and Nasdaq futures are both a touch lower; the VIX is up near nineteen; gold is down around four thousand one thirty; Asia closed lower across the board, Europe's down slightly at midday; gas at the pump is actually down a bit, four twenty-eight a gallon nationally, even as crude spikes; in the strait itself, the UK's maritime agency confirmed at least a dozen ship attacks last week, the worst month for attacks since this war started back in February, and Iran has pushed its tanker blacklist past a hundred and twenty vessels; the Yankees' season is long over, the Knicks open their title defense tonight at home against Philadelphia, and Northwestern played Michigan State Saturday but we couldn't confirm the score this morning.

Here's the lead. Oil is now trading above one eleven a barrel, and the reason isn't really about the strait being a chokepoint anymore — it's that the US and Iran are now in a direct, ongoing fight over who controls it. The UK's maritime trade agency confirmed at least twelve ships were attacked in the week ending last Friday — one sank, two were disabled — making this the worst month for attacks since the war with Iran began back in February. Two of those losses now have names attached: a container ship, the GFS Pride, sank under tow after US Central Command said an Iranian projectile hit it, contradicting Iran's claim that it was a mine. And just Saturday, Iran's Revolutionary Guard claimed a supertanker carrying over two million barrels caught fire after striking a mine on what they called an unauthorized route — nobody's confirmed that one independently. What's genuinely new this week is that both sides are now publicly claiming control of the strait itself. The top US Central Command admiral said American and partner forces control it, not Iran, pointing to four hundred ships and something like a hundred million barrels moved in the past week, and said the naval blockade has cost Iran twenty-five billion dollars in oil revenue. Iran's Revolutionary Guard disputes those numbers entirely and says the strait isn't safe for anyone who doesn't coordinate with their own authority. Iran has also expanded its own blacklist of ships to a hundred and twenty-four, threatening to detain or fine anyone who crosses without their say-so. Here's the read: this is no longer just a shipping and insurance story, it's now showing up in the inflation numbers that drive Fed policy and, two weeks out, the midterms. September's consumer price index, which came out after our last issue went to press, printed hot — three point seven percent year over year, above consensus, with core at two point six percent. Energy alone was over half of that monthly gain, with gasoline up over nine percent for the month. And that print was built on crude in the low hundreds — this month's break above one ten suggests October's number, due out in mid-November, is tracking even hotter, which works against already-thin odds of a rate cut at the Fed's meeting next week. Worth flagging: pump prices have actually ticked down the last few days, a lag that may not survive another two weeks of crude this high. And not everyone thinks this holds — Goldman Sachs is still calling for Brent near ninety dollars by December, which would mean today's price is overshooting the actual disruption. It's also worth noting the maritime agency itself admits its count is probably low — most ships that get hit don't bother reporting it to them, so the real number of incidents is likely higher than the official twelve. Watch tonight's Netflix and Tesla earnings for an early read on how this inflation backdrop is hitting consumers, and watch whether Iran's conditional offer to reopen the strait within six days gets any public response from Washington.

On politics: the midterms are two weeks away, and polling — none of it fresher than early October — shows Democrats favored on the generic ballot, though the margin varies a lot by pollster, anywhere from a couple of points to nine. Trump's approval is sitting in the high thirties, underwater, in every reading we found, though again, nothing recent enough to call fresh.

On economics: that CPI print was the headline economic story of the week, and Thursday's data ran hot too — producer prices up over six percent year over year, roughly double what was expected, and retail sales came in strong as well, up eight-tenths of a percent against a much smaller forecast. Rate-cut odds for next week's Fed meeting remain thin and inconsistent depending which market you check.

On business: the big banks' third-quarter results are in, with some disagreement across sources on the exact figures. BlackRock beat estimates clearly, with assets under management now past fifteen trillion dollars and net client inflows north of two hundred billion dollars for the quarter. Morgan Stanley also beat, with revenue up better than eleven percent from a year ago. Bank of America's results are murkier — one account shows profit falling year over year, tied partly to a sizable regulatory fine from the Treasury. Tonight after the close, Netflix and Tesla both report third-quarter results; analysts are looking for Netflix revenue growth near twelve percent, and this earnings season is unusually busy, with well over two hundred companies reporting this week alone, Coca-Cola and Lockheed Martin among them.

On geopolitics: separately from the Gulf, the Russia-Ukraine energy truce that Trump announced on October eleventh appears to be holding on Ukraine's side — no new Ukrainian refinery strikes reported since then — though Russia claims Ukraine hit two refineries on the fourteenth, which Kyiv denies. OPEC-plus meets November first for its next production decision.

On Iran and the strait: diplomatically, Iran's foreign minister has repeated an offer to reopen the strait within six days if the US accepts Iran's terms — still just an offer, no public US response yet. Militarily, Iran's acting defense minister said over the weekend that Iranian missiles, not American bombs, will decide how this war ends. That comes against unconfirmed reporting that the Pentagon has mapped out a bombing campaign against Iranian infrastructure, meant to launch after the midterms — we can't independently confirm that reporting, so take it as a single, unconfirmed source for now.
