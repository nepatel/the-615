Here's the lead. Nvidia's board announced Thursday that Jensen Huang resigned as president, CEO, and a board member, effective immediately — ending a thirty-three-year run at the company he co-founded, right as the company heads into the most important earnings call of the AI boom. The company's chief financial officer, Colette Kress, steps in as interim CEO while keeping her finance job. The board brought in Harvey Jones, the former CEO of Synopsys, as Nvidia's first-ever independent board chair. Nvidia says it's looking at candidates both inside and outside the company for a permanent CEO, with no timeline given.

This comes just nine days after Nvidia's board opened an investigation into Huang's relationship with a Chinese businesswoman U.S. intelligence reportedly believes was passing information to Beijing — tied to a Singapore-based Nvidia customer accused of smuggling Nvidia chips into China. Huang admitted that affair and apologized, and at the time the board said he'd stay on as CEO through the investigation. What apparently changed the calculus was a second relationship: the New York Times reported Wednesday night that Huang also had a relationship with Kimberly Powell, Nvidia's vice president of healthcare, who reported directly to him. Nvidia, Huang, and Powell haven't confirmed that one on the record, but the Wall Street Journal says that revelation is what pushed Nvidia's normally cautious board to move fast on an ouster.

Here's why this matters beyond the gossip: Nvidia walks into Tuesday's earnings call — still the single most important print for the entire AI trade — being run by a CFO on an interim basis, with an unresolved espionage-adjacent investigation hanging over it, and a congressional briefing request still unresolved. That's a governance vacuum landing on top of the stock that the whole AI-spending narrative hinges on. And here's the twist: this is happening in the same week that Cisco, Applied Materials, and CoreWeave all beat earnings and raised their guidance — meaning investors are getting genuinely strong demand news and a genuinely messy leadership crisis at the same time, from two different directions.

The market's reaction tells you how investors are reading it. Nvidia stock fell more than four percent Thursday to around one-sixty-six, then kept falling Friday — down roughly two percent on the day and nearly thirteen percent for the week. But the broader market barely flinched — the S&P was down less than half a percent both days, and money actually rotated into Nvidia's competitors, AMD and Broadcom, both of which rallied. That tells you Wall Street is treating this as Nvidia's problem specifically, not a verdict on the AI boom overall — and Tuesday's earnings call is going to test that read directly. Watch for whether Nvidia names a permanent CEO before then, and whether Huang's congressional testimony happens at all now that he's not CEO anymore.

A few other things worth knowing. In politics, the Kansas governor's race still hasn't been called — Democrat Cindy Holscher's lead has actually grown to around seventy-six hundred votes, well outside recount range, though a state audit continues and no outlet has officially called it yet.

On the economy: October inflation came in hotter than expected Tuesday — prices up four-tenths of a percent on the month, with core inflation also running a tenth of a point above forecast. Shelter and energy did most of the work. Stocks sold off on the news Tuesday before bouncing back hard Wednesday, a rally that Thursday's Nvidia news then interrupted. We're still waiting to confirm October's producer-price report, which was due Friday.

On business, beyond Nvidia: all three of this week's big tech-adjacent earnings beat and raised guidance. CoreWeave's revenue nearly tripled year over year, though its losses widened too, and its backlog of signed contracts is now above a hundred thirty billion dollars. Applied Materials posted record revenue and record profit, even though its stock barely moved afterward. And Cisco's revenue jumped twenty-four percent, beating estimates comfortably, with the company raising its full-year outlook and now expecting nine billion dollars in AI-related revenue next year, up from a prior target of seven and a half billion. Taken together, those three reports are the strongest evidence this week that AI infrastructure spending itself is fine — whatever is going on at Nvidia is a leadership problem, not a demand problem.

In technology beyond the Nvidia story, Anthropic's IPO is getting more concrete — the company filed an updated prospectus setting an estimated price of seventy to seventy-eight dollars a share, planning to list on the Nasdaq under the ticker A-N-T, alongside a twenty-six-billion-dollar private placement from Nvidia, Google, and Microsoft. Valuation estimates floating around are all over the place, from under a trillion to one-point-four trillion dollars, and none of them are confirmed against the actual filing yet.

And on Iran and the strait: the diplomatic picture has actually gotten worse since we last reported it. Last issue we told you about a Qatari-brokered proposal — Iran reopening the strait in exchange for the U.S. lifting its blockade and releasing frozen funds. Neither government has confirmed those terms, and now Secretary of State Rubio says the U.S. isn't close to a breakthrough and calls Iran's demands outrageous, while also saying there's no need for major new combat operations right now. Iran's own security chief went further and flatly denied that talks are even happening, saying the strait stays closed until the U.S. meets all seven of Iran's conditions. On the ground, tanker traffic through Hormuz is still running at roughly a tenth of its pre-war pace, and oil-flow estimates remain around a third below normal. Oil's own price slide this month looks like it's about a global supply glut more than any reopening of the strait — so don't read falling oil prices as a sign the Hormuz standoff is actually easing.
