The 6:15, for Saturday, October 3rd. Here's what moved overnight: Fed hike odds, which this show already told you crashed earlier this week on a soft inflation print, just crashed again on a weak jobs number, down to somewhere in the high teens percent -- even as oil is holding stubbornly high on a worsening Hormuz picture.

Quick board, all in one breath: Brent's in the low hundreds and WTI's in the low nineties, both off slightly Friday; the ten year's up around five and a quarter percent, the thirty year near five and two-thirds, both near cycle highs; the dollar's flat; stock futures are solidly higher, with the VIX getting crushed down near fifteen; gold's just over four thousand one-sixty; Asia closed mixed, Europe mostly up; gas is at four thirty-eight a gallon nationally; and in the strait, the number of tankers just sitting there waiting dropped again, down to ninety vessels, a third straight daily decline, while the escalation model flipped from easing to rising.

Here's the lead. Friday morning, the jobs report came in soft -- the economy added only twenty-nine thousand jobs in September, well below the recent monthly average, with unemployment basically flat at four point two percent. That's a much weaker number than the labor market strength the Fed had been pointing to as a reason to keep raising rates later this month.

Markets didn't just shrug this off -- they piled on top of what had already happened earlier in the week. Betting markets had priced a rate hike this month at something like two-thirds likely as recently as a week ago Saturday. Wednesday's inflation data knocked that down to the mid-thirties percent. Then Friday's jobs number knocked it down again, to somewhere around seventeen or eighteen percent. Markets now think a hold is roughly five times more likely than a hike. That's three straight big moves down in one week, on two different data points -- about as fast as this market's repriced anything since the Fed actually hiked back in mid-September.

Oil did not get the memo. Brent and WTI were both roughly flat to lower Friday, but they're still sitting well above where they were before this crisis started, and the spread between them is unusually wide. Here's the disconnect: if the economic data is genuinely soft, you'd expect the dollar and long-term rates to fall too, moving in the same direction as a dovish Fed read. Instead, the dollar held firm and both the ten and thirty year yields actually ticked up Friday, sitting near their highs for the cycle. And the volatility index did something different again -- it got crushed, down more than six percent -- so equity traders aren't pricing the same risk the oil market is.

Why is oil stubborn? Because the Hormuz side of this got worse, not better, overnight. The British maritime monitoring agency logged a fourth distinct tanker strike in six days -- another vessel hit by an unidentified projectile Thursday, on the heels of three tankers getting hit Tuesday night. One report, citing a single unnamed source in Tehran, says Iran is preparing for -- quote -- a major round of fighting with the United States. Iran's own foreign minister has said on the record his country would fight even what he called an apocalyptic war rather than back off its negotiating terms. And Washington just sent a fifth aircraft carrier strike group to the region, relieving one that's been out there for months, as this conflict heads into its eighth month.

So here's where that leaves things: the Fed now has two straight soft data points pushing toward holding rates steady this month. But the oil market, and the model this show tracks for Hormuz escalation risk, are both pointing the other way. The inflation and producer price reports in two weeks will matter more than usual, because jobs and the Fed's preferred inflation gauge already did most of the talking on the economy -- the wildcard going into the Fed's late-October meeting is Hormuz, not the domestic data anymore.

One business note. Nike's quarterly numbers came out Thursday night and they're messy. Headline earnings per share blew away expectations, but that's almost entirely a one-time accounting benefit tied to recovering tariff payments -- strip that out, and revenue actually missed what analysts were expecting. The tariff exposure on footwear sourced from Vietnam that this show flagged earlier in the week is still unresolved.

One more piece of the puzzle lands tomorrow: OPEC and its partners hold their regular monthly review Sunday. Don't expect fireworks -- seven of the group's members already confirmed they're holding October production right where it was in September, so this is more a check-in than a decision point. The next real catalysts on the macro calendar are both in two weeks: the September inflation report on the fourteenth, and the wholesale prices report the day after. Those two numbers will carry more weight than usual, precisely because the jobs report and the Fed's preferred inflation gauge already moved the needle this week. Nothing new to report on the frontier AI or rocket-launch fronts in the last two days, for what it's worth -- a quiet stretch there.

On the war itself: diplomacy stalled rather than advanced this week. Trump publicly rejected Iran's offer to reopen the strait within seven days, calling it unacceptable and saying Iran only made the offer because, in his words, they're dying financially. He also claimed the U.S. has built, quote, the greatest blockade in military history. Separately, U.S. officials say renewed airstrikes are more likely after next month's midterm elections than before them. Iran's side says both countries actually agree on most of the steps needed, but are stuck on the order they happen in -- whether sanctions relief comes before or after nuclear concessions, the same sticking point as earlier this week. There's also an unconfirmed report, from a single source, that Iran offered to dilute its most highly enriched uranium and hand it to a third party under international oversight -- interesting if true, but nobody else has corroborated it yet, so treat it skeptically. And physically, that fourth tanker strike Thursday adds to what's now the busiest ten days of attacks since this war started back in late February -- even as the number of ships just parked and waiting in the strait keeps falling, which could mean things are calming down, or could just mean the tracking is getting worse. Nobody's sure yet.

Quick note on Russia and Ukraine and the Saudi-Houthi situation: nothing new and confirmed on either front since earlier this week.

And a correction: Thursday's show told you the Yankees and Red Sox were tied after two games, heading to a decisive game three. That was wrong. The Yankees actually swept the series in two games, outscoring Boston eighteen to two combined -- one of the most lopsided wild card sweeps ever. Sorry about that.

Sports, quickly: the Yankees, fresh off that sweep, open their next series today down in Tampa Bay. The Knicks have no game yet -- preseason starts Monday in Philadelphia. And Northwestern beat Penn State thirty-four to thirteen Friday night in the first game at their new stadium, moving to three and one.
