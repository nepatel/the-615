The 6:15, for Tuesday, September 1st. Here's what moved overnight: the Strait of Hormuz crisis is escalating again — the US struck Iranian rocket launchers over the weekend, Iran hit back at American bases in Jordan, and this morning two more oil tankers got hit near the strait.

Quick tour of the board before we get into it. Oil's up sharply again, in the high eighties to low nineties depending on which quote you trust, with no clean settle yet. Equity futures are lower — the S&P off about six tenths of a percent, the Nasdaq down around a full point — and the VIX jumped more than six percent this morning. The ten-year's sitting just under four and three quarters percent, gold's pulled back to around forty-four hundred an ounce, and the dollar's little changed. Odds of a September rate hike, on the CME's tracker, are now at sixty-six percent, up from fifty-seven on Friday. In the strait itself, transit traffic is still down to a sliver of normal — call it four percent — dark-tanker sightings and war-risk insurance both remain near crisis highs, and the different trackers still don't agree with each other, so take any single number with a grain of salt. On the sports side: the Yankees split their weekend, blowing out Boston sixteen to one Sunday, then getting blown out themselves by the Angels ten to one Monday — they're seventy-eight and sixty, comfortably holding a wild card spot. The Knicks still haven't signed Josh Hart to that extension everyone's expecting. And Northwestern opens their season Saturday.

Now the lead. Here's the sequence: Saturday, US forces struck two Iranian rocket launchers on an island called Larak, right in the strait, after they spotted Iran's Revolutionary Guard getting ready to fire sea mines into the waterway. The Pentagon called it a limited, precise strike on an imminent threat. Iran says three of its Guard personnel were killed. Iran hit back Sunday, launching missiles and drones at two US bases in Jordan — Jordan says it shot down eight of them, no American casualties reported. That's the first direct exchange of fire between the US and Iran in over a month. And then this morning, two more tankers got hit by unidentified projectiles off the Omani coast — no one hurt, no spill, but a clear signal that whatever the US thinks it degraded on Saturday, somebody out there can still hit commercial ships.

Here's the part that should catch your attention: the market reaction is backwards from what you'd expect. Oil did what oil's supposed to do — it jumped, on both benchmarks, though nobody's got a clean settle price yet this early. But gold, which should love a Middle East war more than almost any other asset out there, is instead still falling, extending Friday's big drop, and it's now sitting well below where it was trading at the end of last week. The read here is that everyone's positioning is much more locked onto the Fed's September meeting than onto the shooting war. Friday's hawkish speech from Fed Chair Warsh has become the single dominant force moving markets right now — dominant enough that a live military escalation in the world's most important oil chokepoint isn't lifting the safe-haven trade the way it normally would. That's a genuine divergence from how this crisis has traded since it started back in February, and it's worth watching closely to see whether it holds once the New York markets are fully open and the rest of the world gets a chance to weigh in.

The bigger question going forward: Axios reported over the weekend that the Trump administration has a Pentagon plan on the table for broader strikes on Iran, one that hadn't been approved before this weekend's exchange but could get greenlit now. Trump himself posted an AI-generated video appearing to show a strike on Iran's main oil terminal, and told reporters Iran's navy, air force, and leadership were, quote, all wiped out. This morning's tanker strikes complicate that story quite a bit — a supposedly wiped-out military just hit two ships. And any sustained rise in oil prices from here lands right on the Fed chair's desk, just over a week before the next inflation report and two weeks before the rate decision. A supply-driven oil shock is about the worst possible input for a Fed that's already arguing inflation, not jobs, is the problem.

Onto the rest of the board. In politics: Fed Governor Lisa Cook's lawyer filed a response to the White House's removal threat back on the twenty-sixth, calling the mortgage-fraud allegations baseless and pointedly noting that Trump, his Treasury Secretary, and his Attorney General have all faced similar claims about their own mortgages. As of this morning, the White House still hasn't responded, even though its own deadline has passed. That fight is still headed for the courts if Trump tries again.

On the economy: that jump in rate-hike odds — fifty-seven percent to sixty-six percent in just a few trading days — is almost entirely about Warsh's tone, not new data. The real tests are Friday's jobs report and the inflation print due in ten days. Barclays now thinks the Fed hikes twice more this year, in September and December — that would've sounded like a fringe call two weeks ago.

In business: Marvell is still absorbing Friday's six percent drop, even after a genuinely strong quarter, because investors wanted more detail on next year's guidance and didn't get it. Dell and Palo Alto Networks both report tonight, and Broadcom reports tomorrow — three more data points, back to back to back, on whether the AI spending boom is broadening out beyond just Nvidia, after both Nvidia and Marvell beat expectations last week. Wall Street's expecting a big quarter out of Dell in particular, with server and infrastructure revenue seen up something like two-thirds year over year. And Canada's retaliatory tariffs on US goods — steel, dairy, appliances, and more — kick in a week from today, the next domino in that trade fight.

In tech: Anthropic's IPO paperwork is still confidential, but the roadshow window that bankers have been pointing to just opened, so a public filing could land any day now, still targeting an October listing on the Nasdaq at what would be one of the largest offerings in years. Worth noting, separately, that the company just raised its own API pricing back to standard rates at the end of last month — a small, routine change, but a reminder that the growth numbers underwriters are pitching investors on reflect pricing that just shifted.

Geopolitically, Russia hit Kyiv overnight for a sixth straight day — the heaviest, most sustained barrage on the capital in this whole war, more than two hundred drones and missiles, at least eleven people killed. That's landing in the same seventy-two hours as the Iran story, two separate wars both heating up at once.

And in the strait itself, beyond today's news: the tanker attack from a week ago is still unclaimed by anyone, a separate detained tanker's status is still unknown, and there's been no fresh readout from the Oman-Iran talks that happened a week ago either. Here's an important nuance, though — the collapse in shipping traffic actually predates this weekend's strikes. Owners and charterers were already routing their vessels around Hormuz entirely before the shooting started back up, which tells you the deterrent effect was already doing its work. Today's events are more likely to lock that behavior in for longer than they are to have caused it in the first place.

That's The 6:15. Trains are running.
