The 6:15, for Tuesday, October 27th. Here's what moved overnight: Wall Street shrugged off two new, unconfirmed Hormuz strike reports and kept climbing Monday, erasing Friday's selloff and then some, while oil barely budged — setting up a stacked few days with the Fed deciding rates tomorrow and Microsoft, Alphabet, and Amazon all reporting earnings this week.

Quick scan of the board: the Fed's two-day meeting starts today, with the rate decision tomorrow afternoon — the funds rate's been parked in the mid-threes since June, and the odds of a cut this week look thin. Microsoft and Alphabet report tomorrow after the close, Amazon Thursday. Also Thursday: the first read on third-quarter growth. Brent's sitting around one sixteen a barrel this morning, WTI just over one oh two, both basically flat and a touch below Friday's closing prices. The ten-year's at five percent. Futures are dead flat. The fear gauge has eased back under nineteen. Gold slipped back under four thousand dollars. Gas prices actually ticked down for the first time in four days. OPEC-plus meets this weekend, and the midterms are exactly one week out. In sports: the Yankees' season has been over since early October. The Knicks are off to an 0 and 3 start, the worst season-opening skid by a defending champion since the 2011-12 Mavericks, after blowing a double-digit lead at home to Orlando Sunday. Northwestern, ranked in the twenties, is six and one after blowing out Rutgers. And the Brewers are one win from their first World Series title in over forty years after a blowout Game Three — Game Four is tonight in Tampa, and the Rays are facing elimination.

Now the lead. Monday did something Friday couldn't: it shrugged off the Strait of Hormuz. Two fresh, unconfirmed incident reports landed right in the middle of the trading day — an explosion near a town called Sirik, with claims of anti-ship missile fire, in the morning, and a report midafternoon of a tanker disabled and another vessel on fire in the Persian Gulf. Stocks kept climbing through both. The Dow added about a hundred fifty points, a quarter of a percent. The S&P jumped seven-tenths of a percent. The Nasdaq rose over eight-tenths of a percent. And here's the thing — the S&P and the Nasdaq didn't just claw back Friday's losses, they closed above where they were before Friday's selloff even started. The Dow didn't manage that — it only recovered about a fifth of what it lost Friday.

That gap matters. The S&P and the Nasdaq lean more heavily on the big tech names reporting this week, while the Dow carries more of the industrial and travel stocks that are still exposed to crude oil sitting near one sixteen a barrel. And oil itself barely reacted to the new strike claims — both benchmarks are trading slightly below Friday's closing prices this morning, not above them. If the market actually believed Monday night's reports were a real escalation, you'd expect oil to jump. It didn't. The read here: traders seem to be discounting Iran's strike claims by default at this point, after a run of assertions this month — mines on the seventeenth that an international monitoring group said had, quote, no substantiation, more mine claims on the twentieth, two more disabled tankers claimed on the twenty-fourth — that independent monitors and the US military have repeatedly failed to confirm.

That skepticism now has one more thing to weigh. Iran's top military commander said early this morning that the strait is, quote, completely closed — unconfirmed, and the kind of claim US Central Command has called false before. Meanwhile, the share of tankers going dark on tracking systems has basically leveled off rather than kept climbing — still elevated, but flat versus Friday. It's worth saying plainly: this brief has now tracked four separate Iranian mine or missile claims in the last ten days, and not one of them has been independently confirmed by a flag state, an insurer, or the monitoring groups that track this stuff for a living. That track record is doing real work in how the market is reacting, or rather not reacting.

Into all of this lands a packed calendar. The Fed meets today and tomorrow, with the rate decision at two tomorrow afternoon. Rates have been parked since a June cut, and prediction markets put the odds of another cut this week at somewhere between fifteen and twenty percent — thin. A hold looks like the most likely outcome. Here's the tension: if the Fed holds steady while oil sits near a seven-month high, that's an inflation headache showing up right as the central bank is already reluctant to ease — something the Fed chair will have to address at his press conference no matter what the committee actually decides.

And then there's Big Tech. Microsoft and Alphabet report tomorrow after the close, Amazon Thursday after the close — both landing within about a day of the Fed decision. Analyst estimates for this quarter are genuinely murky right now, with different trackers citing different numbers, so no specific figures here. But the read is simple: Monday's tech-led rally is a bet that doesn't get settled until Wednesday and Thursday nights. A disappointing report from either Microsoft or Alphabet, landing right after a cautious Fed, would hit a market that isn't pricing in much room for bad news.

On Iran and the strait, beyond the new claims already covered: the actual ship-transit count is still stuck at roughly four vessels a day against a normal baseline of eighty-five, a reading that hasn't updated in nine days now — the longest gap this brief has seen. The war-risk insurance premium, still around thirty-two times pre-crisis levels, is now three weeks old as an estimate, so that's background color, not a live number. The crisis-tracking indices barely moved. And nothing has changed diplomatically — Iran's foreign minister's offer to reopen the strait within seven days, in exchange for lifting the naval blockade and sanctions, is still sitting on the table, still unanswered by Washington in anything new. OPEC-plus meets this coming Saturday, its first meeting since this week's claims started piling up.

Sports, quickly. Yankees: season's been over since early October. Knicks dropped to zero and three, blowing a ten-point fourth-quarter lead at home to Orlando on Sunday — Orlando scored forty points in the final period alone. Northwestern improved to six and one, routing Rutgers forty-two to fourteen for a fourth straight win. And the Brewers crushed the Rays ten to one in Game Three Monday night, an eight-run fifth inning that set a World Series record, and now lead the series three games to none — Milwaukee can clinch its first title since nineteen eighty-two tonight in Tampa, with the Rays facing elimination.

That's The 6:15. Trains are running.
