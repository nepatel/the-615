The 6:15, for Thursday, October 8th. Here's what moved overnight: long-term Treasury yields just hit their highest level since 2002, and it's happening for reasons that have nothing to do with the Fed.

Quick scan of the board: the ten-year touched above five and a third percent intraday Wednesday before settling near five and a quarter, mortgage rates jumped to around seven and a half percent, stocks pulled back a touch from Tuesday's record highs, oil snapped back above a hundred dollars a barrel on fresh Houthi strikes on Saudi airports, the Hormuz situation remains at its most intense stretch of the war with a tanker hit off Oman earlier this week, the Yankees got swept out of the playoffs Wednesday night, and Northwestern and the Knicks both have games coming up that haven't been played yet.

Here's the lead. Treasury yields pushed to multi-decade highs yesterday — the ten-year climbed above five-point-three-five percent at one point, the highest since 2002, before closing out around five-point-two-eight. The thirty-year touched roughly five-point-seven. The two-year held around four-point-eight, so the curve steepened a little. And this hit real people fast: the average thirty-year mortgage rate jumped to about seven and a half percent from seven-point-three.

Here's what's interesting, though — this isn't the Fed. Odds of a rate hike later this month have been stuck at a cycle-low, something like one-in-six, since last week, and they didn't move at all with this. If this were a Fed story, you'd expect short rates to move too, and they basically haven't. What's actually happening instead is a term-premium story. The government's issuing a lot of debt, deficits are large, and the usual buyers of that debt — banks, foreign central banks — just aren't soaking it up the way they used to. At the same time, big corporations and tech companies are competing for the same pool of money with their own bond sales. So investors are demanding more compensation to tie their money up for ten or thirty years, regardless of what the Fed does next. That's a slow-moving, structural thing, not a one-day reaction to a headline — and it means short-term Fed expectations and long-term bond yields are now telling two genuinely different stories, something this market hasn't really had to deal with since before the hiking cycle started.

Stocks treated this as a cost-of-capital problem rather than a growth story. The S&P and Nasdaq had both set fresh records Tuesday, then gave a little back Wednesday — down about a fifth of a percent each, the Dow off closer to three-quarters of a percent. The more telling number is the Russell 2000, the small-cap index, which fell over one percent — smaller companies tend to carry more debt and feel higher borrowing costs faster, so that's the rate story flowing straight through into which stocks get hit hardest.

And oil did something unexpected. It had been falling for over a week even as Hormuz attacks intensified, because exports were still flowing around the blockade and the G7 released emergency reserves from storage — a genuine decoupling between the war headlines and the actual price. That decoupling broke this week. Brent's back above a hundred dollars, climbing nearly three percent in the last day alone, with the U.S. benchmark close behind around ninety-two. Why the reversal? It's not the strait this time — it's a second front entirely. Houthi missiles hit two Saudi airports Wednesday, in the capital and down south, and killed three people, continuing a campaign that's also hit Saudi oil infrastructure near Riyadh and at a Red Sea port in recent weeks. Saudi forces say they shot down one of the missiles and are bracing for another attack. Add in a Gulf storm threatening US refining capacity, and you've got two separate conflict fronts — the Hormuz blockade and now a Houthi air campaign on Saudi soil itself — both pushing oil the same direction at the same time, which is a meaningfully bigger risk than either one alone.

What to watch: September inflation data lands October 14th, and it'll tell us whether this rate move is really about bond supply or whether the Fed story reopens. And keep an eye on whether the Houthi-Saudi fight turns into an actual supply disruption, not just headlines — the risk-tracking site this brief follows just upgraded its escalation reading for the first time since we started watching it.

On the Hormuz situation itself: a tanker called the On Peace, Panama-flagged, carrying nineteen crew, was hit by a projectile off the coast of Oman on Tuesday and caught fire. How many crew were hurt depends who you ask — Omani authorities say ten were evacuated by air, Indian officials say twelve were injured, eleven of them Indian nationals — and we're reporting both rather than picking one. Nobody's claimed responsibility. This is part of what trackers are calling the most intense week of attacks since the war began back in February. Diplomatically, Iran's interior minister met with Qatar's emir in Doha earlier this week and called the talks constructive, and Qatar says messages are still moving between Washington and Tehran through its mediators — but there's still no deal. Iran wants to keep some permanent say over traffic through the strait; the US wants it to go back to being open international waters with no tolls. That gap hasn't closed, and there's no confirmed outcome yet from the New York channel we flagged earlier this week. One thing worth noting on the tracking side: the site this brief uses just upgraded its own escalation score from "elevated" to "high" and rising — the first time it's moved that index at all since we started watching it, so take it as a signal worth watching rather than a confirmed fact.

On the economics side, the thing to actually watch is September's inflation report, out October 14th — that's the print that settles whether this week's rate move is purely a bond-supply story or whether it reopens the Fed debate. In the meantime, the clearest real-world effect is in housing: that jump to a seven-and-a-half percent mortgage rate is going to show up in mortgage applications and homebuilder confidence well before it shows up in any official inflation number.

On the business side: PepsiCo reported this morning. They beat on both earnings and revenue — core profit per share of two-thirty-four against a consensus of two-thirty, revenue up five-and-a-half percent, helped by growth internationally — but they also cut their full-year profit growth guidance pretty sharply, roughly in half from where they'd guided before. The CEO said the company's moving with urgency to fix its North America business specifically, and that more cost cuts are coming there. So: a fine quarter, but a materially worse outlook, and the stock reaction will probably hinge on which of those two the market weighs more. Next week brings a wave of earnings, including the big banks — JPMorgan and Wells Fargo both report Tuesday.

Quickly on sports: the Yankees are done for the year — swept in three straight by Tampa Bay, losing the decisive game four-to-three at home Wednesday on a go-ahead homer and an overturned fan-interference call. That's a seventeenth straight year without a title. The Knicks open their home preseason slate tonight against Washington at the Garden — hasn't happened yet as I'm recording this. And Northwestern hosts Ball State Saturday for homecoming, heavily favored, also not yet played.
