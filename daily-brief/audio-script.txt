The 6:15, for Wednesday, November 18th. Here's what moved overnight: markets have flipped from pricing Fed rate cuts to pricing a hike in December instead, and that shift swallowed a genuinely strong Nvidia earnings report whole.

Quick scan of the board: stocks have now fallen three sessions in a row, with the S&P closing Tuesday at its lowest level since late July, down about one percent on the day, the Dow off closer to a percent and a half, the Nasdaq down less than one percent after Monday's much rougher two-and-a-half percent drop. The ten-year Treasury yield is sitting around five-point-one percent, held above five percent for something like twenty-seven separate days this year now. The dollar just touched its highest level in close to two years. Gold's been falling, last seen a little under thirty-nine hundred dollars an ounce. Oil's roughly flat — WTI just above eighty-six dollars a barrel, Brent in the low nineties. Gas at the pump is under four dollars a gallon nationally for the first time since July. Housing starts and the Fed's October meeting minutes both land today. On the Strait of Hormuz, tanker trackers are still showing almost all traffic running dark, with no real diplomatic movement. And a programming note on the Yankees, Knicks and Northwestern in a minute.

Now the lead. Six weeks ago, the conversation in rates markets was about how many more cuts the Fed would deliver. Now it's about whether the Fed hikes instead. Futures markets are pricing something like a nine-in-ten chance of a quarter-point increase at the Fed's December ninth meeting, and a handful of traders are even starting to price a half-point move. What changed is October's inflation report, which came in hotter than expected for a third straight month, and then one of the Fed's own governors, Christopher Waller, came out and said the committee should hike in December because inflation is proving sticky. That's flipped the entire rates complex. The ten-year yield's near five-point-one percent. The dollar's at a multi-year high. And even gold, which is supposed to do well when markets get nervous, has been falling, because higher rates make it more expensive to hold something that pays no yield.

Stocks have taken it badly — three losing sessions in a row, capped Tuesday by the S&P's worst close since July. And that's the backdrop Nvidia reported into Tuesday night. The numbers were genuinely excellent: revenue of about a hundred sixteen billion dollars, blowing past the roughly hundred-nine-billion-dollar estimate, more than double what the company did a year ago, with guidance for next quarter around a hundred thirty billion. In a normal week, that's a monster quarter and the stock rips higher. Instead it rose only around four percent after hours, and a similar amount in Wednesday's early trading — a pretty muted reaction for numbers that strong. Part of that is the macro backdrop eating the good news. Part of it is that Nvidia's new board chair used the call to flatly rule out any return for Jensen Huang, so the company still has an interim C-E-O, no permanent replacement named, and an unresolved chip-smuggling investigation hanging over it. One estimate, which we haven't independently verified, put Nvidia's slide in the two trading days heading into this report at over four hundred billion dollars in market value, and nearly thirty percent off its October peak — which gives you a sense of just how low a bar "muted reaction" was clearing. Put simply: when a hike raises the rate used to value all those future AI profits, even a great quarter buys you less than it used to. That tension, strong fundamentals against a tougher discount rate, is the story to watch into December's Fed meeting. It's also worth saying that other big AI-infrastructure names — CoreWeave, Applied Materials, Cisco — all beat estimates and raised their own guidance in recent weeks. The demand signal across the group still looks solid; it's the discount rate, not the fundamentals, doing the damage to the stocks day to day. On Wall Street itself, J-P-Morgan is reportedly warning clients a broader AI-stock correction is possible, independent of how any individual earnings report comes in — again, a single report we haven't confirmed elsewhere.

A few other things worth knowing. Kansas's governor's race is finally resolved. Democrat Cindy Holscher's win was confirmed after an audit, and the Republican candidate has conceded — a race that was the closest for governor there since nineteen seventy-four. Two other close Kansas races, for attorney general and secretary of state, are still working through that same audit process and haven't been called yet.

On the economy: October retail sales came in soft, up just two-tenths of a percent, a big slowdown from September's eight-tenths. Producer prices, meanwhile, ran hot, up six-and-a-half percent over the past year, the fastest pace in about two years. Housing starts for October land later this morning, and so do the Fed's minutes from its late-October meeting, which will get read closely now that the mood in markets has shifted so much since then. Interestingly, mortgage rates have actually been easing even as the ten-year climbs — the average thirty-year fixed ticked down to just over seven percent in the most recent weekly survey.

On technology: Jensen Huang's path to Congress is a genuine mess right now. There are three different proposed dates floating around — today, Thanksgiving, and December first — and nobody's confirmed which one actually holds. Meanwhile, Anthropic's I-P-O is reportedly pricing today in the seventy-to-seventy-eight-dollar-a-share range, with a Nasdaq debut under the ticker A-N-T possibly as soon as tomorrow, though take the exact timing with a grain of salt since none of that's officially confirmed yet. And over the weekend, SpaceX caught a Starship upper stage back at the launch tower for the first time ever, though the booster missed its own catch and was lost over the Gulf of Mexico.

On Iran: nothing's moving. Qatar wants nuclear inspections before the strait reopens; Iran wants sanctions relief and a ceasefire first, and says it won't give up an ounce of enriched material either way. President Trump said earlier this month that the U.S. doesn't need a deal with Iran at all anymore, a harder line than he was taking just a couple of weeks before that. Brent actually popped briefly above ninety-five dollars on that comment before drifting back down, and most analysts think the broader slide in oil has a lot more to do with a growing global supply surplus than with anything happening in the strait itself. Separately, there's actually a bit of movement on the Russia-Ukraine front: American, Russian and Ukrainian envoys met in Abu Dhabi last week, agreed in principle to a prisoner swap, but made no progress on an actual ceasefire. Putin told U.S. envoys in Moscow right after that any European troops in Ukraine after a ceasefire would be treated as a military target.

Quickly on sports. The Yankees' decision on closer David Bednar's twenty-three-million-dollar qualifying offer was due yesterday afternoon; as of this recording, there's no confirmed word on which way he went. The Knicks beat Toronto in overtime on Sunday, with Karl-Anthony Towns going for twenty-eight points and seventeen boards, sandwiched between losses to Cleveland and Indiana earlier in the week — we're not going to guess at their overall record, since the numbers we're seeing don't line up closely enough to trust. And Northwestern lost to a ranked Ohio State team, twenty-eight to nineteen, on Saturday, with Northwestern actually scoring as time expired; they're on the road at Minnesota next.
