The 6:15, for Wednesday, November eleventh. Here's what moved overnight: a hotter than expected October inflation report sent the ten-year yield toward five percent and clawed back part of Monday's big rally, even as a blowout earnings report from CoreWeave pushed back hard against the AI-spending panic that's been dragging on chip stocks.

Quick numbers, all rounded: the S&P 500 slipped about four-tenths of a percent to around seventy-five twenty, the Dow dropped around one-sixty points, and the Nasdaq fell about three-quarters of a percent — all giving back a slice of Monday's big gain. The ten-year Treasury jumped to just under five percent. Brent crude settled Monday near ninety-three dollars and WTI near eighty-seven, though Tuesday's actual settlement wasn't confirmed by press time. And Nvidia dropped more than five percent for a second straight day.

Here's the lead. Tuesday's inflation report did what Monday's oil-driven optimism couldn't — it gave the market a reason to doubt itself. October consumer prices rose four-tenths of a percent on the month, a notch hotter than expected, and core inflation — which strips out food and energy — came in at three-tenths of a percent for the third month running, also a notch hot. There's no year-over-year comparison this time around, because last year's government shutdown means there's no comparable data from a year ago. Shelter costs drove more than thirty percent of the increase, and gasoline, food, and medical costs all ticked up, while used cars got cheaper. None of this captures Monday's oil crash, by the way — that's too late in the month to show up here, so the real test of whether cheaper oil helps inflation is next month's report, not this one.

Bond traders read this as exactly what it was: a second straight month of sticky core inflation that puts a floor under how much the Fed can ease. The ten-year spiked intraday to just over five percent before settling just under it, up about ten basis points on the day. The two-year, which tracks Fed expectations most closely, jumped even more. And suddenly the December Fed meeting, which looked like a non-event a week ago, is back in play — one regional Fed president this week said she wants at least two more quarter-point hikes, and a Fed governor had already said a half-point hike should be on the table if core inflation kept running around three-tenths of a percent. It just did, for the third month straight. Stocks gave back some of Monday's rally in response — a measured pullback, not a panic, given how strong Monday's session had been.

Nvidia's troubles got worse, not better. The stock fell more than five percent Tuesday — one account put the intraday drop near seven percent, briefly knocking its market value under four trillion dollars — and this is now compounding, not replacing, the overhang from the company's disclosure Monday that its board is investigating CEO Jensen Huang's relationship with a woman U-S intelligence reportedly believes has ties to Chinese intelligence. Congress had given Huang until today to agree to testify at a hearing later this month, and there's no word yet on whether he's responded. But the one bright spot in an otherwise rough session was CoreWeave, which reported after Tuesday's close and crushed expectations: revenue nearly tripled from a year ago, blowing past what Wall Street was looking for, and the company raised its full-year guidance and said its contracted backlog grew to over a hundred thirty billion dollars, up from about a hundred four billion back in June. That's a real re-acceleration in demand, even though the company's losses also widened as its interest costs nearly tripled. Taken together, it's the strongest rebuttal yet to the fear that AI spending is outrunning actual demand — landing, notably, on a day when the broader macro story was working against risk assets, not for them.

What to watch: Friday's producer-price report, and especially next month's consumer-price report — the first one that will actually capture Monday's oil crash — for whether today's hot-inflation story holds up into the December Fed meeting. Whether Huang responds to Congress's deadline. Whether chip and AI-infrastructure stocks can shake off Nvidia's slide using CoreWeave's numbers instead. And whether the Qatar-brokered plan to reopen the Strait of Hormuz survives the week, given that a named Qatari official has now publicly pushed back on the reported terms, and there are unconfirmed reports of fresh tanker attacks since Sunday.

Quickly around the rest of the board. Politics: Kansas's governor's race is still uncalled, but Democrat Cindy Holscher's lead has actually widened to around seventy-six hundred votes as all counties run the mandatory hand-count audit required once a race is this close — and word is the Republican candidate may not fight the result if the audit confirms her win, a softer posture than before. Economics: Friday's producer-price report is the next big data point, and the Fed's December meeting is now genuinely live for a possible hike given this week's inflation numbers. Business: Cisco and Applied Materials both report Thursday after the close — Cisco beat estimates last quarter but still sold off the next day on high expectations, and Applied Materials is a direct read on chip-equipment spending, the same spending that spooked the market a week ago Thursday. Technology: Nvidia reports its own earnings a week from today, now a much higher-stakes date given the board investigation; and Anthropic's IPO timing is still unsettled, with estimates ranging from the week of the sixteenth to a possible slip past Thanksgiving — none of it confirmed, and we're still not repeating the conflicting revenue and profit figures floating around until one is verified against the actual filing.

On Iran and Hormuz, a bit more texture. Maritime authorities confirm another attack on a vessel last Saturday, bringing November's tally to eight ships hit in the strait and the wider Gulf — on top of October's count of at least thirty-four, the worst month of the whole war. The threat level there is still held at its highest setting. A single-sourced report claims attacks have continued since Sunday in a way that's specifically undercutting the truce talks and pushing oil back up Wednesday morning — plausible, given the pattern, but not independently confirmed. None of this shows up yet in the slower-moving tracking numbers we watch every day — actual ship transits and how many tankers are running dark haven't moved, so all of this is still playing out faster than the data can confirm it. Separately, there's a different count worth mentioning: one maritime-industry tracker says that since the naval blockade went back into effect in mid-July, there have been just over a hundred tanker transits total, with roughly six in ten of those ships running dark — but that's measuring something different, over a much longer stretch, than the day-to-day numbers we usually cite, so don't try to reconcile the two.
