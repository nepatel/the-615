The 6:15, for Monday, October 12th. Here's what moved overnight: a Houthi strike on Saudi Arabia's main airport in Riyadh killed at least twelve people and wounded roughly three hundred over the weekend — the third hit on that airport in a week — and Trump says he's weighing joining Saudi strikes on the Houthis in Yemen, even after promising just two days earlier that he won't strike Iran itself before the midterms.

Quick sweep of the board: markets are open today but the bond market's closed for Columbus Day, so Treasury yields are frozen at Friday's levels, with the ten-year sitting around five and a quarter percent; oil's actually easing this morning, Brent down near the low one-oh-threes and WTI near ninety-two, on Trump's claim that Russia and Ukraine agreed to stop hitting each other's energy grids, a claim nobody in Moscow or Kyiv has confirmed; stock futures are little changed, the Nasdaq basically flat, the S&P up a touch; gold's down slightly; gas prices ticked down to about four thirty-five a gallon nationally; and in the strait, ship traffic is still a shadow of normal — anywhere from about one in five to as little as one in eight of the normal number of transits, depending whose count you trust — while tanker strikes keep coming, including a new one overnight on a tanker called the Petro Dolphin, with two Indian crew members missing.

Here's the lead. Saudi Arabia says a Houthi strike on King Khalid International Airport in Riyadh killed at least twelve people and wounded around three hundred on Saturday — the third strike on that airport in a week, with a second hit Sunday starting a fire in one of the terminals. The dead include Saudis, Bangladeshis, and nationals of the US, Jordan, and several other countries. The Saudi-led coalition is calling it a war crime and promising to respond. And Trump — who just two days before this happened had posted that the US will not attack Iran before the November third midterms — is now saying he's considering joining Saudi strikes against the Houthis in Yemen.

Why that matters: Trump's no-strike pledge was specifically about Iran. It said nothing about Yemen, and the naval blockade on Iran stays in place either way. So there's a theater — the Houthi theater — where Washington can escalate without technically breaking the promise it just made to voters. And Saudi Arabia, having just taken the worst civilian casualties of this whole war on its own soil, with its flagship airport shut down, has every reason to push the US toward exactly that. There was also an earlier report that the White House had asked the Pentagon to develop Iran strike options before the midterms, even before the no-strike pledge came out — so the administration was already weighing its options on the Iran track, and a parallel buildup toward Houthi strikes would run on its own separate clock.

Markets, interestingly, aren't treating this as the big shock this morning — oil's actually down, because Trump is simultaneously claiming a separate Russia-Ukraine energy truce, which would ease a different source of pressure on diesel supplies. That claim isn't confirmed by either Moscow or Kyiv. But the fact that oil can fall on the morning after a mass-casualty strike on a G20 capital's airport tells you traders think this adds to an already-priced-in regional risk, not a new one — even as shipping through Hormuz is still running at somewhere between one-eighth and one-fifth of normal, and tankers keep getting hit nearly every day.

There's also a read worth stating plainly: some hardliners in Iran are reportedly calling the weeks before the election "three golden weeks" — a window to act, specifically because Washington has taken direct strikes off the table until the vote. That doesn't mean Tehran ordered the Houthi attack. But it does suggest the no-strike pledge might function less as de-escalation and more as an invitation, at least to Iran's harder factions. Watch whether Trump actually authorizes strikes on the Houthis — that would be the first direct US military action against them since this whole war began back in February — and whether Saudi Arabia acts on its own regardless of what Washington decides.

On politics: the midterm picture hasn't changed much — polling still favors Democrats taking the House, with the Iran war, immigration, and affordability as the top issues, and the president's approval still at record lows. Worth noting plainly: Trump has now tied his own restraint on Iran directly to an election date, which means any escalation between now and November third — the Houthi situation included — is going to get read politically whether that's the intent or not.

On economics: the big number this week is Wednesday's September inflation report. The Cleveland Fed's early estimate has headline inflation around three point six percent and core around two point four — both above where the Fed wants to be, and if that holds, it makes another rate cut at the end-of-October Fed meeting less likely. Remember, the Fed actually raised rates a quarter point in September, to a range of three-three-quarters to four percent, citing inflation that just won't come down — so this cuts against the idea that cuts are coming steadily. Producer prices and retail sales follow Thursday. And because today's a holiday, there's no new two-year Treasury data to check against Wall Street's rate forecasts until trading resumes tomorrow.

On business: earnings season really kicks off tomorrow. JPMorgan, Goldman Sachs, and Citigroup all report before the opening bell, with Wall Street expecting roughly six dollars a share from JPMorgan, over fifteen dollars from Goldman, and two-sixty-six from Citi. Wells Fargo's also expected tomorrow morning, and Johnson and Johnson reports too, making it the first big industrial name of the season. Worth noting: the S&P 500 is up about thirteen and a half percent this year, but if you strip out the AI-related names, the rest of the market's only up around five and a half percent — so when the banks report, what matters more than the headline beat is whether credit quality away from the AI spending story is holding up.

On geopolitics: the carrier buildup continues on its own track — the Theodore Roosevelt strike group left San Diego late last month, and the latest reporting puts full arrival in the Gulf region by late November, which would put three US carriers in the Middle East at once for the first time since April, and before that, since 2003. Separately, and a little ironically, Iran reportedly reached out to Saudi Arabia after the airport attack and offered to help broker talks between Riyadh and the Houthis — the same group Iran arms. Make of that what you will.

On Iran and the strait: there's no confirmed update on whether Iran ever sent the "definitive response" it promised on the US counterproposal for reopening Hormuz — that's a real gap, not resolved either way. And coverage is genuinely split on direction: one outlet is reporting Iran's grip on the strait is loosening, pointing to the US military's claim that it cleared mines from the main shipping lanes; another is reporting the whole effort to end the war is stalling, pointing to the unresolved diplomacy and that new tanker strike overnight. Both can be true in different ways — the lanes the Navy actively escorts might be getting safer while the attacks keep spreading to new places, including, as of this weekend, Saudi civil aviation.

Sports, quickly: the Yankees' season is over, swept out of the playoffs last week. The Knicks are off to an 0 and 2 start in the preseason after losing to Washington Thursday, and they host Minnesota tonight at the Garden. And Northwestern beat Ball State 45 to 14 Saturday at home for Homecoming, moving to 4 and 1 on the season.

That's The 6:15. Trains are running.
