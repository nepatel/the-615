Here's the lead. Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline — the one that lets Saudi crude reach export terminals on the Red Sea without ever going through the Strait of Hormuz — restarted this week after Houthi drone attacks knocked it offline for nine days. It routes oil out through the port of Yanbu. Thursday morning, the Saudi-led coalition said it intercepted six Houthi ballistic missiles aimed at exactly that port, plus the city of Taif. The Houthis later claimed they'd hit Aramco facilities at Yanbu specifically. No damage, no casualties reported, and the alerts got lifted within hours.

But almost at the same time, Reuters and Bloomberg reported that American and Iranian negotiators in New York are exploring a phased deal — Iran reopens the strait to shipping, the US lifts its naval blockade on Iranian ports — with a senior Iranian official calling it the most realistic path forward yet. So you've got two opposite signals landing in the same news cycle: an attack that tests whether the Saudi bypass route is really safe, and a diplomatic track that would, if it holds, actually de-escalate the whole standoff.

That's exactly why oil spiked to over one oh eight and then gave back half of it, settling up three and a half percent. The read here is about what got targeted, not just the price swing. Markets have been leaning on that Saudi pipeline as the release valve — proof that Gulf oil doesn't need Hormuz at all. A missile aimed right at the port that pipeline feeds into is a direct challenge to that idea, even though it didn't land. Worth noting, war-risk insurance premiums on tankers didn't spike further — they held around seven and a half to ten percent of a ship's hull value — so underwriters are treating Thursday as a near-miss, not a new regime. Meanwhile, the actual hard data on strait traffic ticked up only slightly, to thirteen crossings a day against a normal baseline of around eighty-five, and a former US negotiator put the odds of an actual deal this week at only around thirty percent. So: real but unconfirmed improvement in shipping, a credible but long-shot negotiating track, and a demonstrated willingness to test the alternative route. That argues for treating yesterday's oil relief as provisional. And it lands badly for the inflation story — crude sitting near a post-attack high right before next Wednesday's big inflation print, the one the Fed will be watching closest, with gas prices already at a seasonal record.
