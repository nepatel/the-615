The 6:15, for Saturday, September 12th. Here's what moved overnight: Friday's inflation report came in hot, the Fed is now all but certain to raise rates next week, and stocks rallied anyway — while oil, which has been driving this whole inflation scare, suddenly reversed lower. Two signals that should have pointed the same direction didn't, and that's the story this morning.

Before we get into it, quickly, the board. Gulf diplomats are reportedly meeting Monday to talk about a temporary arrangement for shipping through the Strait of Hormuz — that's a single, unconfirmed report, but it's already moving oil prices. Delaware holds the last primary of this election cycle on Tuesday. The Fed meets Tuesday and Wednesday, with markets now pricing somewhere close to nine-in-ten odds of a rate hike. Lennar reports earnings Wednesday, FedEx on Friday. Brent crude settled just above one-oh-four a barrel Friday, down almost three percent on the day but still up nearly nine percent on the week; the ten-year Treasury closed at four ninety-six, just a hair under that five percent line everyone's watching. The Dow, the S&P, and the Nasdaq each bounced around one percent and snapped a four-day losing streak. Gas at the pump is a touch above four-thirty a gallon nationally. The Hormuz trackers still disagree by about a day on how long the closure's run, and shipping through the strait remains a small fraction of normal. And in sports, the Yankees beat the Mets Friday night to move to eighty-five and sixty-two, comfortably ahead in the wild card race.

Now, the lead. Friday delivered two numbers that should have told the same story and didn't. August's inflation report matched expectations on the headline — up four-tenths of a percent for the month, three point four percent for the year, same as July. But the core number — the one that strips out food and energy — came in hotter than expected, up three-tenths of a percent against a forecast of two-tenths. That's the biggest one-month jump in core prices since April. The government's own data said gasoline prices did a lot of the work on the headline number, but the core surprise was somewhere else entirely — services, shelter, goods — not just energy passing through twice.

The market's first reaction was mechanical. Traders raced to price in a rate hike — and remember, a hike, not a cut, which is the unusual direction this whole cycle has been leaning since Thursday's oil and producer-price shock. By Friday afternoon, the odds of a hike next week were somewhere in the high eighties to low nineties percent, depending on whose numbers you use, up from an already-elevated seventy percent Friday morning. The ten-year Treasury touched just under five percent during the day before settling at four ninety-six. The two-year moved even more, according to one source, though that number's only confirmed in one place so treat it cautiously. When short-term rates move harder than long-term rates, that's usually the bond market saying: we're pricing an immediate policy response to a shock, not rewriting our whole view of where inflation and growth are headed for the next ten years.

Here's where it gets interesting. Stocks did not sell off. All three major averages had their best day of the week, snapping a four-session losing streak — the Dow up about one percent, the S&P up nearly one percent, the Nasdaq about the same. The market's fear gauge, the VIX, dropped by more than eleven percent, its sharpest one-day drop all week. That is not what you'd expect on a day when the market also just priced in higher odds of a rate hike — unless you look at the other half of Friday's data. Oil, which has been the other big driver of this inflation scare, reversed hard. Brent gave back almost three percent, West Texas Intermediate gave back a bit more than two percent, and traders are pointing to a report — so far just one source, not confirmed by any of the governments involved — that Gulf and Iranian diplomats are set to meet Monday about reopening shipping through Hormuz.

Put it together and Friday's trading day looks like a market treating two signals separately instead of reconciling them. Rate-hike odds went up because the inflation data said they should. But risk appetite came back because the thing that's actually been driving this inflation scare — oil, not underlying consumer demand — looked like it might already be turning, days before the Fed even sits down. And that sets up a real risk for next week: if the Fed hikes on the strength of Thursday's oil spike and Friday's inflation report, and then the Gulf talks actually produce some kind of de-escalation before Wednesday's decision, the Fed could end up looking like it's fighting yesterday's shock instead of today's. That's not a policy mistake exactly — more like being one meeting behind — but it's exactly the kind of thing we'll be listening for in the Fed's language next Wednesday. And again: nobody's confirmed Monday's meeting is actually happening. Right now it's a report that already moved the oil market, not a fact about what happens next week.

On to business. Oracle and Adobe both had a very different Friday than their first-hour trading suggested. Oracle had jumped as much as six or seven percent before the opening bell on excitement about its cloud backlog, touching the low one-sixties — and then gave the whole move back, closing down almost two percent at a hundred fifty and a quarter. Adobe went the other way: it had looked like it might sell off hard in the morning, dropping three or four percent, and then clawed all the way back to close up more than one percent. The lesson from Friday: don't trust the premarket move on either of these — the full trading day told a completely different story than the first hour did.

In technology, the dispute over OpenAI's claimed math breakthrough got sharper. OpenAI says it solved a famous, unsolved fluid-dynamics problem using thousands of AI agents working in parallel. A mathematician at NYU says OpenAI pressured him to remove a co-author's name — someone affiliated with Anthropic — from the announcement. OpenAI's side pushed back hard Friday, calling that accusation false. Nobody's independently verified either version, and it's not resolved.

On geopolitics: Russia launched more than a hundred and sixty drones into Ukraine overnight Thursday into Friday, and neither side gained ground, according to the usual war trackers. Ukraine and Canada signed a hundred-year partnership deal that includes drone production. And there are reports — from a single source each, not confirmed — that Putin said European troops in Ukraine would mean war with Russia, and that Indonesia is still buying Russian oil despite sanctions. Treat both of those as directional, not confirmed.

On Iran and the Gulf: separate from the oil-market story, Houthi forces on the ground seized the port of Mokha and an island called Perim, at the mouth of the Red Sea — that's confirmed by multiple outlets — putting them in control of basically the entire Yemeni coastline near that chokepoint. Saudi Arabia responded with airstrikes on the airport there. That's a different strait than Hormuz — it's the Bab el-Mandeb passage — but a Houthi-controlled coastline there is one more complication for any eventual Gulf settlement. And on that Pakistan-Saudi-Turkey defense pact we flagged last time — the only new development is an unconfirmed report that Iran's been invited to join it, sourced to one unnamed official. Nobody's confirmed that.

Sports, quickly. The Knicks still haven't signed Josh Hart to that extension everyone's expecting. And Northwestern is on a bye this week, back in action against Colorado a week from today.
