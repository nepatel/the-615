The 6:15, for Monday, August tenth. Here's what moved overnight: Iran's foreign minister said the Strait of Hormuz stays closed until Washington eases sanctions and pays reparations, even as Tehran and Oman edge toward a technical shipping deal — and that's putting the Fed's new hawkish chair right back on the hot seat two days before CPI.

Quick numbers, all rounded, all as of around six a.m. Eastern this morning: Brent's in the low eighties, up about a dollar and clawing back a piece of last week's steep slide; WTI's in the upper seventies; the ten-year's a bit above four percent; the dollar index is basically flat; S&P and Nasdaq futures are barely moved; the VIX is calm, around fifteen; gold's near forty-four hundred an ounce, up more than two percent; Asian markets closed sharply higher overnight; national average gas is right around four dollars a gallon; the Hormuz closure rolls into its hundred sixty-second day; and no trade moves overnight for the Yankees, the Knicks, or Northwestern.

Now the lead. Brent and WTI both ticked up this morning, clawing back a sliver of last week's more-than-seven-percent drop, after Iran's foreign minister, Araghchi, said Sunday that Hormuz doesn't reopen until Washington eases sanctions and pays reparations — this even as Tehran and Oman get close to a technical shipping-lane agreement. The Revolutionary Guard echoed that same line on Saturday. And prediction markets are telling you how much to believe the diplomacy: under one percent odds of a reopening by the fifteenth, four percent by the end of the month, forty-eight percent by year end. In other words, the market still thinks a reopening is more likely not to happen this year than to happen.

The physical numbers back that up. Trackers watching the strait counted something like two vessels crossing on one day earlier this month, against a pre-crisis baseline in the seventies — that's using an all-vessel AIS snapshot. A separate weekly count from Lloyd's List, looking only at the bigger tankers, found total transits ticked up week over week, but roughly a quarter of the traffic they can see is still moving through an untracked, unofficial corridor. And insuring a single supertanker to make that run now costs on the order of thirty times what it cost before the war. Three different vendors, three different methodologies — none of them agree on an exact number, but they all point the same direction: throughput is still deeply depressed, months into this.

Layer the Fed on top of that. Kevin Warsh — hawkish, Trump's pick, chair since May — is facing his first real test. July's jobs report was ugly, payrolls down twenty-three thousand against expectations of plus eighty thousand, and that cut September rate-hike odds to around forty-four percent, down from sixty-seven a week earlier. Notice what that is — hike odds falling, not cut odds rising. That's an unusual reaction to a bad jobs number, and it tells you markets are pricing in Warsh's hawkishness as much as they're pricing the data itself. Wednesday's CPI and Thursday's PPI are the next real tests of that view, and this morning's oil bounce is a live pass-through risk into those prints.

Meanwhile equities are shrugging the whole thing off — the S&P closed at a record Friday, up almost three and a half percent on the week, and Monday's futures are flat. But gold's move and strength in materials stocks are pulling away from that equity story, and that gap is worth watching into Wednesday: either the safe-haven bid fades as the Hormuz talk cools, or stocks are underpricing a scenario where the diplomacy stalls and oil stays sticky.

On politics: nothing new since late July's House vote on the war-powers resolution, two-fourteen to two-oh-eight, which the Senate companion bill failed to match. It's non-binding either way, and the president's said he'd veto it if it ever reached his desk. Warsh's Senate confirmation back in May and Powell's decision to stick around as a Fed governor with two years left on his term are still the backdrop to that Fed story in the lead — no new moves there today.

On business: Alphabet's twenty-five-billion-dollar bond sale earlier this month, with peak demand north of a hundred and fifteen billion, keeps underlining the real story of this earnings season — the company guided twenty-twenty-six capital spending to about two hundred and five billion dollars, more than double last year. Add in Amazon, Meta, and Oracle, and you've got roughly a hundred and ninety-four billion dollars in investment-grade debt issued through July, up nearly eighty percent year over year. The AI buildout is increasingly running on borrowed money, not free cash flow. And the market's getting pickier about what counts as a good quarter — Shopify jumped seventeen percent on AI-driven growth, while AMD fell seven percent despite beating and raising guidance.

On technology, mostly carried background here: AMD's data-center revenue more than doubled last quarter, Samsung and SK Hynix unveiled new memory architectures aimed at the AI memory bottleneck, and SK Hynix is putting thirty-eight billion dollars into two new plants. Nothing new there today — it's included only because it feeds directly into that debt-financed capex story in business.

On geopolitics: the Houthis claimed a strike on a Saudi Aramco refinery Saturday — that's the Red Sea theater, not the Strait itself, and it's single-sourced, unconfirmed independently as of this morning. Quick background for anyone just tuning in: this war started back in late February with U-S and Israeli strikes on Iran, Iran blockaded Hormuz in early March, and a ceasefire that held for about a month collapsed in early July. Open reporting on the toll so far — not independently verified by us — puts it at around seventeen seafarers and one port worker killed, with something like twenty thousand mariners and two thousand ships stranded at the worst point back in April. Brent peaked near a hundred twenty-six dollars that same month and is well off that even with this morning's bounce.

And on Hormuz itself: Oman and Iran have worked out shipping-lane coordinates and floated a joint coordination center, but Iran is tying an actual reopening to a separate list — ending the naval blockade, a troop withdrawal, compensation — the same list Araghchi repeated over the weekend. One read worth flagging: insurers say war-risk premiums shoot up fast on any attack headline but only come down slowly through calm stretches, so shipping costs are likely to stay elevated for a while even after any deal gets signed. Worth remembering too — the mainstream Gulf tanker count had actually recovered to somewhere north of a hundred sixty during that June-July ceasefire before collapsing back down to under thirty once it fell apart. That's the scale of what's riding on whether this technical deal actually turns into a real one.

The Knicks are in the off-season, still the reigning NBA champions after beating the Spurs in five back in June — no game today. And Northwestern's also in the off-season, opening their year September fifth against South Dakota State.
