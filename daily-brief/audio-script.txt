The 6:15, for Monday, October 5th. Here's what moved overnight: September hiring came in at just 29,000 jobs, way below the 84,000 economists expected, and that's knocked the odds of an October rate hike down to around one in six — but oil is still climbing, because the Strait of Hormuz just had its worst four days of this entire crisis.

Quickly, where everything sits before we get into it: Brent's up near one-oh-three, WTI's in the low nineties, and the gap between them has widened a lot, which tells you oil traders are pricing Gulf risk, not the Fed; the ten-year's sitting a bit above five and a quarter percent, barely budged; the dollar's a touch firmer; futures are a little soft; gold keeps grinding higher, near forty-one eighty-five; Tokyo and Hong Kong closed up, Europe's mixed and red in Paris; national average gas is around four thirty-six a gallon; and in the Gulf, transit traffic is still basically frozen at roughly one percent of normal, with war-risk insurance running something like forty times peacetime levels.

Here's the lead. Friday's jobs report was ugly across the board — just 29,000 jobs added, the unemployment rate ticked up to four point two percent, and the two prior months got revised down by a combined sixty thousand. That's the weakest print of this whole cycle, and it landed two days after an already-cool inflation reading. Traders reacted fast: the chance of a Fed rate hike later this month collapsed to somewhere around seventeen percent, down from about a third a week ago, and down from near seventy percent before that. Two Fed officials — Williams and Jefferson — both signaled there's no rush to move.

Now, normally that combination — weak jobs, crashing hike odds — would send bond yields and the dollar sharply lower. It didn't happen. Yields are flat to slightly higher, the dollar's a touch firmer, and oil is doing its own thing entirely, because the Strait of Hormuz just logged a fourth separate tanker attack in four straight days. A tanker called the Lipsi took a hit Saturday, there was a drone strike through another ship's funnel on Friday, and a third vessel lost power after being struck the day before that. So you've got two different markets reading two completely different stories off the same morning: bonds and the dollar are trading the jobs number, crude is trading the Gulf.

And here's the part worth sitting with: stock futures are actually down this morning, not up, even though the Fed just got a lot less likely to hike. Normally a jobs miss this big, with hike odds cut in half, gets cheered across every asset at once. It isn't being cheered. That tells you desks are weighing the growth-scare side of this print — weak hiring, those downward revisions, unemployment ticking higher — more heavily than the rate-relief side. That's not a market celebrating a pause; that's a market quietly starting to question the soft-landing story itself, a few weeks before CPI gets a chance to settle it one way or the other.

The stakes into the next few weeks: the Fed now has real cover to sit tight at its meeting later this month, but if Hormuz keeps escalating, energy prices could reassert themselves right as September's CPI and PPI come out in a week and a half — and that risk just got sharper, given a suspected terror plot tied to the air base the US has been flying Iran strikes out of, which I'll get to. Bank earnings kick off the following week with JPMorgan and Wells Fargo — the first real read on whether cracks are showing up in credit beneath a labor market that just posted its weakest month of this entire cycle. Lower stakes, but landing in that same window: the Yankees play an elimination-adjacent game tonight, and the Knicks open their preseason.

For anyone balancing rate-sensitive and energy positions, this isn't a clean "risk on" or "risk off" morning. A genuine dovish pivot would normally argue for leaning into duration and trimming energy exposure at the same time — but a Hormuz-driven oil bid breaks that pairing. Duration still benefits from a Fed on hold; energy keeps its own geopolitical floor underneath it that has nothing to do with the Fed at all. Two separate bets, partially offsetting, not one consensus call.

On politics: early voting opened in several more states this weekend, and the midterms are now under a month away. Two new polls both show President Trump's approval on the economy sliding hard — one put it in the mid-twenties, with most people saying his own policies, not outside forces, are driving high prices. That economic squeeze, plus the ongoing Iran war, is shaping up as the core storyline heading into November.

On economics: OPEC-plus met over the weekend and kept oil production steady into November, so no near-term supply relief there — and Gulf producers are still pumping below their own targets anyway, because of export disruptions tied to the broader US-Israeli campaign against Iran. The bigger thing I'd flag is the wage number buried in Friday's jobs report — pay growth has slowed to basically flat in real terms against inflation that was still running above three percent as of August, which makes this a messier picture for the Fed to manage than either a clean slowdown or a clean inflation scare would be on its own. Today's only other data point is the services sector survey at ten, and there wasn't a reliable forecast available for that one as of this morning.

On business: Nike's numbers were mixed — revenue missed, down four percent, but earnings per share actually beat, at forty-eight cents. The ugly spot was Greater China, down twenty-two percent, and the stock fell toward a twelve-year low even with the earnings beat. Nike's CEO acknowledged they've still got work to do in sportswear, Jordan Brand, and China specifically, and laid out a multi-year restructuring plan to cut costs.

On geopolitics: Russia hit Ukraine overnight with one of the largest barrages in a while — roughly fifty missiles and five hundred drones aimed squarely at the power grid, leaving whole regions in the dark. Zelensky said five people were killed, including four members of one family in Lviv, and called it the worst hit that region's seen since the full invasion started back in twenty twenty-two.

On Iran and Hormuz: that fourth tanker strike in four days hit a ship called the Lipsi, a Liberian-flagged tanker, taking engine-room damage — no attribution yet, as usual. Beyond the physical attacks, there's a second storyline developing in Britain — police arrested, and later released on bail, five young men in their twenties near the air base that's been housing the American bombers striking Iran for the past seven months, on suspicion of a terror plot, with investigators reportedly leaning toward Iran as the likely state actor behind it, though that's unconfirmed. One network is reporting the bombers have since been moved out of that base entirely, though that's only come from a single source so far. Meanwhile, Qatar's floated its own two-page compromise plan trying to bridge the gap between Iran wanting the naval blockade lifted and the US wanting nuclear concessions — separate from the counter-proposal Washington already sent Tehran earlier last week. By all accounts, talks remain stuck, and some officials in Washington reportedly think a return to major combat after the midterms is becoming more likely, not less. Put together, it's less one dramatic escalation than a steady grind: four strikes in four days, a bomber base scare, and two separate diplomatic tracks that aren't talking to each other.

And sports: the Yankees dropped Game 1 of the ALDS, one-nothing, in a near no-hitter against them down in Tampa Bay — they're down a game in that series with Game 2 tonight. The Knicks open their preseason tonight on the road at Philadelphia. And Northwestern christened their brand-new Ryan Field with a win, beating Penn State thirty-four to thirteen.

That's The 6:15. Trains are running.
