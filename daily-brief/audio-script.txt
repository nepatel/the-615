The 6:15, for Thursday, August 13th. Here's what moved overnight: July inflation came in cool and right on target, and Wall Street thinks the Fed now has room to sit on its hands next month — hike odds for September have slid to somewhere around forty percent, down from two-thirds just a couple weeks ago.

Quick lap around the board before we get into it. Brent's pulled back to the high eighties, WTI's around eighty-two, both down about a point and a half overnight after a couple days of gains; the ten-year's sitting at four point six eight percent, the two-year at four point one eight, the thirty-year above five and a quarter; the dollar index is flat around ninety-nine and change; S&P futures are up a couple tenths of a percent and the Nasdaq's up more like six tenths; the VIX closed Wednesday just above fifteen; gold's a bit above forty-three ninety an ounce; Tokyo closed up over a percent above sixty-eight thousand; Hong Kong was roughly flat to slightly down; Europe closed lower across the board Wednesday; gas is holding just above four dollars a gallon nationally; and over in the Gulf, the strait's been effectively closed for a hundred sixty-five days now, transit counts remain a small fraction of normal depending which tracker you believe, dark-tanker activity spiked hard overnight, and war-risk insurance is still running thirty times normal — about ten million dollars to insure a single supertanker run. No transactions to report for the Yankees, Knicks, or Northwestern.

Okay, the lead. July's Consumer Price Index came in at three point four percent year-over-year, with core inflation — that's stripping out food and energy — at two and a half percent. Both of those were a tenth of a point below June, and both landed exactly where Wall Street's consensus expected. Shelter did most of the work on the monthly number, energy actually fell for the month even though gas prices are still up nearly a quarter from a year ago. Markets read this as the Fed getting some breathing room. Odds of a September hike, according to the futures market, have eased to around forty percent — that's down from about two-thirds just two or three weeks ago, a slide that really started with that ugly jobs report at the start of the month and continued right through yesterday's inflation data. Bonds rallied a bit, stocks were up modestly Wednesday, and futures are pointing higher again this morning.

But here's the wrinkle: Fed Chair Kevin Warsh, who came out surprisingly hawkish at his first meeting, is reportedly still open to raising rates in September if the data over the next few weeks runs hot. And that's exactly the test this morning's producer price report sets up — it drops at eight-thirty, before the market even opens, and economists are looking for a modest increase. Producer prices matter more than usual right now because they're the more direct channel for oil costs to filter into everything else in the economy. A hot number reopens the hike debate; a soft one probably locks in a hold and leans on the dollar.

And speaking of oil — it's telling a genuinely different story than it was two days ago. Brent and WTI are both down around a point and a half this morning, a sharp reversal from Tuesday's rally, even though nothing's actually resolved in the Gulf — Iran restated just yesterday that the strait stays closed until its conditions are met, directly contradicting the President's claim that the U.S. has, quote, "total control." The more likely explanation isn't de-escalation at all — it's just profit-taking after a multi-day run, plus the first confirmed case of the U.S. Navy actually firing on a vessel trying to run the blockade, which reads as tougher enforcement, not a softer standoff. Bottom line for the desk: the rate story is now driving the tape more than the oil story is, which is the reverse of Tuesday — and that makes this morning's producer price number more important than it would normally be.

A few other things worth knowing. On politics: Alaska and Florida hold primaries a week from Tuesday — both have wide-open governor's races since the incumbents are term-limited — and that's the only election on the calendar in the next ten days. The House still hasn't voted on the funding bill the Senate passed before recess, so the shutdown risk is on pause, not resolved, since both chambers are out until mid-September.

On the economy: after today's producer prices and jobless claims, watch Friday for retail sales and the University of Michigan's consumer sentiment survey — sentiment's expected to slip even as hard spending data holds up, which tells you households feel worse than their wallets suggest. One more wrinkle in the jobs data: continuing claims, meaning people who've been out of work a while, actually ticked up even though new claims stayed low — so this looks like a labor market that's cooling through slower hiring, not through layoffs.

On business: CoreWeave and Cisco both beat estimates this week, but got very different reactions — CoreWeave's stock jumped after its report, while Cisco's record numbers reportedly failed to excite anyone. That split says the bar for AI infrastructure stocks is now set for perfection, not just growth. Nvidia's five-hundred-billion-dollar financing plan with six major Wall Street firms is now official, though the actual terms are still under wraps. And away from AI entirely — Wendy's jumped as much as fifteen percent and was briefly halted after reports that Nelson Peltz's Trian fund is preparing to take the chain private.

On technology: that Nvidia financing deal is really an attempt to move the risk of funding all these AI data centers off the tech companies' own balance sheets and onto Wall Street's — the open question is whether investors price that as safe infrastructure debt or as risky tech exposure. Applied Materials reports tonight, which will be a cleaner read on whether actual equipment orders are keeping pace with all this financing news.

On geopolitics: Washington and Beijing are now expected to hold a summit next month, September twenty-fourth, with tariffs and Taiwan on the agenda. And the new Saudi-Pakistan-Turkey defense pact signed last week is already drawing interest from other countries that might want to join.

And on Iran and the strait: the big development was a U.S. Navy helicopter firing missiles to disable the steering of a cargo ship that was trying to run the blockade toward Iran — the first time American forces have used an actual airborne strike for enforcement, rather than just boarding or escorting vessels away. Meanwhile the shipping data is getting messier, not clearer — the number of ships actually crossing the strait each day remains a small fraction of normal, dark-tanker activity spiked hard overnight, and the total economic toll is now estimated at one-and-a-half to two billion dollars in insurance claims since the closure began in February. Insurance costs themselves haven't moved despite all this — still thirty times normal.

Quick word on sports. The Yankees beat Seattle back-to-back — ten to five on Wednesday, four to one Tuesday — for their second straight win; the Knicks and Northwestern are both still in their off-seasons, nothing to report there.
