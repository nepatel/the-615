The 6:15, for Wednesday, November 25th. Here's what moved overnight: Monday's Nvidia-fueled rally is coming apart. Nvidia gave back more than half of Monday's huge gain in a day and a half, Asian chip stocks got hit even harder overnight, and a sitting Republican senator just broke publicly with the board's reasoning for bringing Jensen Huang back as CEO.

Quick scan of the board: stocks fell again Tuesday with chip names leading the drop even as the Dow passed fifty thousand for the first time, futures are down further this morning, the VIX has jumped into the high twenties, oil's sitting in the low nineties for Brent and low eighties for W-T-I, the ten-year's close to five percent, gas prices keep falling toward three sixty a gallon, there's been no fresh word out of the Strait of Hormuz since last week's tanker strike near Oman, and in sports the Knicks are up to nine and six after beating Minnesota.

Here's the lead. Nvidia fell almost six percent on Tuesday to right around one fifty-five, and that's on top of Monday's huge thirty-one percent surge when the board brought Jensen Huang back as CEO. And this morning, before the opening bell, it's falling again — down another five percent, to around one forty-seven. Put those two days together, and more than half of Monday's gain is already gone. The broader market followed it down Tuesday: the S&P slipped about half a percent, the Nasdaq dropped close to one and a half percent, and only the Dow managed a gain, closing above fifty thousand for the first time ever — but that's a defensive move into safer stocks, not a vote of confidence in the rally. Futures are pointing lower again this morning, by anywhere from under one percent to more than two percent depending which feed you look at, and the VIX, Wall Street's fear gauge, has jumped to almost twenty-nine, up more than nine percent on the day. That's the clearest sign yet that Monday's rally was a short squeeze, not a real re-rating.

Asia felt it worse than we did. South Korea's Kospi, which had jumped more than six percent on Monday riding the coattails of the Nvidia rally, gave almost the entire thing back overnight — down about six percent. Japan's Nikkei fell another two and a half percent, its second losing day in a row. Interestingly, India's stock market was actually up on the same morning, which tells you this is a chip-specific unwind, not a broad flight from risk everywhere. This is exactly the second-day fade we flagged yesterday as the thing to watch, and now it's run two full sessions deep.

And the reinstatement is now drawing real political heat, not just scrutiny. Senator Kevin Cramer of North Dakota, a Republican who sits on the Senate Banking Committee and who'd actually called for Huang to resign before the board reversed course, went on CNBC Tuesday and said the board — not Huang — now owns the security and reputational risk here. His line was, quote, they can no longer say it was Jensen's fault, end quote. He said Huang might need, quote, a babysitter, and he's calling on Congress to immediately pass legislation limiting AI chip sales to China. That lands the same week Commerce's export-control office confirmed it added a Singapore firm called Megaspeed, plus six affiliated companies including an Alibaba subsidiary, to its trade blacklist over fears that advanced Nvidia chips were being diverted into China. But several former officials are already saying that listing has a loophole big enough to drive a truck through — it doesn't cover the rule that would actually reach Nvidia chips made in Taiwan and routed through those same companies, so in practice it may be more symbolic than binding. Megaspeed, for its part, denies doing anything wrong.

So what to watch: today brings the biggest pile of economic data in weeks — the Fed's favorite inflation gauge, an updated read on third-quarter growth, jobless claims, durable goods orders, new home sales, and the Fed's Beige Book this afternoon, all before markets close early for Thanksgiving tomorrow and a half-day Friday. Will any of that give the market a reason to calm down, or does it pile onto the chip-stock selloff? And does Huang now walk into his three days of Congressional testimony in December with at least one sitting senator who's already said publicly he doesn't buy the board's story?

On the political side, Cramer's comments are the first time a sitting member of the committee that will question Huang in December has broken this openly with the board. There's actually already a bipartisan Senate bill, from Republican Dave McCormick and Democrats Jeanne Shaheen and Andy Kim, that would lock in the current chip-export rules and block looser licensing to China for at least two years — though Cramer isn't one of its sponsors, so his comments sound more like a push for something even tougher.

On the economy: today's data cluster — inflation, growth, jobless claims, durable goods, new home sales, and the Beige Book — is the most important morning since the Fed's October meeting minutes. The futures market is pricing in better than ninety percent odds of a quarter-point rate hike in December, and a hot inflation print this morning would lock that in even further, while a soft one is really the only thing that could change the Fed's mind. Treasury is also selling forty-four billion dollars of seven-year notes today, on top of this week's two- and five-year auctions, so there's a lot of government debt hitting the market during a short holiday week.

On business: Best Buy reports its own quarterly results this morning, which will be the next read on how willing people are to spend on electronics heading into Black Friday — that's set against gas prices that are now down for a seventh straight week, which should help holiday spending at the margins.

On technology, beyond Nvidia itself: that Megaspeed blacklisting closes the loop on something we'd flagged as unconfirmed yesterday — it's now officially confirmed, it covers Megaspeed and six related companies, and it requires a license for any of them to buy controlled chips going forward, even if critics say it has real gaps. And Huang's December testimony schedule stands as it was: closed-door House hearings December first, Nvidia's general counsel testifying December second, and Huang's first public testimony, in front of the Senate Banking Committee, on December third.

On Iran and the Strait of Hormuz: nothing new to report. Diplomacy remains where it's been — Washington still wants a full halt to enrichment and a reopened strait, no deadline set — and the most recent confirmed attack on shipping is still last week's strike on a tanker near Khasab, Oman, with the crew reported safe. We keep flagging this every day: the shipping-attack count, the barrel-flow estimates, and the war-risk insurance pricing all measure different things on different schedules, so we never add them together or net them against each other.

And sports: the Yankees' offseason continues quietly, with nothing new to report beyond last week's roster moves. The Knicks beat Minnesota one-oh-two to ninety-nine and are now nine and six. And Northwestern's football season is over at six and five, waiting to hear about a bowl game.
