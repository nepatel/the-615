Here's the lead. The escalation that defined Tuesday continued overnight instead of cooling off. Iran's military says it hit an air base in Kuwait and another in the UAE with missiles and drones overnight — the first time this round of the fighting has reportedly reached those two countries, after Tuesday's exchange had been limited to Jordan, Bahrain, and Iraqi Kurdistan. Tehran claims it hit satellite communications gear, equipment depots, and jet hangars in Kuwait, and troop positions and radar in the UAE. None of that's been confirmed by U.S. Central Command, and no damage assessment or casualty report had surfaced as of this writing — so treat it as a claim, not a fact, for now. This comes three days after the U.S. hit around a hundred targets in Iran in response to the tanker attacks Monday, and one day after Washington debuted a new "tanker for tanker" policy, striking two Iranian government tankers directly. Tuesday's issue called that a de-escalation with, quote, a built-in expiration date, not a resolution — and that's holding up. The open question now is whether Washington treats strikes on Gulf-state soil differently than it treated the tanker exchanges.

The human cost from Monday's opening attack came into sharper focus overnight too. Saudi Arabia confirmed two of the sixteen Filipino crew aboard the tanker Sidr died when it was hit approaching the strait Sunday night into Monday. The remains have been moved to Oman; the fourteen survivors are being prepared to go home. That brings the crisis-wide total to twelve seafarers killed or missing since the fighting started back in late February, by one tracker's count — though that's a single source, worth flagging.

Now, the mechanism, and this is the interesting part. Oil actually eased Thursday morning instead of extending Tuesday's spike — Brent's down around four-tenths of a percent, snapping three straight days of gains, even with WTI holding roughly flat. That's the real divergence this morning: a fresh, unconfirmed strike claim against two more Gulf countries didn't move the tape the way Tuesday's confirmed hundred-target U.S. operation did. The VIX told the same story, dropping back to fifteen after Tuesday's brief pop above sixteen. Two ways to read that, and honestly I can't fully resolve it for you: either markets have already priced in a baseline level of strikes as the new normal for this conflict and are waiting for actual disruption before repricing again, or last night's claims just haven't been verified enough yet to trade on. Treasuries barely moved either — the ten-year's basically flat at four point seven nine percent, and fed funds futures still put September hike odds around two-thirds, roughly where they sat Monday.

Here's why that matters for your Friday: tomorrow's jobs report lands directly into this ambiguity. Consensus is clustering around fifty-three to fifty-five thousand jobs added, which only has to clear the low bar set by July's negative twenty-three thousand miss. A soft number would normally argue for the Fed to look past an oil-driven inflation bump. A hot one reinforces Chair Warsh's hawkish framing from Jackson Hole at about the worst possible moment — because risk assets are also digesting Broadcom's stock dropping five percent after hours Wednesday, despite beating on both revenue and earnings, purely because next quarter's guidance came in a bit light. That's a market drawing a much harder line than it did two weeks ago, when Nvidia and Marvell got rewarded for less. One thing that did react to the overnight news: gold, which pushed back above forty-three hundred, extending its rebound — a haven bid that stocks and bonds haven't echoed. Bottom line for your commute: absent confirmation of real damage overnight, or a fresh hit on shipping itself, it's the jobs number and the Fed path that move this market today, not the Hormuz headlines.
