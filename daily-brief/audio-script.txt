The 6:15, for Monday, November 23rd. Here's what moved overnight: Wall Street's AI trade finally broke. The S&P 500 and the Dow fell into a correction on Friday, and the Nasdaq just had its worst week since the pandemic crash back in March of 2020 — and then futures came roaring back this morning. We'll get into why.

Quickly, the rest of the board: oil's sitting in the low nineties for Brent and the mid-eighties for WTI; the ten-year yield dropped to just under five percent on a flight to safety; the VIX, the market's fear gauge, spiked about a quarter higher on Friday before easing back; gold popped a couple percent this morning after actually getting sold off hard last week, right alongside stocks; gas prices keep falling, down to about three sixty-four a gallon nationally; out in the Strait of Hormuz, tanker traffic is still running way below normal and a couple more ships got hit by projectiles over the weekend; and on the calendar, watch for Best Buy earnings tomorrow, the October inflation reading on Wednesday, Thanksgiving closing the markets Thursday, OPEC-plus meeting a week from Sunday, and Jensen Huang's run of closed-door congressional interviews starting December first.

Okay, the lead. Friday was ugly. The S&P 500 dropped almost four percent on the day and was down something like eight and a half percent for the week — that's a correction, roughly twelve percent off its high from October. The Nasdaq hit even harder, down over five percent Friday, more than ten percent for the week, its worst stretch since the COVID crash. The Dow fell almost two percent. And this wasn't your normal flight-to-safety selloff — even gold got sold, down two and a half percent Friday, ten percent on the week. When the safe-haven asset falls alongside stocks, that tells you people were forced to sell everything to raise cash, not calmly rotating into safety.

So what triggered it? Nvidia. The stock was already sliding on general worries about whether all this AI spending will ever pay for itself, when Reuters dropped a report, citing sources familiar with the matter, that the SEC is looking into potential accounting issues at the company. Nobody knows yet exactly what the SEC is examining, or when the inquiry even started — Nvidia didn't respond to requests for comment, and the SEC won't say anything either. But the stock closed down fourteen percent on the day, its worst week since the two thousand eight financial crisis. And this is landing right on top of an already messy story — Nvidia's board chair had just told investors on the earnings call that ousted CEO Jensen Huang will not be coming back, even though the company's own quarterly numbers had actually beaten expectations two days earlier. So you've got a governance mess, a fresh accounting cloud, and a strong print all getting priced in at the same moment — and the market chose to focus on the first two.

It wasn't just Nvidia, either — AMD, Micron, and Broadcom all fell somewhere in that twelve to fourteen percent range Friday as the whole chip sector repriced together. Some commentators are openly drawing dot-com comparisons now, wondering out loud whether AI capital spending — a lot of it debt-financed — can actually generate the returns everyone's assumed. Anthropic's stock, which had such a strong debut just last week, gave most of that back too, falling somewhere between eight and a half and eleven percent Friday depending on whose numbers you trust, though it's still above its IPO price.

And here's the tension to watch: even with stocks getting crushed, the bond market rallied — yields fell hard — but futures still show the Fed hiking rates in December as close to a sure thing. That's because October's inflation data came in hot, and nothing since has walked that back. Treasury Secretary Bessent actually warned over the weekend that the Fed's own hawkish talk could end up hurting the economy. So you've got a market under stress, pricing in a hike anyway, three days before an inflation report that could make this whole dynamic worse or better.

One more wrinkle worth knowing: Nvidia actually went through almost this exact thing before, back in two thousand two — an SEC accounting inquiry that eventually closed with a slap on the wrist and no real penalty. That's not a prediction this one goes the same way, just useful context before you panic.

Which brings us to this morning's bounce. Futures are up nicely — S&P futures up over two percent, Nasdaq futures up somewhere between three and four percent depending on when you check. Taiwan's market, which just had its worst week since nineteen ninety-one, rallied too. South Korea's still cautious, down more than two percent. And regulators across Asia are stepping in — Korea's talking about a market stabilization fund, Taiwan tightened its short-selling rules, China eased margin lending. That's real concern about contagion, not just dip-buyers showing up. The question for today is whether this bounce survives the actual opening bell, or fades the way last week's enthusiasm did.

On to the sections. Economics: Walmart's quarterly numbers, out last Thursday, were strong — revenue up over five percent, and they raised their full-year guidance. That's the best read we've got on the consumer heading into the holidays, though it came out before Friday's chaos. Wednesday's inflation report now carries a lot of weight.

Business: beyond Nvidia's accounting story, there's also a separate Justice Department antitrust review of Nvidia's deal to buy Hugging Face — that's been going on since October, not new, just context on how much scrutiny this company is under right now. Best Buy reports tomorrow morning. And OPEC-plus meets a week from Sunday for what one analyst called the real test of this cycle — deciding their production framework for all of twenty twenty-seven.

Technology: on top of Huang's already-scheduled House interviews December first, the Senate Banking Committee chairman has now separately asked him to show up December third. That's three different congressional venues within about a week for one executive.

Geopolitics: Trump sat down separately with Putin and then with Xi in China over the weekend — a three-way format that hasn't happened before in this war. Ukraine was reportedly front and center in the Putin conversation, but there was no joint statement, and Trump wouldn't say whether he'll meet Zelensky separately. Meanwhile, Zelensky claims Ukrainian forces sank a Russian warship all the way over in Libya, plus hit a cargo ship in the Baltic — that's Ukraine's account only, Russia hasn't responded yet.

On Iran and the strait: diplomacy is still frozen, no sign of direct contact between Washington and Tehran. There were fresh attacks on tankers near Oman over the weekend, and international trackers are now counting over a hundred incidents in the strait since the war started back in February. Nobody's claimed responsibility for the latest strikes. And a quick note on the numbers you'll see in the paper version — the shipping trackers, the insurance underwriters, and the various military claims about Hormuz all measure completely different things on different timelines. We report them side by side rather than pretending they average out to one clean number, because they don't.

Sports, quickly: the Yankees are in full offseason mode — they cut two relievers and re-signed another to a multi-year deal. The Knicks won a thriller Saturday, hitting a buzzer-beater three to beat Portland, their fourth straight win. And Northwestern lost a heartbreaker up in Minnesota on a field goal as time expired, their fourth loss in a row.

That's The 6:15. Trains are running.
