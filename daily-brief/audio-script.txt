The 6:15, for Friday, October 23rd. Here's what moved overnight: President Trump came out and publicly called Iran's claim of hitting seven tankers in the Gulf fabricated, saying Tehran's faking strikes and mine hits to push oil prices up before the midterms — and markets spent this morning quietly unwinding a lot of yesterday's panic.

Quickly, the board: the Fed meets Tuesday and Wednesday with cut odds still thin, around a fifth to a quarter; GE Vernova reports Wednesday and Microsoft, Alphabet, and Amazon are all expected sometime next week though none of those dates are independently confirmed yet; the PCE inflation number and the third-quarter GDP estimate both land Thursday the 29th; OPEC-plus meets Sunday week after next; midterms are November 3rd; Brent's bouncing between roughly one-ten and one-fourteen a barrel depending which feed you trust, which is itself part of the story; the ten-year's down in the high fours after Thursday's flight to safety; S&P and Nasdaq futures are only fractionally lower this morning, nothing like yesterday; gold's still sliding, oddly, on what should be a risk-off morning; Asia actually closed mixed, not down; Hormuz is still showing just a handful of tracked transits a day against a baseline of eighty-five; dark tankers ticked up again to over half of everything screened; and on the Yankees, Knicks, and Northwestern front — Yankees are done for the year, Knicks are oh and one heading into Boston tonight, Northwestern's five and one and now ranked, hosting Rutgers Saturday.

Now the lead. Trump went on social media Thursday and flatly rejected Iran's claim that its forces hit seven tankers in the Gulf in one day — the biggest single-day tally Tehran's claimed since this whole thing started back in February. He said Iran's making it up, faking the strikes and the mine damage, specifically to drive oil prices higher right before the midterms, and he said more oil is moving through the Strait of Hormuz than ever. He didn't back that up with his own government's numbers. And Iran's Revolutionary Guard, for its part, hasn't walked back the warning it issued the same day threatening American bases in the region.

Underneath that fight is a data fight that was already a mess before Trump even weighed in. The IMF's own tracker, which only counts ships broadcasting their position, still shows basically nothing moving through the strait — about four vessels a day against a normal baseline near eighty-five. That reading is now five days old and hasn't budged. But the Wall Street Journal, citing the tanker-tracking firm Kpler, has reported that Iran's own oil loadings are actually running higher than they were back in February — which is the number the administration seems to be leaning on. Meanwhile another tracker, Vortexa, put total oil-product flow through the strait at something like two-thirds of pre-war levels as of late September, and Kpler's own broader number for the whole region's exports was down sharply over the same stretch. None of those three numbers are measuring the same thing, and none of them really supports a simple story that everything's back to normal. We're not going to pretend to reconcile them for you — that's the honest answer right now.

What markets did with all that: they calmed down, a lot. Asia didn't extend yesterday's selloff — Hong Kong and Shanghai actually closed higher, only Tokyo dipped, and barely. Europe's down a little at midday, nothing dramatic. US futures are pointing to an open that's nowhere near as rough as yesterday's. The volatility index is up, but nothing like yesterday's double-digit spike. And oil itself is telling two different stories depending which feed you check — one data source has Brent easing back toward one-ten, another has it pushing toward one-fourteen — a wider gap between the two than we've seen before, which tells you something about how unsettled the picture still is.

The bond market's interesting too. Treasury yields actually fell Thursday — a classic flight-to-safety move — even though that same day's jobless claims number came in stronger than expected, near a fifty-seven-year low, which would normally push yields the other way. So that tells you the rates move was about fear over Hormuz, not about the economic data. This morning, yields are creeping back up a bit, same partial-recovery pattern you're seeing in stocks.

Bottom line, and this is worth sitting with: if Iran's claim turns out to be basically true, this is a real wartime escalation that the trackers just haven't caught up to yet. If it's basically fabricated, like the President is now saying on the record, then yesterday's oil spike was priced on a false premise, and that's a mispricing waiting to get unwound, not a real supply signal. This morning's partial bounce across stocks, bonds, and at least one of the oil feeds looks like markets quietly leaning toward that second read without anyone saying so out loud. Either way, nobody's resolved anything — and this lands eleven days before the Fed meets with cut odds still thin, and eleven days before an election where two-thirds of the country already disapproves of how the administration's handling the economy.

On economics: initial jobless claims fell to a hundred ninety-four thousand last week, well below what economists expected and near a fifty-seven-year low. The four-week average's at its lowest since 2022. A strong labor market like that takes any case for a near-term rate cut off the table on the data alone — so the oil shock, and the fight over whether it's even real, becomes the wildcard for the Fed next week. Separately, OPEC-plus meets November 1st after holding output flat for a second straight month; Gulf producers are reportedly pumping well below their own quotas already, and the war's also delayed the review that would set next year's quotas.

On business: big tech earnings are coming up fast — Microsoft and Alphabet likely report around next Tuesday or Wednesday, Amazon around Thursday, though none of those dates are confirmed yet and Meta's date isn't even rumored solidly. The stakes are the same AI-spending question we flagged yesterday — Tesla cutting its spending guidance the same week Meta raised its own — against something like seven hundred to seven-fifty billion dollars in combined spending plans across the big four this year. Next week's reports are the next real test of whether that spending holds.

On technology: no major AI model launch, no rocket launch, no big venture round cleared our bar for confirmation again — eighth issue in a row without one. The capex story is really a business story right now, resolving next week.

On Iran and Hormuz, beyond the fabrication fight: dark tankers going dark on their tracking signals ticked up again, now over half of everything screened, continuing a slow drift upward. The crisis-pressure and escalation gauges from our aggregator are basically flat to slightly higher. Prediction markets are now pricing normal transit returning by three different dates — under one percent by the end of this month, six percent by the end of November, twelve percent by year-end — all still betting on a long crisis no matter what gets said this week. The war-risk insurance premium we've been carrying is now three weeks stale, so treat that as background, not current pricing. And Iran's informal exchange rate kept drifting weaker, up about three percent on the week — a separate pressure point from the tanker dispute. Diplomatically, nothing's moved.
