The 6:15, for Friday, August 14th. Here's what moved overnight: Iran's newly reshuffled military command claimed public control of the Strait of Hormuz for the first time, right as two separate attacks hit shipping in the strait within about a day of each other.

Quick board, all in one breath: retail sales land at eight-thirty this morning with markets still digesting a cooler CPI Wednesday and an even cooler producer-price report Thursday; Brent's sitting around eighty-seven and WTI around eighty-two, both up just a touch this morning after WTI actually fell more than two percent yesterday; the ten-year's near four point six five percent; the S and P closed at a record last night; the VIX is down near its lowest since January; gold's pulled back from a ten-week high to around forty-three fifty; and in the strait itself, it's day one-sixty-six of the closure, transit counts are still down around ninety-nine percent from normal by every tracker's math even if they don't agree on the exact number, war-risk insurance is still running about thirty times pre-crisis levels, and there's no reported roster news for the Yankees, Knicks, or Northwestern.

Here's the lead. Iran escalated its pushback against Trump's "total control" claim yesterday, and for the first time did it through the actual new chain of command instead of just the foreign ministry. The guy Khamenei's son installed to run the Basij militia after Sunday's big reshuffle came out and said, flatly, the strait is under Iran's control and management. Iran's joint military command backed him up, saying no ship moves through there without Tehran's permission, and calling the American line about normal shipping "lies." The foreign minister piled on too, saying Washington has been miscalculating for years and that this war itself was the biggest miscalculation yet. That matters because it's the first time this new command structure — installed just days ago — has actually been used to make a real-world claim, rather than just existing on paper.

And the rhetoric landed right alongside the worst stretch for ship safety in weeks. A commercial tanker took a drone hit overnight — minor damage, everyone's fine, confirmed by the UK's maritime monitor — and separately, the Emirati state oil company said two of its vessels were attacked Thursday evening, the second time that's happened to them in under a week. Abu Dhabi didn't just complain this time — they formally cited a UN Security Council resolution on freedom of navigation, which is a noticeably more formal response than they gave after an earlier drone strike on their consulate a few weeks back. So you've now got Trump saying Wednesday that the US basically owns the strait and might just keep it, and Iran answering that with actual attacks on shipping within about twenty-four hours. That's a sharper contradiction than what we've been tracking, and it's playing out through data that, for once, all points the same direction even if the trackers don't agree on the exact size of it: the IMF's shipping count, the live Hormuz dashboard, and the weekly commercial data from Lloyd's List are all showing transit volumes falling for a second straight week.

Here's the part that should catch your attention, though: oil actually fell yesterday. WTI dropped more than two percent, its first down day after five straight days of gains, even as the security picture in the strait got worse, not better. So crude's trading on the macro story right now — soft inflation data, falling odds of a Fed hike — rather than on what's actually happening on the water. Either the market's already pricing in more escalation at these levels, or it's underpricing the risk that two ADNOC attacks in a week is a pattern and not a one-off. Honestly, the data we've got this morning can't tell you which one it is, and that uncertainty is kind of the whole story for the desk today. On the Fed side, both Wednesday's CPI and Thursday's producer prices came in soft, and September rate-hike odds have come down noticeably from where they were earlier this week, though the different trackers don't fully agree on the exact number — call it somewhere in the thirty-to-forty percent range now, versus something closer to sixty percent a few days ago. The honest way to think about this morning is that you've got two markets pricing two completely different sets of information — oil's reacting to macro data, the strait's reacting to who-attacked-whom-yesterday — and they happen to be pulling in opposite directions this week. They'll only line back up once one of those two data streams stops moving faster than the other.

Quickly through the rest. On politics: the funding fight actually got messier this week — the Senate passed its own bill funding the government into December, but the House had already passed a different one that runs out earlier and doesn't match. Both chambers are out until September, so nothing gets reconciled until then, and that pushes the resolution into a pretty tight window before the fiscal year ends. Florida, Alaska, and Wyoming hold primaries Tuesday.

On the economy: July inflation came in mild both ways this week — consumer prices up just a tenth of a percent on the month, three and a half percent year over year headline, with shelter still doing most of the work; producer prices basically flat, well below what economists expected. Jobless claims ticked up slightly to around two hundred nine thousand but remain low by historical standards, and continuing claims actually fell. Retail sales data lands this morning, right around when this podcast's out, along with consumer sentiment — together they're the next real test of whether the consumer's actually slowing down under higher gas prices, or shrugging it off. Both of those numbers also matter for how much room the new Fed chair has at his first big speech, out in Jackson Hole, in a couple of weeks.

On business: CoreWeave's earnings beat Tuesday night, revenue more than doubled year over year to two point six billion dollars, and the stock jumped as much as thirteen percent after hours, though their losses are still widening on interest costs, now over six hundred million dollars for the quarter. Cisco beat and raised guidance Wednesday — record revenue, strong AI-order backlog — but the stock fell anyway, and nobody's fully agreed on why yet. And Applied Materials had a record quarter Thursday, beating on earnings, though revenue came in just a touch light versus estimates. Put those three together and the read is: two clean beats, one beat that got sold anyway, which tells you expectations in this sector are getting harder to clear, not easier.

On technology: it was a busy week for new AI models — both an American lab and a Chinese lab shipped major new releases within a day of each other, which tells you the gap between the two sides has basically closed to nothing. That's part of why chip equipment and networking demand isn't slowing down, even with all the noise around how this AI buildout gets financed.

On geopolitics: the bigger story is that the UAE is now building a documented, resolution-by-resolution case against Iran rather than just reacting incident by incident — that's a real shift, and it matters for how quickly shipping risk in the Gulf could ever normalize, separate from whatever happens in direct US-Iran talks.

And on Iran and Hormuz specifically: the insurance market hasn't budged — premiums are still thirty times normal — because underwriters need sustained calm to bring that number down, and yesterday's two attacks reset that clock instead of testing it. Dark-tanker traffic, meaning ships going dark on their tracking to move oil quietly, ticked up again too.

Sports, quick: Yankees dropped a tight one-nothing game to Seattle Thursday after winning ten-five the night before, keeping them third in the AL East. Knicks and Northwestern are both in the off-season, nothing to report.
