The 6:15, for Monday, August tenth. Here's what moved overnight: oil snapped back higher after Iran said the Strait of Hormuz stays shut until Washington meets its conditions, and that's now colliding head-on with a Federal Reserve that might still be leaning toward a hike, not a cut.

Quick lay of the land before we get into it. Brent's back up near the mid-eighties, WTI in the high seventies, both clawing back part of last week's steep drop. The ten-year's sitting around four and a quarter percent, the dollar's a touch softer, and gold just jumped more than two percent to a fresh high, which tells you somebody's hedging something. Stocks, meanwhile, are basically shrugging — futures are flattish to slightly higher after the S&P notched a record close Friday. Consumer price index lands Wednesday, producer prices Thursday, and Cisco and Applied Materials both report this week — those earnings, plus the inflation data, are the two things likely to actually move markets before Friday. And the Strait of Hormuz itself: still effectively closed, day one-sixty-two now, tanker traffic a small fraction of normal, and war-risk insurance running something like thirty times what it cost before the war.

Okay, the lead. Crude reversed hard overnight. Brent added about a percent, WTI three-quarters of a percent, clawing back a sliver of the more than seven percent both benchmarks lost last week. The trigger: Iran's foreign minister said Sunday that even though Tehran and Oman are close to a technical agreement on shipping lanes through Hormuz, the strait itself won't actually reopen until the U.S. eases sanctions and pays reparations. Iran's Revolutionary Guard backed that up Saturday, saying flatly they'll hold the strait until the U.S. accepts all their conditions. Markets had spent last week pricing a deal as basically done. That conditionality just got repriced back in.

Here's why it matters beyond the headline: the Oman-Iran track was always narrower than it sounded. It's a technical arrangement — shipping-lane coordinates, a joint coordination center — not a ceasefire, and Iran's been explicit that it's not enough on its own to lift the closure. Betting markets now put the odds of a full reopening by mid-August at under one percent, by the end of the month at four percent, and by year-end at under fifty-fifty. This is a crisis that's going to run past its five-month mark with no fixed end in sight.

The second-order effect is on the Fed. Kevin Warsh — Trump's hand-picked, openly hawkish successor to Powell — has been in the chair since late May and is now facing his first real collision between his inflation instincts and the incoming data. July's jobs report was ugly: the economy actually lost jobs, twenty-three thousand of them, against a forecast for a gain of eighty thousand. That miss knocked the odds of a rate hike at the September Fed meeting down to around forty-four percent, from about two-thirds a week earlier. But notice what kind of odds those are — hike odds, not cut odds. Even with a weak jobs number, the market still thinks there's a real chance the Fed raises rates, because Warsh was put there specifically to fight inflation, and oil's back above eighty-four dollars. Wednesday and Thursday's inflation data are the next test of that.

And stocks aren't really engaging with any of this yet. The S&P hit a record Friday, its best week since April, on the theory that a weak jobs market means the Fed eases. Monday's futures are barely moved. Gold, on the other hand, is up sharply — a much cleaner signal that at least part of the market is hedging an outcome the stock market isn't pricing at all. That gap, between what equities expect and what oil and gold and rate-hike odds expect, is the thing to watch heading into Wednesday. Worth noting, too: last week's rally wasn't just tech — materials led right alongside it, tracking that same gold move. Tech and materials leading together, at the same time, is not the pattern you usually see unless part of the market is quietly bracing for an inflation scare underneath a record-high tape.

A few other things worth flagging briefly. On politics: the House passed a war-powers resolution in late July telling Trump to end the war with Iran — two-fourteen to two-oh-eight, with four Republicans crossing over — though it's non-binding and a companion measure died in the Senate. It's the clearest sign yet of congressional discomfort with a war they never voted to authorize. And Warsh's own confirmation was a near party-line vote back in May, which matters because it tells you how much political capital is behind his hawkish stance.

On the economy: that jobs miss wasn't a soft beat, it was an outright contraction, and it's doing more to move rate expectations than anything the Fed itself has said lately. Gas prices are already over four dollars a gallon nationally, which feeds straight into Wednesday's inflation print. Retail sales and consumer sentiment data on Friday will show whether that's starting to bite.

On business: Alphabet raised twenty-five billion dollars in a bond sale last week to fund AI infrastructure, one of the biggest debt deals of the year, and it's not alone — Amazon, Meta and Oracle have collectively issued something like two hundred billion dollars in bonds this year to fund the AI buildout. That's real: it means the AI spending boom is now running partly on borrowed money, not just cash flow, which makes this week's inflation data relevant to credit markets too, not only stocks. And investors are getting picky about who actually benefits from all that spending: Shopify jumped seventeen percent on AI-driven growth, while AMD actually fell seven percent despite beating estimates, because the bar for "good enough" keeps rising. It's also a packed week for earnings generally, so Wednesday's inflation print lands right in the thick of it.

On technology: AMD's numbers were genuinely strong — revenue up fifty percent, data center sales more than doubling — but sold off anyway. Samsung and SK Hynix are both racing to fix the memory bottleneck that's constraining the whole AI buildout. And the broader trend is AI spending spilling out past the traditional chip companies — even Tesla and SpaceX are reportedly planning their own chip plant in Texas.

On the geopolitical side beyond Hormuz itself: Yemen's Houthis claimed an attack on a Saudi Aramco refinery Saturday, a reminder that this conflict's footprint keeps widening beyond the strait. And stepping back, this war started back in late February with U.S. and Israeli strikes on Iran, a ceasefire in mid-June that collapsed by early July, and it's now cost at least eighteen lives at sea by open reporting, with thousands of stranded sailors and ships still sitting in the Gulf.

And on Hormuz itself, past what we already covered: the technical talks with Oman have made real progress on shipping routes, but Iran's list of conditions for actually reopening is the harder, separate problem — ending the naval blockade, a troop withdrawal, compensation. And insurers are telling shippers that war-risk premiums won't come down until there's a long stretch with no incidents at all — meaning even after a deal, shipping costs stay elevated for a while longer.

Sports, quickly. The Yankees beat the Braves five to four Saturday, Gerrit Cole went seven strong, and they're now sixty-four and fifty-one, third in the AL East. The Knicks are in the off-season as the reigning NBA champions, their first title since nineteen seventy-three, no game today. And Northwestern football opens its season September fifth against South Dakota State.
