The 6:15, for Friday, October 16th. Here's what moved overnight: two straight hot inflation prints out of Washington this week, and the bond market basically shrugged them off, with Treasury yields falling for three sessions straight right through both reports.

Quick spin through the board: Brent's sitting around one-oh-six, WTI in the mid-nineties, both a touch softer this morning; the ten-year's down around five-ten after sliding through both inflation prints; stock futures are roughly flat to a little lower; gold's pushing back above forty-one, forty-two hundred; the dollar's softer; Asian markets closed higher overnight, Europe's mixed in midday trading; gas prices have eased slightly to around four thirty-three a gallon; shipping through the Strait of Hormuz is still running around forty percent below the pre-war baseline by one tanker-tracking firm's count, after the worst single day for ship strikes of the war last Saturday and a container ship sinking Wednesday; the Yankees' season is long over; the Knicks wrapped the preseason with an opening loss in Philly and not much else confirmed; and Northwestern is four and one, off to Michigan State tomorrow.

Here's the lead. September CPI and PPI both came in hotter than expected this week, and they're both telling the same story about why. Consumer prices rose three point seven percent year over year Wednesday, above the three point six expected, and energy alone was up five percent on the month and twenty percent on the year — gasoline did more than half the work in that headline number. Then Thursday, producer prices blew past expectations even more: final demand jumped one point three percent on the month, six point three percent year over year, a sharp acceleration from August. Stocks did not like the Wednesday number — the S and P fell about one percent, the Nasdaq nearly two, tech leading it down.

Here's the mechanism: this is the Houthi campaign against Saudi oil infrastructure we've been covering for two issues now — the strikes on Aramco's headquarters, on Riyadh's main airport — and it's pushing energy costs higher everywhere, and that's exactly what's showing up in both of these reports. What doesn't fit the textbook is what happened to bonds. Hot, accelerating inflation should push yields up. Instead, the whole curve has fallen three sessions running — the ten-year went from about five-twenty-one on Tuesday to five-nineteen on CPI day to five-ten on PPI day. Same story on the two-year and the thirty-year.

So what's going on? Read number one: the bond market is treating this as a transient, war-driven supply shock, not a demand problem the Fed has to fight — and that lines up with prediction markets, which still have the odds of an October rate cut stuck in the fifteen-to-twenty-two percent range, barely moving through either report. Nobody thinks the Fed reacts to this either way. Read number two, the simpler one: a direct missile campaign against the capital and headquarters complex of the world's largest oil exporter is also just a risk-off event, and safe-haven demand for bonds can swamp an inflation-driven selloff in the same couple of sessions. Those two reads aren't fighting each other — they both say the Fed sits tight — but they matter for how long this bond rally runs. If it's really transient, yields snap back once the energy pass-through fades out of the data in a cycle or two. If it's a real growth scare, there's more room to run, and stocks have further to fall than Wednesday's one-day drop suggests.

Worth noting, oil itself isn't confirming more escalation this morning — both benchmarks are down slightly and off this week's highs, and the gap between Brent and WTI has actually narrowed to under ten dollars, from around eleven a couple issues back — which leans toward the risk-off, transient read for now, though it's one morning of pre-settle pricing and not enough to call a trend yet. Watch whether Saudi Arabia's promised firm response widens this war in a way that breaks that story, and whether next month's CPI print still shows energy doing all the work or whether it's starting to broaden out.

On politics: midterm polling as of midweek has Democrats favored to retake the House and competitive for the Senate, though the polling environment itself is getting called out this cycle — a lot of low-quality and AI-generated polls floating around, and at least one outright fraudulent pollster exposed. Everything's on the ballot November third — all four thirty-five House seats, a third of the Senate, thirty-nine governorships — and voters say they're most focused on the Iran war, immigration, tariffs, and affordability, which puts this week's energy-driven inflation prints right in the middle of the campaign.

On business: bank earnings season is basically done, and it's a split verdict. Goldman Sachs crushed it — seventeen dollars a share, way above estimates, equities trading up seventy percent. Citigroup and BlackRock both beat too, BlackRock with assets near fifteen and a half trillion dollars. Morgan Stanley beat on the headline number as well. On the other side, Bank of America missed — profit down to six point nine billion from eight point three a year ago, hit by a one point two billion dollar anti-money-laundering charge — and JPMorgan, reported the week before, also came up short of forecast on weak trading revenue. Ahead: Netflix reports around October twentieth, guiding to eighty-two cents a share against a consensus of eighty-four.

On geopolitics: the Houthi campaign against Saudi Arabia is still escalating. New reporting this week says the strike on Aramco's Dhahran headquarters hit an office tower directly, damaging its top three floors, even as the Saudi government's public line calls it just a limited fire with no casualties. Riyadh's main airport stays closed to commercial traffic through at least tomorrow. The Houthis are now threatening a refinery north of Jeddah as well. Meanwhile, on the broader US-Iran front, there's been no new American or Israeli strike on Iranian territory in the past two days, consistent with the president's pledge to hold off until after the midterms — even as this connected proxy war with the Houthis gets hotter, not cooler.

On Iran and Hormuz specifically: the best new data point this week comes from ship-tracking firm Kpler, which puts crude exports through the strait at around eight and a half million barrels a day for the week — about forty percent below the normal pre-war level, a real, physical supply loss, not just a pricing story. That's roughly five and a half to six million barrels a day of oil simply not moving through the strait compared with a normal flow near fourteen million. Separately, last Saturday saw six vessels struck in a single day, the worst day of the war for shipping so far, and a container ship sank Wednesday after being hit by something unidentified — Iran says it was a mine, an independent maritime security firm says there's no evidence for that, so the cause stays unresolved. Count the vessel strikes and the barrels missing as two different problems, not one — insurance and diversion risk on top of a genuine supply shortfall, not instead of it.
