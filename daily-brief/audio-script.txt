The 6:15, for Thursday, October 15th. Here's what moved overnight: a hotter than expected inflation print has cracked the AI trade, and South Korea just had its worst trading day of this entire cycle.

Quickly, the board: Treasury yields and the dollar are little changed, but stocks are a different story — Asian markets got hammered overnight, with South Korea's market down around eight percent and Japan's Nikkei down close to five, US futures are pointing lower again after Wall Street already fell more than one percent yesterday, gold is up sharply as money runs to safety, oil is actually down a couple percent despite the Middle East getting more dangerous, not less, retail gas is basically flat around four thirty-three a gallon, the Strait of Hormuz saw its lowest tanker traffic of this whole crisis, there's a confusing tangle of disputed tanker strikes nobody's fully sorted out yet, the Yankees' season is long over, the Knicks play their preseason finale tonight before opening the real season October twentieth against Philadelphia with a championship banner ceremony, and Northwestern is on a bye before hosting Rutgers on the twenty-fourth.

Here's the lead. Yesterday morning, the government put out September's inflation numbers, and they came in hot. Prices were up three point seven percent from a year ago — hotter than the roughly three point six percent economists were expecting — and the core number, which strips out food and energy, accelerated to two point six percent, up from two point four the month before. On a monthly basis prices jumped six tenths of a percent, double the prior month's pace. The headline move was mostly about fuel costs.

There was one odd little detail buried in the report, by the way: candy and chewing gum prices are up almost eight percent from a year ago, apparently because cocoa costs have spiked. Small thing, but it tells you this wasn't a clean, single-category print — price pressure showed up in some unexpected corners.

Now, why does a hot inflation number crack open an AI stock selloff? Here's the mechanism. A hotter inflation path means less room for the Fed to keep cutting rates, and that matters enormously for a trade that's been built entirely on cheap money: the artificial intelligence infrastructure buildout. Every data center, every chip order, every one of these giant capital commitments has been priced assuming borrowing stays cheap and demand never slows down. Raise the implied cost of capital even a little, and that whole structure gets more expensive to justify. The analyst community's read on this, at least from one strategist quoted overnight, is that investors aren't actually questioning whether the AI demand is real — they're questioning what it should cost to finance it. That's a meaningfully different, and in some ways scarier, kind of repricing than a demand panic would be.

Asia felt it first, and felt it hardest. South Korea's Kospi index fell around eight percent in a single session — its sharpest drop of this entire cycle — dragged down by Samsung, which fell almost ten percent, and by SK Hynix and a company called Kioxia, both down more than eleven percent. All three make the memory chips that go straight into AI servers. Japan's Nikkei closed down almost five percent. And this wasn't just an Asia story — Wall Street had already fallen hard Wednesday, with the S&P 500, which had just hit a record high the week before, dropping about one percent, and the tech-heavy Nasdaq falling closer to two percent. This morning, futures are pointing lower again, there's no bounce yet, volatility is up sharply, and gold is catching a real safe-haven bid.

Here's the part that should really catch your attention, though: oil is not playing along. Brent crude is down over two percent this morning, even as the situation in the Strait of Hormuz got messier and more dangerous overnight — ship traffic through the strait fell to its lowest level of this entire crisis, and there's a tangle of disputed attacks on tankers that nobody's been able to fully sort out yet. Normally that kind of Middle East escalation pushes oil up. It isn't this morning. Either the market's already priced in about as much Hormuz risk as it's going to without an actual confirmed hit to Saudi oil production, or the stock-market panic is simply the bigger story right now and is dragging oil down with everything else. Either way, if you've been trading on the assumption that Hormuz risk means oil goes up, that relationship broke this morning, and it's worth watching closely. Today's producer price, retail sales, and jobless claims numbers at eight thirty will tell us whether yesterday's inflation surprise was broad-based or just a fuel-price blip.

A few other things worth knowing. On the earnings side, the big bank reporting season is basically wrapped up: Goldman Sachs and Citigroup both beat expectations clearly, Morgan Stanley beat too, with revenue up meaningfully from a year ago, and BlackRock beat on its preferred earnings measure with assets under management now above fifteen trillion dollars. The one disappointment was Bank of America, which missed estimates after taking a one-point-two-billion dollar regulatory charge that alone cost them seventeen cents a share. Add it up and this earnings season has mostly been a story of Wall Street's trading and asset-management arms doing just fine, even as the broader market wobbles underneath them.

On the Saudi-Houthi situation — the airport strikes and Aramco threats we've been tracking all week — things have actually gone quiet in terms of fresh, verifiable news; nothing new confirmed in the last day or so, which is a gap worth watching rather than a sign it's resolved. Saudi Aramco hasn't said anything public about production impact from this month's strikes at all, and the company's last comment on the record — that attacks had, quote, no material impact — is from back in August, well before this escalation, so don't treat that as current.

And on Hormuz itself, the tracking service we rely on for those numbers changed its methodology overnight, dropping some of the specific figures we've been quoting all week in favor of vaguer categories — so take tonight's numbers with that caveat. What we can say concretely: actual ship traffic through the strait is now down to just two vessels on one recent count, versus a normal pre-crisis pace of around eighty-five a day. That's the real, measurable story — far less shipping, not more, even while oil refuses to price in extra risk for it this morning.

Politically, nothing new: the president's pledge not to resume strikes on Iran before the November third midterms still stands, and there's no government shutdown risk before funding runs out in December. OPEC and its partners already decided to hold oil output steady into November, so no fresh decision there until their November first meeting.

Quickly on sports: the Yankees' season is over, swept out of the playoffs by Tampa Bay. The Knicks play their preseason finale tonight against Toronto, and they open the real season October twentieth at home against Philadelphia with a championship banner ceremony before tipoff. Northwestern is on a bye this week at four and one, hosting Rutgers on the twenty-fourth.
