The 6:15, for Friday, November 6th. Here's what moved overnight: Wall Street just had its worst day since April of last year, and Asia hasn't stopped falling.

Quick board, all in one breath: jobs report lands at eight-thirty this morning with forecasters split somewhere around sixty to sixty-five thousand jobs added and unemployment expected to hold around four point two percent; CPI follows next Tuesday, PPI the Friday after; Northwestern hosts a ranked Iowa team tomorrow; the government's funded through December eleventh, so no shutdown fight this month; the Fed's next move is December ninth, holding in the mid-threes for now; Brent's sitting in the high nineties and WTI near ninety, both down roughly five percent since Tuesday; the ten-year's around four point eight four percent, a touch lower on the week; the dollar's actually firmer despite everything going on; gold's down too, which is the tell I'll come back to; volatility spiked hard Thursday and is easing this morning but still elevated; Nikkei and Kospi both fell about six percent overnight; and the Strait of Hormuz picture is basically unchanged from earlier this week — still closed, still running on stale and foggy data.

Here's the lead. Thursday was the reckoning this show's been watching build all year. The S&P 500 fell about four and a third percent, the Nasdaq fell five and a half and closed in a technical correction — more than ten percent off its October record — and the Dow lost over eighteen hundred points, roughly three and a half percent. All three had their worst single day since April of twenty twenty-five. Across US stocks, something like two and a half trillion dollars in value just evaporated in one session, and the Magnificent Seven alone lost over a trillion of that. Every single sector in the S&P closed lower, but the hardest-hit names were the AI infrastructure trade specifically: Nvidia fell around nine percent, wiping out something like four hundred fifty billion dollars of its own value and pushing the stock negative for the year after carrying a huge share of the market's gains through October; AMD and Micron both fell somewhere in the fifteen-to-seventeen percent range, depending which report you believe — this brief won't pretend to have the exact number when the sources don't agree; CoreWeave, Tesla, and GE Vernova, the power company that's been riding the data-center build-out, all took double-digit hits too. The volatility index spiked hard on the day and is only partially unwound this morning.

Why? The dominant explanation in the coverage is that investors are using this week's Democratic sweep of Congress — and Democratic candidates in several states explicitly campaigning against new data centers, Texas's governor nominee calling for an outright moratorium — to reprice how fast the AI buildout can actually keep scaling. That's landing on top of a valuation question that was already simmering: are these companies spending more building AI than AI is actually earning them back? One strategist's framing stuck with me: so much of this year's gains have been concentrated in a handful of mega-cap names, that when those names move hard, they can drag the whole index down almost by themselves, regardless of what everybody else is doing. Trump blamed Democrats for the selloff on social media — worth noting, but the reporting is pretty clear the actual trigger was AI-capex fear, not some broader economic shift.

Here's the part that matters for how you think about this: a genuine flight-to-safety selloff usually sends money into the dollar, into bonds, and into gold, all at once. We only got two out of three. The dollar actually strengthened, Treasuries caught a modest bid, but gold fell right alongside stocks. That combination reads less like classic safe-haven hedging and more like straight-up de-risking — people raising cash and unwinding leverage rather than rotating into traditional shelters. Oil fell in sympathy too, on growth-scare demand fears, not because anything new happened in the Strait of Hormuz.

And Asia shows this isn't over. Japan's Nikkei fell almost six percent, Korea's Kospi fell about the same — both markets loaded with the same chip supply chain that got hit on Wall Street. Hong Kong and Shanghai were much more insulated, down only around one percent each, which makes sense given lighter AI exposure in those indexes. US futures are trying to bounce back this morning — S&P futures up a quarter percent, Nasdaq futures up more than one percent — but that's a fragile, untested move, and it's about to run straight into the jobs report at eight-thirty, after this brief goes to print. A weak number would normally read as more room for the Fed to cut in December. This week, it lands on a market that's already asking whether the sector carrying most of this year's gains can keep growing into its own valuation, so don't assume the two reactions cancel out cleanly.

On politics beyond that: Hakeem Jeffries is on track to become the first Black Speaker of the House when the new Congress convenes in January, though that's still a formality that hasn't technically happened yet. Kansas and Ohio's governor races are still unconfirmed in this brief's research, both flagged as newly competitive late in the campaign. And the federal government's funding is locked in through December eleventh under the deal signed back in September, so no shutdown risk between now and then.

On economics: today's the big one — October jobs, eight-thirty eastern, consensus somewhere between fifty-nine and sixty-five thousand, which would be a historically weak number either way. Wednesday's ADP report showed private payrolls up eighty-six thousand, beating expectations. Neither of those drove Thursday's crash — that was purely an equity story, not a macro one. Retail gas keeps sliding too, now just over four dollars nationally and still falling.

On business: Qualcomm's fiscal fourth quarter beat estimates, helped by a big one-time tax benefit from releasing an old reserve, and the company guided non-handset revenue up more than sixty percent next year while reaffirming a five-billion-dollar data-center revenue target. Caterpillar's third quarter, reported back on October twenty-ninth, showed sales up seventeen percent and a record order backlog over seventy-eight billion dollars, much of it tied to power generation for, you guessed it, data centers. Both of those prints are unrelated to Thursday's selloff — genuinely good underlying numbers getting completely overshadowed by the AI trade unwind.

On the Strait of Hormuz: no confirmed Iranian response to the strikes near Qeshm Island earlier this week, and no update on the mediation framework this brief has been tracking. The one data point worth flagging is the dark-tanker count, which dropped sharply overnight after holding steady in the high sixties for weeks — but it's a single noisy reading from a count-based methodology, not yet a confirmed trend, so treat it cautiously.

Quick sports: the Yankees' season is over, but they extended a qualifying offer worth just over twenty-three million dollars to closer David Bednar, who has until November seventeenth to decide. The Knicks were three and four as of their last confirmed result; nothing newer checked out this run. Northwestern is three and six, idle since late October, hosting a ranked Iowa team tomorrow at Ryan Field.

That's The 6:15. Trains are running.
