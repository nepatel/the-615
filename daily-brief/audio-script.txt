Here's the lead. US Central Command says a fighter jet hit the stern of a cargo ship called the Ocean Molika, also known as the Arika Sun, on Saturday, after the ship left an Iranian port and tried to run the US blockade anyway. The crew had ignored repeated warnings, nobody on board was hurt, and CENTCOM says this is the fourth ship it's disabled, and the hundred and thirty-fifth it's turned back, since the blockade restarted back in mid-July. What's actually new here isn't the running tally — it's the method. Up to now, enforcing that blockade has mainly meant turning ships around, or clearing mines out of the shipping lanes. This time, the US itself used a weapon on a civilian-flagged hull. That's a real escalation in how Washington is enforcing its own side of this standoff, separate from anything Iran is doing.

And that strike landed right in the middle of Iran's own worst week of the war. Shipping trackers counted at least a dozen attacks or near-attacks on tankers in the week running up to October 5th, the highest weekly count since the war started back in February. After Iran's Revolutionary Guard said Friday that it had hit a tanker called the Sun Shine for taking what it called an unauthorized route through the strait, it didn't stop there — it announced it was widening its own rules of engagement. No longer just the strait itself: any ship, anywhere in the region, and any company Iran accuses of cooperating with Washington, could now be a target. Then Saturday brought a pile-up of incidents on top of that: a claimed mine strike on another, unnamed supertanker, which nobody's independently confirmed yet; a separate projectile strike on an outbound tanker near the strait; an unexplained incident out in the Gulf of Oman that the shipping trackers flagged without much detail; and a fire aboard a tanker in the Arabian Gulf, where the UAE's coast guard pulled twenty-two crew members out safely.

So here's the read on all of it. This doesn't undo what Trump said earlier in the week — that he won't strike Iran itself before the midterm elections. That promise was specifically about hitting Iranian territory, and the blockade itself was always carved out as something that would keep going regardless. But what's actually happening on the ground is that both sides are escalating the blockade fight at the same time, on a track that's completely separate from whatever happens around the midterm deadline. And here's the thing — none of this has actually been priced by markets yet, because Friday's closing numbers came before Saturday's strike even happened, and everything's been shut since then. So Monday's opening bell is the very first chance traders get to react to a weekend where the US Navy itself joined the list of parties using force in that strait. And that opening bell lands right at the start of a week that's also carrying four big bank earnings reports and the inflation print, both competing hard for the market's attention against a maritime story that just got a lot more complicated. Worth watching: whether that strike on the Ocean Molika was a one-off, or the start of a pattern CENTCOM keeps repeating — and whether Iran actually follows through on its threat to hit ships outside the strait itself.

A couple more details on the Hormuz story that are worth knowing beyond the main thread. Iran says the tanker it hit Friday, the Sun Shine, is linked to a shipping firm called Natwit, and a senior Tehran cleric said that same day that the strait stays closed until Iran's own conditions are met, and that Iran's whole posture has shifted from defense to offense. Meanwhile, CENTCOM is telling a completely different story about the same waterway — it says it's actually cleared the mines and reopened the main shipping lanes, and that thousands of commercial ships have transited safely under American protection. Those two accounts really don't square with each other, and this brief isn't going to try to referee which one's closer to the truth. It's worth sitting with that gap for a second: one side says the strait is closed and getting more dangerous by the day, the other says it's basically functioning under escort. Traders pricing oil risk off this story are, in effect, picking one of those two narratives whether they realize it or not.
