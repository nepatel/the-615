The 6:15, for Friday, September 18th. Here's what moved overnight — well, really, over the last six days, since there was no issue between last Saturday and this morning. The big one: the Fed hiked rates for the first time since 2023, a quarter point, to a range of three-and-three-quarters to four percent. And stocks partied through it.

Quick sweep of the board before we get into it. Oil's fallen for three straight sessions, Brent down around two percent to just above one-oh-two a barrel, WTI just under a hundred; the ten-year yield eased to just under five percent; stocks just had their best run in weeks, with the Nasdaq up nearly two percent Thursday; the dollar hit its highest level in about seven weeks; gold's bouncing around forty-three hundred an ounce; gas at the pump crept up to about four forty-four a gallon nationally even as oil falls; the Hormuz numbers are a mess this issue, with tanker-transit counts from different trackers flatly disagreeing with each other; the Yankees split two with Minnesota and still hold the top wild card spot; the Knicks still haven't extended Josh Hart; and Northwestern hosts Colorado Saturday evening.

Now the lead. On Wednesday, the Federal Reserve's policy committee voted unanimously to raise interest rates a quarter point, its first hike in three years, breaking with the cutting bias that's defined this cycle. Fed official Kevin Warsh told reporters afterward that inflation is, quote, still too high, end quote — defending the move even though the Fed's own statement described the economy as running pretty hot: solid spending, strong productivity, robust investment. In other words, they're tightening from a position of strength, not because things are falling apart. And the committee isn't done — the updated projections showed sixteen of eighteen policymakers expect at least one more hike before this is over, and four of those think there are two more coming.

Here's the strange part. Stocks did not treat any of this as bad news. The day after the decision, all three major averages climbed for a third straight session — the Dow, the S&P, the Nasdaq, all up solidly, with the Nasdaq leading, up nearly one and three quarters percent. Oddly, the VIX — the market's fear gauge — actually ticked up three percent that same day, which doesn't fit the normal script of stocks rallying while fear recedes. That tells you options traders are still bracing for volatility even as the headline numbers celebrate.

So what's actually driving the rally, if not the Fed news itself? Two things. First, Nvidia's CEO told investors Thursday he expects the company to sell twice as many chips next year — that re-lit the whole AI trade, pushed Nvidia up about two percent, and dragged Samsung and SK Hynix higher in Asian trading Friday morning. Second, oil just keeps falling — a third straight losing session — on reports that more Saudi crude is finding its way to market through workarounds via Oman, even as the actual Gulf diplomacy that first triggered oil's reversal a week ago has stalled out completely, more on that in a minute.

Put it together and here's the read: the Fed hiked into an inflation picture that was substantially an energy story, and that energy story is already unwinding on its own, for reasons that have nothing to do with the interest rate decision. Meanwhile a chip-demand narrative is giving stocks a completely separate reason to rally. That sets up a real risk heading into next month — if oil keeps sliding on supply workarounds rather than an actual settlement in the Gulf, the Fed may end up having tightened into a shock that was already fading by the time they acted, right as they're signaling three or four more hikes are still on the table. There's a consumer angle here too — that quarter-point hike raises the floor under things like credit cards and home equity lines right as gas prices tick up and homebuilders cut their forecasts, so tighter money and a softening housing market are arriving at the same time, not offsetting each other.

On politics — Delaware's Senate primary, the last one of this year's cycle, wrapped up Tuesday. Incumbent Chris Coons crushed three primary challengers with about eighty-four percent of the vote and moves on to face Republican Doctor Mike Katz in November. It's not considered a competitive seat, so mostly this just clears the primary calendar going forward.

On the economy — beyond the Fed's own hike, watch the calendar: initial jobless claims and the Fed's preferred inflation gauge, the PCE, are both expected in the back half of next week, though those dates come from a single preview and aren't fully nailed down. One thing we genuinely don't know right now: how the two-year and thirty-year Treasury yields moved, which would tell us whether the bond market sees this as a one-time inflation response or the start of something longer.

In business — homebuilder Lennar badly missed earnings Wednesday, on both profit and revenue, and cut its full-year delivery guidance for the second quarter running, blaming higher rates and weakening conditions. FedEx reports after today's close; that's often read as a bellwether for freight and consumer spending broadly, so a weak number there on top of Lennar's miss would be a real signal that tighter policy is starting to bite.

In technology — that's really the Nvidia story already covered in the lead. Worth noting: Huang's doubled-demand comment was just a comment to investors, not an updated official filing, and it comes with the same caveat that's dogged this trade all year — whether actual chip production can keep up with demand.

Turning to the war in Ukraine — Russia hit a major steel plant with ballistic missiles Thursday and struck Zaporizhzhia city in a wave that Ukrainian officials say killed at least one person and injured close to twenty. President Zelensky says Russia has now launched more than thirty-five hundred drones and close to one hundred ninety missiles at Ukraine just this month. Ukraine kept up its own strikes on Russian air defense and drone sites. Overall, the picture across the last several days is less about the front line moving and more about a grinding air war on infrastructure on both sides.

And on Iran and the Strait of Hormuz — remember that meeting we mentioned last issue, the one Oman was brokering between Gulf states and Iran to reopen shipping? It got postponed indefinitely, with no real explanation beyond the Omani foreign minister saying it was, quote, in the interests of consensus. Diplomats say a real fix still needs a separate US-Iran deal, and Washington and Tehran remain far apart on what that would even look like. Separately, Iran's state media claims one of its commercial ships was hit near Qeshm Island last weekend; the US military says that's false and blames an Iranian drone instead — so, another unresolved dispute. And the shipping-traffic numbers out of the strait are genuinely contradictory this week, with two different data services reporting opposite trends for the exact same day. So, bottom line: the diplomacy went backward, the on-the-ground picture stayed murky, and yet oil prices fell anyway — which tells you markets are trading the reported diplomacy and workarounds, not the underlying security situation itself.

Quickly to sports. The Yankees split a two-game set with Minnesota, winning big Tuesday and dropping a close one Wednesday; they're eighty-eight and sixty-four, holding the top wild card spot in the American League. The Knicks still haven't locked up Josh Hart on a contract extension, and there's no urgency to get it done before the season starts. And Northwestern hosts an undefeated Colorado team Saturday evening, favored by a field goal and a half, in what's actually the last game at their current lakefront stadium before the move to the new Ryan Field.
