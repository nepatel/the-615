The 6:15, for Tuesday, October sixth. Here's what moved overnight: the Fed's odds of an October hike just collapsed to a cycle low, after Friday's jobs report badly missed.

Quick board, all rounded: oil's slipping again this morning, Brent in the high nineties, WTI around eighty-eight, even after a wartime-high run of tanker strikes in the Strait of Hormuz; the ten-year's sitting a bit above five and a quarter percent; stock futures are modestly higher, building on Friday's record Nasdaq close; the dollar and gold are both a touch firmer than Monday; markets in Tokyo and Hong Kong closed up around one percent; gas is a little over four thirty-five a gallon nationally; in the Gulf, transit traffic is still basically frozen, vessel-holding counts are bouncing around in the low two hundreds, and war-risk insurance is still running about thirty times pre-crisis levels; and on the sports side, the Yankees are down two games to none in the ALDS, the Knicks dropped their preseason opener, and Northwestern beat Penn State.

Now the lead. September payrolls, out Friday morning, were ugly: the economy added only about twenty-nine thousand jobs, against expectations for something like eighty to ninety thousand — one of the biggest misses of this cycle. Unemployment ticked up to four point two percent. The two prior months got revised down by a combined sixty thousand, so on net, including those revisions, the economy actually lost jobs in the period. Wage growth was soft too, up just a tenth of a percent.

Markets reacted fast and hard. The odds of a quarter-point hike at the October twenty-seventh and twenty-eighth Fed meeting collapsed to around seventeen percent — a cycle low — down from the high seventies just before the report came out. That's the same story this show has been tracking all week, just the next chapter: Thursday's issue had hike odds already falling to around thirty-five percent on a cooler inflation read; Friday's jobs report finished the job. December's a different story — odds of a hike at that meeting are still sitting around two-thirds, so the market thinks the Fed skips October but keeps the door open for December. Stocks loved it: the Nasdaq crossed thirty-one thousand for the first time ever on an intraday basis Friday, the Dow added a few hundred points, chip stocks led the way, and the ten-year yield dropped more than five basis points. This morning's futures are extending that move, modestly higher.

Here's the twist, though: oil did not play along. Normally, weak jobs data that's dovish for the Fed pulls oil down with yields, and that's roughly what's happening — but for a completely different reason than you'd expect. Even as oil fell, UK Maritime Trade Operations disclosed that four tankers were hit and a fifth directly threatened in the Strait of Hormuz since Sunday — the most intense stretch of attacks since the US-Iran war started back in February. You'd think that would send oil higher, the way it did last week. Instead, Brent and WTI are both down for a second straight session. Why? Middle East crude exports actually topped pre-war levels on four of the last seven days, despite the attacks — the oil is still getting out. The G7 agreed over the weekend to release a hundred million barrels from emergency reserves and promised not to restrict exports. And Saudi Arabia just cut its November price to Asia by five dollars a barrel, a much deeper discount than before, which reads like Riyadh is bracing for weak demand, not tight supply. So right now, rates and stocks are trading off the weak jobs data, and oil is trading off exports and reserve releases — two completely decoupled stories, which is a genuine flip from last week, when it was oil setting the tone and the Fed data getting ignored. The thing to watch is CPI on October fourteenth, which will decide whether that October hold sticks or the December hike case comes roaring back. Also worth watching: the IMF's own count of ships actually transiting the strait hasn't updated in ten days, stuck at a single vessel — so there's a real gap between the export data everyone's trading off and the official transit numbers, and eventually one of those has to catch up to the other.

On politics: a new NBC poll out Sunday has Democrats up eight points on the generic ballot heading into next month's midterms, with two-thirds of voters saying they're highly engaged. That's roughly the same margin Democrats had heading into the 2018 wave, when they flipped forty House seats — worth noting as a read, not a result, this far out. The government funding deal that runs through December eleventh is still the thing keeping a shutdown off the table before Election Day.

On the economy: September's ISM manufacturing survey came in a bit soft, missing expectations, though still in expansion territory — but that's old news next to Friday's jobs report. The next big data point is CPI on the fourteenth, then producer prices the next day. The question now is whether a cooling labor market and already-soft inflation pull December's hike odds down the same way October's just got wiped out.

On business: Nike's actual results landed better than feared — they beat on earnings per share but missed slightly on revenue, with sales down four percent from a year ago. Margins actually improved, helped by cost management, but their China business remains rough, down more than a quarter. They're guiding to a tough fiscal year ahead, with revenue declining at a high single-digit pace.

On geopolitics: Russia hit Ukraine with another large overnight barrage — somewhere around ninety drones and a handful of missiles aimed at Kyiv and the port city of Odesa — most of it was intercepted, but there's at least one confirmed death in the south. Separately, OPEC-plus's monitoring committee met over the weekend and basically punted — no new signal on whether they'll keep unwinding their production cuts.

On Iran and the strait: the attack picture there is genuinely the worst it's been since the war started in February — four tankers hit, one more threatened, all since Sunday, with no one claiming responsibility. At the same time, there's a diplomatic track running in parallel — Qatari mediators were set to hold separate talks with Iran's foreign minister and with the US side in New York this week, built around a seven-day plan to reopen the strait in exchange for unfreezing Iranian assets and lifting sanctions. Trump's already called that unacceptable in public, and Tehran says it's still waiting for something official. No outcome from those talks yet. The damage pattern itself has been fairly consistent — projectiles hitting engine rooms and sterns, several ships losing power and needing a tow before they could get going again, nobody hurt so far in this latest batch. And as covered in the lead, that physical escalation isn't actually showing up in the export numbers, and insurers are still pricing transits at something like thirty times what they cost before the crisis — which is the strangest part of this whole morning: the war is visibly getting worse, and the oil market is shrugging.

Sports, quickly: the Yankees lost game two in Tampa Bay, five to two, and are now down oh-and-two in the Division Series, facing elimination Wednesday night at home. The Knicks got blown out in their preseason opener in Philadelphia, ninety-seven to one-twenty. And Northwestern beat Penn State handily, thirty-four to thirteen, improving to three and one on the season.

That's The 6:15. Trains are running.
