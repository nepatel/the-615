The 6:15, for Wednesday, September 16th. Here's what moved overnight: the ten-year Treasury yield closed above five percent for the first time since 2007, just hours before a Fed that's almost certain to raise rates today.

Quick board sweep before the lead: oil is back near one-oh-eight after the one Gulf diplomacy meeting that had been propping up hopes for a Hormuz reopening got postponed over the weekend; stocks fell for a third straight session into the Fed decision; a tanker got hit in the strait again; the Yankees split two with the Twins; and Northwestern's still waiting on Saturday's home opener against Colorado.

Now the lead. Tuesday did something Friday's relief rally couldn't undo. The ten-year touched just above five percent in the morning, and closed at five-oh-one — the first close above that level since the summer of 2007. That's not a domestic data story. It's a Middle East story. Over the weekend, Oman's foreign minister announced that the Salalah talks — the meeting between Gulf Arab states and Iran that was supposed to produce a temporary way to get commercial ships back through Hormuz — got postponed indefinitely, one day before it was supposed to happen. Nobody's said why.

With that off-ramp gone, oil went right back to climbing. Brent had settled Friday around one-oh-four and a half; by Tuesday it was back up near one-oh-nine. And just to reinforce that the underlying risk hasn't gone anywhere, a tanker — the El Gaia, flying a Panama flag with an Indian crew — got hit near Hengam Island inside the strait. Most of the crew was rescued; one person's still missing.

Put that together and you get the mechanism for why yields ripped through five percent: the inflation shock from oil has no near-term relief valve now, which means the Fed probably has to lean harder and longer than just one hike. Futures markets have today's two o'clock decision priced at ninety-two, ninety-three percent for a quarter-point hike — the first hike since 2023. And it's not just the long end moving — the two-year's up too, at four point three nine percent, and the thirty-year's over five and a quarter. That's a curve that's risen across the board, which tells you the market isn't just pricing one supply shock — it thinks this could be the start of an actual tightening cycle.

Stocks did not like it. The Dow, S&P, and Nasdaq all fell Tuesday — third straight losing session — and this time there's no oil-reversal story to hide behind like there was Friday. Futures ticked up a little before the open this morning, but that looks more like positioning ahead of the two-thirty press conference than any real read on what's coming.

So today's statement matters less for the hike itself, which is basically already priced, and more for what the dot plot says about next year. A hawkish set of projections stacked on top of an already-priced hike is what could push the ten-year even higher from here — and remember, a jump like this in a single session hits mortgage rates and corporate borrowing costs immediately, it's not some abstract bond-market thing. The last time yields sat up this high for any real stretch was right before the 2007, 2008 credit crisis — worth keeping in mind that this move is about the level, not a repeat of that cause. This is an oil and geopolitics story wearing a rates costume, not a housing story.

On to the rest. In politics: the Supreme Court blocked the Trump administration's attempt to let the Postal Service withhold mail ballots for the midterms — a real loss for the White House, though one justice noted the underlying rule might've been legal if there'd been more time to implement it. Trump called the ruling a disaster. And Delaware wrapped up the entire 2026 primary season Tuesday — Chris Coons won his primary easily and moves on to the general election in November.

On economics: today's Fed decision is the story, covered in the lead. The one thing to flag separately — this would be the first hike of the current Fed chair's tenure, and the first hike of any kind since 2023, coming not because the economy's overheating in the usual sense but because an oil shock from a war zone won't quit. Worth remembering the last time the Fed hiked into a supply shock like this was arguably back in the nineteen-seventies oil embargoes — a comparison to use carefully, but one that's probably going to come up in today's press conference.

In business: Oracle's stock is still up more than ten percent over the past week, even after giving back its initial post-earnings pop — investors keep rewarding its cloud backlog and its ties to the OpenAI ecosystem. Adobe's Friday bounce-back hasn't moved much since, and neither name looks like it's driving this week's broader market weakness — that's a macro story, not an earnings story. And tonight, hours after the Fed decision, Lennar reports earnings — consensus has earnings down more than a third year over year and margins compressing to around sixteen percent from seventeen and a half a year ago. Watch orders and cancellation rates for a read on whether builders are already feeling higher rates bite. Given the timing, tonight's guidance might already look stale by the time investors read it, and FedEx follows Friday with its own read on freight demand.

In technology: that OpenAI mathematics dispute got messier. The NYU mathematician on the other side, Buckmaster, is now alleging OpenAI's team argued twice that his collaborator shouldn't get co-author credit specifically because that collaborator works at Anthropic — and that he was told, quote, "why would you ruin your career," when he pushed to go public. OpenAI's Bubeck is calling the whole account false, and Sam Altman publicly backed him within half an hour. Neither side's story is independently confirmed. Meanwhile, a Chinese AI lab, Z dot A-I, just closed roughly five billion dollars in financing for its next generation of models — a reminder the AI capital race isn't just a U.S. story.

In geopolitics: Ukraine gave its first explicit confirmation that it's running a real counteroffensive northwest of a town called Lyman, aimed at breaking up a Russian encirclement attempt. Zelensky again said Ukraine would accept a mutual ceasefire on energy targets, something Trump's been pushing; the Kremlin's still saying no to anything short of total war aims. Russia hit Kyiv with drones Tuesday, killing at least one person.

And on Iran and Hormuz, beyond what's already in the lead: the ground situation is basically frozen in place even as the diplomatic track gets worse. Single-digit daily ship transits, insurance quotes that are over a week stale because nobody's writing new ones, and the Houthis still holding the stretch of Yemen's coast they took control of last week. Two separate trackers now agree the strait's been effectively closed for a hundred and ninety nine days.
