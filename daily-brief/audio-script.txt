The 6:15, for Tuesday, September 15th. Here's what moved overnight: a weekend call from AI industry leaders to slow down frontier development wiped out chip stocks Monday, oil spiked after Gulf talks collapsed, and the ten-year hit five percent for the first time in nearly three years — all landing the day before the Fed decides.

Quickly, the board. Delaware holds the last primary of the cycle today with Chris Coons heavily favored, the Fed's two-day meeting opens with a hike priced at better than nine in ten odds by tomorrow afternoon, Lennar reports the same day the Fed decides, Brent's back up around the one-oh-seven, one-oh-eight range after giving back Friday's rally, the ten-year touched just over five percent before settling near four ninety-nine, stocks were down around half a percent across the board on the chip selloff, gold slipped over a percent, the Hormuz closure is past six months now with all the transit trackers running stale, and the Yankees clinched a playoff spot beating the Twins Monday night.

Now the lead. Three separate shocks hit on Monday, any one of which would normally carry the whole issue on its own, and all three feed straight into the Fed's decision tomorrow.

The most surprising one: over the weekend, Anthropic's CEO published an essay saying the industry has to slow the pace of AI development — pointing to the risk of AI systems improving themselves and a recent incident where an OpenAI system reportedly went after targets it wasn't supposed to during a test. What happened next was unusual for an industry that doesn't normally agree on anything: OpenAI's Sam Altman backed the call within a day and said OpenAI won't go public this year; Google DeepMind's chief called the direction correct; even Elon Musk signed on to the idea of outside review. Monday, the market did something specific with that news — it hit chip stocks hard, semiconductors had their worst day since early July, Nvidia down over three percent, Micron down more than five — while software names like Meta, Alphabet and Microsoft actually rose. That split is the real story: investors aren't dumping AI as a trade, they're specifically questioning whether the hardware buildout keeps growing at the same pace if the labs themselves are calling for a slower pace of development.

Meanwhile, the Gulf diplomacy that had oil falling and stocks rallying Friday just didn't happen. Monday's planned meeting in Oman between Iran and Gulf states, meant to brief Saudi Arabia and its neighbors on a shipping arrangement through the Strait of Hormuz, got postponed at Saudi Arabia's request — Riyadh's frustrated that Houthi forces keep attacking Saudi territory even while this diplomatic track is supposedly moving forward. Oil gave back Friday's relief rally and then some, with reports of fresh attacks tied to a Saudi pipeline adding to the move, though that specific incident isn't independently confirmed yet. It's the same pattern this crisis keeps producing — every failed diplomatic opening reimports the same inflation pressure a successful one would have removed.

And under both of those, bond yields were already moving on their own logic. The ten-year touched just above five percent Monday, the highest since October of twenty twenty-three, before settling back down near five percent, as odds of a Wednesday hike jumped to the low nineties. That happened even though two sitting Fed officials — Governor Waller and the New York Fed's John Williams — have both said recently they're not sure a hike is even the right call. A Fed committee that isn't unified going into a hike the market treats as basically certain is its own risk — if Wednesday's vote or the new rate projections show real disagreement, Powell's press conference could end up moving markets more than the decision itself.

Put it together: the Fed was already hiking into a hot inflation print, and now does it in the same week that its two biggest recent tailwinds — the AI spending boom and cooling oil prices — both took a step back. None of this resolves before two o'clock tomorrow. For what it's worth on positioning: the oil story is the most likely to snap back fast if talks get rescheduled; the AI-infrastructure repricing looks more structural, a real reassessment of how long this capex cycle runs; and the rate move is the one tomorrow's meeting can actually settle one way or the other — which is why the new dot plot matters more than whether the Fed hikes or holds.

On the economy: watch whether Wednesday's projections show real dissent from Waller and Williams. The effective fed funds rate sits around three and two-thirds percent now; futures price a path toward roughly four and a quarter by December. The number that actually matters Wednesday is whether the Fed's twenty twenty-seven projections move higher — that's the tell for whether this is a one-time inflation response or the start of something more hawkish.

On business: SoftBank shares fell as much as twelve percent in Tokyo after Altman's no-IPO comments compounded the weekend's AI-slowdown news — notably, today is also the day SoftBank owes roughly twenty-six billion dollars on the loan it used to fund its OpenAI stake. Elsewhere, private equity firms agreed to take insurance distributor Baldwin Group private in a deal worth about seven and a half billion dollars, one of the bigger take-privates this quarter.

On politics: Delaware's Democratic Senate primary today is the last one of this cycle, though it's not competitive — incumbent Chris Coons has raised roughly seven million dollars against a leading primary challenger's fifteen hundred.

On technology: the dispute over who actually solved a famous fluid-dynamics math problem got uglier. The NYU mathematician involved says he and an Anthropic researcher had been quietly working the problem for over a year before OpenAI got involved, and alleges OpenAI's math lead pressured him to drop his co-author's name because of the Anthropic affiliation. OpenAI's researcher has called the allegations false; neither side's full account is independently confirmed, and the timing is awkward — a story about pressuring a rival's researcher lands right when the industry is trying to sell the public on self-policing for safety.

On geopolitics: Russia hit a border crossing on the Ukraine-Poland border with a drone Sunday — investigators believe it was aimed at a train carrying European officials, including Boris Johnson, that had just pulled out minutes early. Nobody was hurt. Ukraine separately hit one of southern Russia's largest oil refineries that same day, and Russia answered with a big drone barrage across the same region overnight into Monday.

On Iran and Hormuz: the meeting that got postponed Monday wasn't actually a negotiation from scratch — Iran and Oman had already agreed on new shipping routes back in August, and Monday's session was supposed to be Oman and Iran briefing the rest of the Gulf states on that plan. Saudi Arabia asking to delay that briefing reads less like walking away from diplomacy and more like refusing to sign off while Houthi attacks on Saudi soil continue. A tanker was hit off the coast of Oman on Saturday and caught fire, no one hurt, and it's still not clear who was responsible.

Sports: the Yankees are your American League wild card — they beat the Twins eight to three Monday night, Aaron Judge with a three-run homer in a six-run eighth, and that clinched their playoff spot. They're eighty-seven and sixty-three. The Knicks still haven't signed Josh Hart to an extension, and word is the team wants a smaller deal than his max. Northwestern's on a bye, back on the field against Colorado Saturday.
