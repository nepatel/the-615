The 6:15, for Thursday, September 17th. Here's what moved overnight: the Fed hiked rates for the first time since 2023, and it did it under a brand new chair.

Quick sweep of the board: the Fed's new chair, Kevin Warsh, just delivered his first rate hike, a quarter point, taking the Fed's target up to a range of three-and-three-quarters to four percent, on a unanimous vote, with most of the committee penciling in at least one more hike before year end; the ten-year treasury yield jumped through five percent for the first time since 2007; the dollar broke above a level it hadn't cleared in weeks; stocks slipped on the decision but futures are pointing modestly higher this morning; oil eased back, with Brent in the high one-oh-sevens and WTI just above one-oh-two, not because Gulf diplomacy worked, but because Saudi Arabia says it can partially repair a damaged pipeline within days; shipping through the Strait of Hormuz is still running at a small fraction of normal; gas at the pump keeps climbing, north of four dollars thirty-five a gallon nationally; and the Yankees already clinched a playoff spot, then lost a heartbreaker in extra innings.

Here's the lead. Wednesday closed a loop we've been tracking for weeks: the Fed raised rates a quarter point, to a range of three-and-three-quarters to four percent, on a unanimous vote — the first hike since 2023. The committee's statement talked about resilient spending, strong productivity, robust investment, and a job market keeping pace, while flagging elevated uncertainty tied partly to geopolitics. It was also a first in a different way: this was Kevin Warsh's debut running the press conference as Fed chair, not Jerome Powell's. Warsh was confirmed back in May and sworn in May twenty-second; Powell's still a sitting governor, but he's no longer running the show — worth being precise about, since we've been sloppy on that point before.

Warsh's framing in the presser was pointed. "We cannot affect any individual price," he said, naming oil and groceries specifically — the Fed's job, as he put it, is to stop a relative-price shock from spreading into what he called second and third order effects across the rest of the economy. He said three things changed since the last meeting, and led with stronger-than-expected data — almost certainly a nod to Wednesday's retail sales report, which beat sharply, up one-point-two percent against a consensus of point-eight, the best monthly gain in five months. Multiple reports on the dot plot have sixteen of eighteen committee members projecting at least one more hike this year. Put together: a hawkish committee, under a new chair drawing a hard line against inflation spreading, hiking into strength rather than weakness.

Markets took the hawkishness seriously. All three major averages had been higher ahead of the two o'clock decision and gave it back: the Dow fell just over one percent, the S&P slipped almost half a percent, the Nasdaq closed basically flat. The bond market moved harder and it stuck: the ten-year yield pushed through five percent and closed around five-point-oh-two — the highest since July of 2007, a level that had held since late 2023 through every scare we've covered. The dollar index cleared one hundred for the first time in weeks, with one technical read calling the move overbought and noting the hawkish dot plot has essentially wiped out expectations for rate cuts in 2027.

Here's the part that complicates the simple story. Remember last issue, we flagged a reported meeting in Oman where Gulf and Iranian diplomats were supposed to discuss easing Hormuz shipping. That meeting never happened — it got postponed at the last minute after Saudi Arabia raised objections. So the diplomatic path to easing oil-supply risk collapsed completely — and yet oil eased anyway, for a totally different reason. Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline, the route that lets the kingdom move crude to the Red Sea without going through Hormuz at all, got knocked offline after a drone strike damaged two pumping stations — and there's real disagreement in our sourcing about whether that strike came from Iraq or was a Houthi operation; we're not resolving that here. What actually moved the market Wednesday was a Bloomberg report that Saudi Aramco can restore about half that pipeline's capacity within days, with full repair targeted in about six weeks. That pushed crude lower. But to be clear: that's Saudi Arabia fixing its own workaround, not Iran reopening the strait. Hormuz itself is still running at a small fraction of normal traffic.

So the setup into the next stretch is genuinely two-sided. You've got a Fed that just hiked, with most of the committee expecting more, sitting against an eighteen-year high in the ten-year and a dollar breaking out — that's a real tightening of financial conditions on top of the rate move itself. And whether oil keeps easing depends entirely on execution risk in a six-week pipeline repair timeline, not on any actual reduction in the underlying risk of running the strait.

Quick hits around the rest of the board. In politics: the Supreme Court blocked the administration's push for new mail-in ballot rules ahead of the midterms, and the House voted unanimously to recommend contempt charges against Leon Black over the Epstein subpoenas.

In economics: there's a new estimate out that the Iran war has driven more than forty percent of this year's second-quarter inflation, mostly through reduced shipping, and it's expected to keep adding to inflation into next year — plus the war's direct cost is now estimated around thirty-eight billion dollars and climbing. Keep an eye on the Bank of Japan tomorrow too — their rate's still under one percent against a Fed that just moved above three-and-a-half, and that gap, combined with the stronger dollar, is the kind of setup that's caused trouble before.

In business: Oracle has given back its entire 2025 stock gain this year as investors weigh its huge order backlog against how much cash the AI buildout is burning — Larry Ellison actually canceled a planned stock sale last week. Adobe beat on both revenue and earnings but still dropped after-hours because next quarter's guidance came in just a touch light, and Adobe also announced its CEO will hand off to Anil Chakravarthy in December.

In technology: no big new AI model this week, but there was a cluster of funding rounds — orbital transport, AI drug discovery, enterprise software — that's worth watching as a sign capital might be spreading out beyond the big foundation-model labs.

In geopolitics: Ukraine killed a Russian brigade commander in a drone strike, Russia sent about eighty drones at Ukraine overnight, and both sides made small gains on the ground. Zelensky says Russian drones have targeted his plane over Moldova twice in the past two weeks.

And on Iran and Hormuz: beyond the pipeline story in the lead, shipping through the strait is still stuck at roughly ten to fifteen percent of normal, essentially unchanged. And there's a mutual-defense pact between Saudi Arabia, Pakistan and Turkey that this week's pipeline strike would seem to test — nobody's invoked it yet, but it's one to watch.
