The 6:15, for Saturday, September 5th. Here's what moved overnight: the August jobs report blew past expectations Friday morning, and paradoxically, that sent stocks down and yields up, because it wrecked half of Fed Governor Waller's case for holding rates steady this month.

Quickly, the state of the board, condensed into one breath since it's the weekend and most of it goes stale by Monday anyway: stocks fell about half a percent across the board Friday, giving back Thursday's rally, with the Dow down around two hundred seventy points; the ten-year yield ticked back up into the high fours; the dollar firmed a touch; gold slipped, though spot and futures prices disagree by more than a hundred dollars an ounce this morning, so don't trust either number too hard; oil settles are genuinely disputed across data providers, with three different sources giving three different Brent prints; the Strait of Hormuz sits at day one hundred eighty-eight of its closure, with shipping trackers now weeks stale and official government claims about how much oil is still flowing wildly outpacing what any tracker can actually verify; Oracle just moved its earnings date, so now it reports the same day as Adobe, next Wednesday; and on the sports side, the Yankees dropped one to the Padres and sit four games back in the AL East, the Knicks still haven't signed Josh Hart to an extension, and Northwestern kicks off its football season tonight.

Now the lead. Friday's August jobs report came in at plus one hundred sixty-two thousand payrolls — that's roughly three times what economists were expecting, which had been in the neighborhood of fifty-three to fifty-five thousand. It's the strongest monthly gain since March. Unemployment held steady at four point one percent, and average hourly earnings ticked up three-tenths of a percent. On its face, that's an unambiguously strong number, a clean beat with no asterisks.

But here's the thing about markets right now: stocks actually fell on it. The S&P, the Dow, and the Nasdaq all gave back most of Thursday's gains in a single session. Bond yields rose, and the dollar firmed up. Gold slipped too. Why would a good jobs number be bad for stocks? Because of the Fed. Just Thursday, Governor Waller had broken publicly with the Fed chair's hawkish stance, essentially saying he wanted to hold rates steady this month, and that he'd give the disinflation trend, quote, "a chance" — he'd even paraphrased John Lennon to make the point. Part of his reasoning leaned on a labor market that already looked like it was cooling — remember, July's payrolls had actually shown an outright decline, something like twenty-three thousand jobs lost. Well, Friday's blowout number takes that leg out from under him. It doesn't kill his argument entirely — he'd already said the inflation report coming on September eleventh would be the truly decisive piece of data for him, not the jobs number — but it makes the "the economy's cooling, there's no rush" case a lot harder to sell to the rest of the committee. A hundred sixty-two thousand new jobs, with wages still climbing, just isn't the picture you'd expect from an economy that needs the Fed's help.

So what markets are pricing in now is a better chance of a rate hike later this month, not a recession scare — and here's the tell: volatility actually ticked down on the day. That's not what you'd see if traders were spooked about growth. It's what you see when they're repricing a policy outcome. Different trading desks put the odds of a September hike anywhere from the high fifties to the low sixties percent, up from something close to a coin flip on Thursday afternoon. The upshot: that coin-flip didn't survive the weekend. All eyes now shift to next Thursday's producer prices and jobless claims, and especially next Friday's consumer inflation report, which just got a lot more important than it already was, because it's now doing double duty — it has to make Waller's case for him, with the jobs data no longer backing him up.

On business, three things worth knowing. Lululemon had an ugly day, falling somewhere around seventeen to eighteen percent after a disappointing quarter, with sales down four percent and same-store sales down nine percent, driven largely by weakness in mainland China. Snowflake, on the other hand, beat estimates comfortably on both earnings and revenue, continuing to grind higher off its report from earlier in the week. And federal regulators opened a formal investigation into Tesla's driverless Cybercabs, which the company just started running commercially in Austin — about a thousand of them are on the road, and the question is whether vehicles that don't even have a steering wheel actually meet federal safety standards under Tesla's own self-certification. That echoes a similar multi-year probe into Amazon's Zoox unit.

On geopolitics: Russia hit Ukraine's intelligence service headquarters in the middle of Kyiv with a jet-powered attack drone, right across from St. Sophia's Cathedral, injuring nine people, two of them seriously. Ukraine's president said the strike specifically targeted the office of the agency's acting chief, who wasn't hurt, and he's promised what he called a symmetrical response. Ukraine, for its part, says it struck two Russian oil refineries deep inside Russian territory, more than five hundred miles from the border. And the planned summit between Xi Jinping and President Trump later this month in Washington is still on the calendar, though Beijing hasn't formally confirmed it's actually coming.

On Iran and the Strait of Hormuz: worth a correction here — the Vice President's comment earlier this week is more precisely quoted as saying Iran's grip on the strait is, quote, "effectively gone," not that the broader six-week conflict itself is over, as we'd characterized it in an earlier issue. He did call it not a war, though, and set no timeline for how this resolves. On the ground, the U.S. military kept up strikes on Iranian targets Friday, but there wasn't a third straight night of Iranian retaliation after this week's attacks on Jordan, Kuwait, the Emirates and Bahrain — call that a pause, not a stand-down. And the numbers coming out of the region remain a mess: the shipping trackers say only a handful of vessels are transiting the strait each day, against a pre-crisis baseline in the dozens; the Treasury Secretary claims millions of barrels a day are still flowing; the President claims dozens of ships a day; and none of those numbers agree with each other or with what independent trackers can verify.

Sports, quickly. The Yankees lost to the Padres four to one Friday night and sit at eighty and sixty-one, four games behind Tampa Bay in the AL East, with a rematch tonight. The Knicks still haven't locked up Josh Hart on a new deal — a three-year contract in the sixty-to-sixty-two-million-dollar range still looks like the favorite, and reports of a bigger four-year offer appear to have been recycled from an old story. And Northwestern opens its football season tonight against South Dakota State, results after this recording.
