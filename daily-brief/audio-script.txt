Now the lead. Two things happened this weekend that you need to hold in your head together. First, Iran and Oman say they're in the final stage of drafting an arrangement to reopen Hormuz — both sides have agreed on the routes, and an Iranian official called it on the verge of being finalized, pending sign-off at higher levels. Trump said Thursday a deal could be announced this week. Second, and this is the part markets haven't fully priced: Turkey, Saudi Arabia, and Pakistan signed a mutual defense agreement in Mecca on Friday — an attack on one is an attack on all three. That pairs NATO's second-largest army, the only Muslim nuclear power, and the strait's biggest oil exporter, all in one pact, all in the same week the Hormuz talks are supposedly close.

Here's the mechanism connecting them. The Hormuz talks are stuck on sequencing, not geography. Tehran wants the war ended, troops withdrawn, and compensation paid before traffic normalizes — Washington's treating that as a non-starter. Washington wants a corridor with no Iranian approval or fee authority — Tehran's resisting that. Oman, brokering the whole thing, has warned that continued attacks on shipping could still blow the deal up before signature. That gap, between routes agreed and a deal actually signed, is exactly where the Mecca pact lands. Riyadh spent the weekend building its own insurance policy, independent of a U.S. security umbrella that hasn't fully reopened the strait in five months of trying.

And here's my read, clearly labeled as a read, not a fact: this looks less like a hedge against Iran, and more like a hedge against Washington's own timeline. A Gulf producer locking in defense guarantees from Ankara and Islamabad, while separately negotiating its own transit corridor with Tehran through Oman, is a state planning for the possibility that American involvement is durable but still not enough — or that it ends before the strait is genuinely safe. It also raises the cost of any American move Riyadh sees as freelancing, right as Trump faces his own domestic pressure to wind the war down, visible in his escalating public feud with Tucker Carlson over whether this whole intervention betrayed what he ran on.

Markets are already pricing convergence, not resolution. Oil's well off its highs, and Friday's rally basically shrugged at the Hormuz story. But the operational reality hasn't caught up to the diplomatic one — insurance is still sky-high, and most of what's moving through that strait right now is running dark. Watch the gap between "deal announced" and "the insurance market actually believes it." That gap is the next trade, not the announcement itself.
