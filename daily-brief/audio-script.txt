The 6:15, for Wednesday, September 2nd. Here's what moved overnight: the U.S. and Iran are exchanging fire around the Strait of Hormuz again, oil jumped to a six-week high, and the bond market just hit its highest yield in a year and a half.

Quick lay of the land before we get into it. The strait's been closed for a hundred eighty five days now; Brent's up around ninety five a barrel and WTI's pushing ninety; the ten-year's up near four point eight percent, the highest since last January; the dollar's holding just under a hundred; the VIX popped back above sixteen after a calm August; stocks slipped about three quarters of a point to a full point across the board Tuesday; gas is right around four bucks nationally; and on the sports side, the Yankees beat the Angels, the Knicks are locking up Josh Hart on a new deal, and Northwestern kicks off Saturday.

Okay, the lead. The calm that held for most of August broke this week, and it broke hard. Late Monday into Tuesday, unknown projectiles hit two commercial tankers — one called the Sidr, one called the Senegal Prosperity — right inside the safe corridor the U.S. Navy patrols. Iran also tried to lay mines in the waterway and hit at a U.S. base in Jordan, according to Trump. The U.S. response Tuesday was big: CENTCOM says it struck about a hundred Iranian targets — air defense, radar, mine-laying gear, communications, missile and drone launchers. And for the first time in this whole conflict, the U.S. also hit two Iranian government tankers directly, missiles into their engine rooms while they sat at anchor. That's a brand new policy — tanker for tanker, Trump approved it recently — meant to make Iran pay a price in ships, not just in strikes.

Iran hit back, but most of it didn't land. Something like twenty five ballistic missiles, only about half even reached Jordanian airspace; drones toward Bahrain and Kuwait mostly got shot down. Iranian state media claims a U.S. strike hit a wedding and killed at least four people — CENTCOM disputes that hard, says they don't target civilians, and nobody's independently confirmed it either way, so take that one with real caution. A U.S. official told Axios the strikes probably bought about a month of lower threat levels for shipping. But Trump's also threatening a much bigger response if Iran hits back again, and Iran's Revolutionary Guard is vowing this continues until, quote, they regret their crime. So — neither side sounds like it's done.

Here's the market mechanism. Brent spiked about five percent Tuesday to near ninety five, its best level since late July. That oil shock landed right on top of a bond market that was already turning hawkish after Fed Chair Warsh's Jackson Hole speech last Friday — the ten-year jumped to its highest since January of last year, and betting markets now put the odds of a September rate hike at about two in three, up from roughly coin-flip odds just a few days ago. Stocks fell across the board Tuesday, and volatility jumped back above sixteen after basically the calmest August in two years. The uncomfortable part for the Fed: an oil-driven inflation shock is landing exactly when Warsh has said he wants to treat underlying inflation as the thing that matters most, two weeks before the Fed's September meeting and with the August inflation report due out in a week and a half — now carrying a fresh energy tailwind that wasn't there when he spoke. A hawkish Fed chair who already wanted to hike into a strong economy now has a geopolitical excuse to treat any inflation bump as real rather than temporary. And there's a third wrinkle: Broadcom reports earnings tonight, right into a market that's digesting an oil shock instead of the calm backdrop Nvidia and Marvell got a week ago.

One more thing on that oil-and-Fed collision before we move on: futures this morning are only modestly lower — S&P futures down less than a tenth of a percent, Nasdaq futures down a quarter point — which tells you markets are treating this as more of the same simmering risk rather than a brand new crisis, at least for now. That could change fast if Iran hits back harder.

On politics: Congress passed a funding bill Tuesday, an overwhelming bipartisan vote, funding the government at current levels through December, avoiding a shutdown before the end of the month. It heads to Trump's desk now. Separately, Massachusetts Democrats picked their Senate nominee Monday — eighty-year-old incumbent Ed Markey beat back a primary challenge from Congressman Seth Moulton pretty comfortably, about two to one, despite real grumbling in the party about its aging leadership.

On the economy: July's jobs report — the one that came out back on August seventh — was ugly. Payrolls actually fell by twenty three thousand, way below the roughly eighty thousand economists expected, and almost all of the miss was public-sector jobs disappearing. But weekly unemployment claims haven't shown that same weakness — the most recent week came in at two-oh-three thousand, actually down a bit. That gap between soft monthly jobs numbers and steady weekly claims is exactly the kind of mixed signal a hawkish Fed can read however it wants. Friday's August jobs report either clears that up or muddies it further, right before the Fed meets.

Business: Apple turned a page Tuesday — Tim Cook's fifteen years as CEO are over, hardware chief John Ternus is now running the company, and Cook moves up to executive chairman. It's a planned, on-schedule handoff, but it puts an engineer in charge of fixing Apple's weakest spot against Google and Microsoft, which is artificial intelligence. Also Tuesday: the FTC and twenty two states sued Amazon, accusing it of secretly jacking up ad auction prices and overcharging more than a million advertisers by over twenty billion dollars since twenty nineteen.

Technology: Anthropic signed its second massive computing deal in a week — thirty five billion dollars this time, with an Nvidia-backed cloud provider called Lambda, for a big data center site in Texas. Nvidia's playing three roles in that one deal — chip supplier, investor in the cloud company, and the landlord on the building — which is drawing some raised eyebrows about circular financing, though nobody's officially called it that.

Geopolitics: Russia hit Kyiv for a sixth straight night, killing seven people, six of them railway workers, in a missile strike on a rail depot. Ukraine hit back at a Russian Baltic port with drones the same night. European officials are scrambling to find more air defense missiles for Ukraine as Zelensky keeps pushing allies to speed up Patriot deliveries.

And on Iran and Hormuz specifically, beyond today's strikes: there's still no confirmed answer on who hit that tanker, the Metro Venetian, back on the twenty third; a detained tanker called the Amara is still being held by Iran with no fresh update; and there's a real head-scratcher in the data — the Energy Secretary says seventeen million barrels moved through the strait Monday, the most since this whole conflict started, but that doesn't square at all with the shipping trackers showing traffic still down around ninety six percent from normal. Nobody's reconciled that yet, so we're flagging it, not explaining it away.

Sports, quick: Yankees beat the Angels seven to three, they're seventy eight and sixty, second in the AL East, holding the top wild card spot. Knicks are finalizing a four-year, eighty one million dollar extension for Josh Hart. Northwestern opens their season Saturday against South Dakota State.
