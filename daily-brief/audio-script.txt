The 6:15, for Saturday, November 21st. Here's what moved overnight: Wall Street's AI trade just rolled over into a full correction, and the stock everybody was celebrating two days ago, Anthropic, got dragged down with it.

Quick scan of the board before we get into it. Northwestern's at Minnesota at noon today, the Knicks played at Minnesota's Timberwolves Thursday night but we don't have a confirmed score for you, Walmart beat earnings and raised its outlook, the S&P is down about three and three quarters percent and into correction territory, oil's in the low nineties for Brent and low eighties for WTI, the ten-year's around four point nine percent, and the Hormuz situation is unchanged, still stalled, still getting hit every few days. Markets are closed Thanksgiving Thursday, there's a big OPEC meeting the Sunday after next, and Jensen Huang is now on the hook for two separate rounds of testimony in Washington, not just one.

Okay, the lead. Stocks had their worst week since last spring. The S&P 500 closed Friday down almost four percent, its seventh straight losing session, which is enough to put it more than ten percent below its October high — that's officially a correction. The Dow fell about two percent, the Nasdaq fell more than five percent and wiped out all of its gains for the year, and the VIX, the market's fear gauge, jumped better than a quarter higher. So this was not a one-stock story, this was broad.

What actually triggered it was chips. Nvidia fell fourteen percent in a single session after Reuters, citing the Wall Street Journal, reported that the SEC has opened an inquiry into Nvidia's disclosures — this is following Jensen Huang's resignation as CEO back on November twelfth — and part of what they're looking at is whether the company misled investors. Nvidia hasn't commented, the SEC won't confirm or deny it exists, but California's attorney general is separately weighing action, and the Treasury Secretary has said publicly he wants answers from Nvidia's board within ten days — so watch for that deadline around Thanksgiving week. On top of that, Broadcom fell about twelve percent on an unconfirmed report that Google might cut next year's chip orders, and AMD and Micron each fell more than thirteen percent too.

Worth sitting with that Nvidia number for a second: fourteen percent in one day is a huge move for a company that size, and it's happening just over a week after the company's own CEO walked away following reports about an affair with a woman accused of ties to Chinese intelligence. The board already said flatly it's not bringing him back. Now add a federal investigation into the disclosures on top of that, and you can see why the stock is the single biggest driver of Friday's selling.

And here's the part that connects to what we told you Thursday: Anthropic. Remember, Anthropic's stock, ticker ANT, had this huge debut Thursday, opened at around a hundred eleven and closed its first day up seventeen percent from the eighty-three dollar IPO price. Well, Friday, its second day ever trading, it fell another eight and a half percent. It's still above its IPO price, but it's now down about twenty percent from where it opened on day one. A couple of days ago, one Wall Street analyst said a push toward one-forty would prove AI enthusiasm had fully come back. Instead, two sessions in, Anthropic's riding down with the very sector it was supposed to be vindicating. That's a read on sentiment, not a verdict on the company.

What's strange is this all happened while the Fed is still talking tough on rates. A Fed governor, Lisa Cook, said this week that waiting too long to raise rates risks falling behind the curve — so you've got a hawkish Fed and a stock-market panic happening at the same time, which doesn't usually go together. One strategist at BNP Paribas called Friday's action flat-out panic selling. A Deutsche Bank strategist took the other side, called the market very oversold, and said the actual growth and earnings underneath all this are still strong. Two very different reads on the same selloff — we're not picking a winner there.

What to watch: does Monday's open make it eight losing sessions in a row, or does it stabilize? Does the SEC actually confirm a formal Nvidia investigation before that ten-day clock runs out? And does Anthropic's stock find a floor above its eighty-three dollar offer price once the post-IPO quiet period ends?

A quick word on Walmart, since it got a little lost in all this. Their earnings Thursday actually beat on both revenue and profit, online sales in the US were up twenty-three percent, and they raised their full-year guidance for the third time this year. Shares popped on that news Thursday morning. Then Friday happened, and Walmart went down with basically everything else that isn't a meme of the AI story. That's the tell that Friday was systemic, not about any one company.

On technology beyond the market stuff: Jensen Huang now has to testify twice. There's the closed-door House hearing already set for December first, and now he's also agreed to go in front of the Senate Banking Committee, sometime before Christmas, no firm date yet. The Commerce Secretary is expected to testify there too, voluntarily. So you've got the SEC inquiry, a California attorney general review, and now two rounds of Congressional testimony, all stacking up against the same resignation.

On the war in Ukraine: Russia's defense ministry says it intercepted over fourteen hundred Ukrainian drones in one overnight attack this past week, which it's calling the largest since the war started — that's even bigger than the sixteen-hundred — sorry, the thirteen-hundred-plus drone attack we told you about just two days before that. Separately, the US envoy Jared Kushner says Russia has now accepted a format that brings European negotiators into the talks, and he's pointing to a recent prisoner exchange as a sign of real progress. Kyiv wants that European role made official and permanent, not just occasional.

On Iran and the Strait of Hormuz: no real change to report. Attacks are continuing at roughly the same pace we've been tracking since the summer, diplomacy is still stalled, and the flow and war-risk numbers from different trackers still don't agree with each other, so we're not forcing them into one number.
