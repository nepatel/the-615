The 6:15, for Tuesday, November 3rd. Here's what moved overnight: hours before the midterms, the U.S. struck Iranian targets near the Strait of Hormuz — breaking the president's own promise not to hit Iran before the election.

Quick version of the board, because the story's bigger than the numbers today: stocks closed mixed Monday, the S and P and Nasdaq down around half a percent to one percent, the Dow up around four tenths of a percent; oil had its worst day in over two months, Brent down about seven and a half percent to just over a hundred five dollars, on hopes of an Iran deal, before the overnight strikes muddied that; the ten-year's at about four point nine percent; gas prices keep drifting lower, now around four fifteen a gallon nationally; and in Hormuz-tracker terms, the strait's been effectively closed since late February, dark-tanker traffic spiked overnight to nearly seventy percent of screened vessels, up sharply from Sunday, though that could be noise as easily as signal. On the board otherwise: the Knicks finally have a clean record — they beat Brooklyn Monday night and sit at three and four — and Pfizer and AMD both report today, Pfizer this morning, AMD after the close.

Here's the lead. Just after the market closed Monday, multiple outlets reported that American forces hit Iranian targets near the Strait of Hormuz, apparently on Qeshm Island, after Iran's Revolutionary Guard fired on a U.S. aircraft. Nobody announced this publicly — it came out through reporting, not a Pentagon statement. And the timing is really the story: the president had told voters for weeks he wouldn't strike Iran before the midterms, insisting it wasn't really a war because there'd been no sustained fighting in months. At the same time, he posted that a deal with Tehran could happen soon. So you've got a defensive strike and talk of an imminent settlement landing in the news cycle together, hours before polls opened.

The market had already bet on de-escalation, and this strike cuts right across that bet. Oil fell hard Monday — Brent's worst one-day drop in more than two months — on reporting that Qatar and Pakistan were brokering a framework where Iran stops hitting shipping and the U.S. lifts its naval blockade. That relief rally now has to digest Monday night's strikes, and honestly the early numbers are all over the place — some quotes have oil back around where it settled Monday, others several dollars higher — so take any single number you see this morning with a grain of salt. The mechanism that matters for you: that Hormuz risk premium traders were starting to price out on Monday touches everything downstream, jet fuel, diesel, the gas price string that's been falling for weeks — and any re-escalation puts that tail risk right back on the table. And there's a second-order piece: Israel's prime minister is reportedly pushing Washington for a bigger air campaign once the election's over, and the president's said he's in no rush to decide — so last night's strike-then-settle signal might be a floor under what's coming, not a ceiling.

And then there's the election itself. Polls start closing at six Eastern tonight and run through early tomorrow morning in Alaska. Georgia, Virginia and South Carolina should give you the first real signal within an hour or two. But Pennsylvania, Michigan and Wisconsin — three of the most contested battlegrounds this cycle — are flagged as likely to take longer, maybe past tonight entirely. Democrats go in with a mid-to-high single digit lead on the generic ballot by this brief's last clean reading, and the president's approval is still historically weak for this point in a midterm, though it's ticked up slightly off its low. North Carolina and Georgia's Senate races are the top Democratic targets. Republicans currently hold the Senate fifty-three to forty-seven and the House by a handful of seats.

Here's the read: an administration that just authorized a strike it promised to hold off on, hours before voters who've watched gas prices move with this Hormuz crisis for months head to the polls — that's going to be argued about on Wednesday no matter how the vote count goes. Did it leak because it was meant to project strength, or because it just couldn't be contained until the polls closed? For you as an investor, the thing that actually matters tonight isn't the exact House margin — it's whether divided government brings back 2027 fiscal brinkmanship as the default posture, and whether this strike-then-settle pattern holds, or gives way to more targets the president's hinted at, including a suspected nuclear site. Watch the polls starting at six, watch for any Pentagon confirmation of the strikes, watch Iran's response, and watch where oil actually settles today.

A few other things worth knowing. On the election mechanics: prediction markets had Democrats heavily favored for the House, something like ninety to ninety-two percent, with the Senate much closer to a coin flip — though those numbers are a few weeks stale as of this morning. There's also that fake-polling scandal from a couple weeks back, Wedgewood Polls, which admitted to fabricating data — that's still an open wound on both sides' trust in the numbers.

On the economy: September's JOLTS job openings number comes out at ten this morning, right alongside the election, and it carries extra weight because recent jobs data's been thin. Friday brings the actual October jobs report. There's no Fed meeting this month — they held rates steady late last month and don't meet again until December ninth. CPI follows in a week, PPI the week after.

On earnings: Apple had a really strong quarter — record revenue, up twelve percent, beating their own guidance, with the first earnings call under new CEO John Ternus, services at an all-time high and the iPhone posting its best-ever September quarter. Pfizer reports this morning and AMD tonight after the close, but neither had actually printed as of this recording, so no numbers to share yet. Overnight in Asia, chip-heavy markets in Korea and Taiwan sold off hard, three to four percent, which reads like nerves ahead of AMD's print and still some hangover from Intel's historic crash last week — though it wasn't universal, Hong Kong and Australia both traded up the same session, so don't read it as one clean story.

One more thing on Hormuz, since the numbers moved more than usual overnight: the tracker that watches dark-tanker traffic — ships that go quiet on their transponders — jumped to about seventy percent of screened vessels in the last day, way up from the low forties on Sunday. That could be ships genuinely going dark to dodge the strikes, or it could just be ambient noise in the tracking, the kind of jump this brief has seen before that didn't hold. One data point, not a trend yet. The broader crisis indices were basically flat — still reading extreme on pressure, elevated on escalation risk, same as yesterday.

Sports. The Yankees' season is over — they're still waiting on a decision about whether to make their closer, David Bednar, a qualifying offer. The Knicks beat Brooklyn Monday night, a big bench game from Landry Shamet, twenty-two points, and they pulled away with a twenty-five to five run in the fourth quarter — that puts them at three and four, finally a clean record after weeks of conflicting reports. And Northwestern lost at Oregon Saturday in the two programs' first-ever meeting, thirty-four to fourteen; their next game isn't confirmed yet.
