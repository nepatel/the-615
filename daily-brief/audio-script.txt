The 6:15, for Tuesday, September 29th. Here's what moved overnight.

The Iran story just turned into a dispute over the actual facts on the table. Over the weekend, the US ambassador to the UN, Mike Waltz, gave the first detailed American account of what Trump rejected from Tehran: he says Iran wanted sanctions relief and access to billions in frozen assets up front, before any nuclear talks even started. Iran's foreign minister, Araghchi, says that's wrong — that Iran made no such demand and that Waltz never saw the real proposal. Both sides, weirdly, seem to agree roughly on the destination — some sanctions relief in exchange for nuclear progress — they're just fighting over who moves first. And here's the thing: Qatari mediators met with both sides yesterday, and Araghchi says he's expecting an official American response, quote, "hopefully," by today. That's actually today, while you're listening to this on the train.

Quickly, the board: oil's messy and unreconciled this morning — Brent's easing back toward the low one-oh-fives after Monday's spike, while WTI's actually pushing higher, up over two percent to the mid-nineties, so treat that as noise for now. The ten-year's basically flat around five and a quarter percent, a two-thousand-seven high; the thirty-year pushed to a fresh two-thousand-four high, above five and a half percent. The dollar's near a two-month high. Gold's down almost three percent, a seven-week low. Equity futures are soft, but tech's actually up on chip strength — another split market. Hormuz transit counts are still frozen at basically nothing versus normal, the tanker count is still bouncing around in that noisy range from the past couple weeks, and war-risk insurance is still running around ten percent of a ship's value. On sports: the Yankees host the Red Sox tonight at eight for game one of the wild card series; the Knicks opened training camp with no new moves; Northwestern hosts Penn State Friday night at the new Ryan Field.

So, back to the lead, because today really is the hinge point. Waltz's framing on Sunday shows was pretty blunt — he called Iran's offer, quote, "a pretty cynical attempt to put something on the table they knew was unacceptable." But separately, reporting out of Axios and CNN says Trump himself is privately willing to give Iran sanctions relief and release frozen funds — just not until Iran shows concrete nuclear progress first. So the US position isn't actually "no," it's "not yet, and not in that order." Araghchi, for his part, insists Iran's position hasn't changed at all, and that the deal on the table already involves opening the Strait if the US releases frozen assets and allows oil sales. When you strip away the public theater, both governments seem to be describing something close to the same trade. What's actually being negotiated in public right now is trust, and sequencing — not substance.

None of this has shown up yet in the physical numbers — no new transits, no confirmed change in the tanker count. What has moved is price, and it's moved inconsistently, which tells you the market doesn't have real conviction yet about which way today breaks. That matters because the Fed is already pricing in something like a two in three chance of another rate hike at its late-October meeting, on top of the hike it already did in September, and tomorrow morning brings the first real inflation read since that hike. So you've got three things landing inside about forty-eight hours: today's Iran response, this morning's jobs and confidence data, and tomorrow's inflation print — each one capable of changing how the market reads the other two. The thing to actually watch today isn't whether the headline is "yes" or "no" — it's whether Washington's response engages with the sequencing question at all. If it does, that's a real crack in the logjam. If it doesn't, this stays exactly where it's been for two weeks, just with angrier rhetoric.

On politics: the US and China's trade truce turns out to be bigger than first reported — it's a thirty-for-thirty framework, thirty billion dollars of tariff cuts on each side, sixty billion combined, running through January tenth. There's also a new channel for the two countries to talk to each other about AI incidents, with a follow-up meeting expected in late November, though nobody's said yet what's actually on that agenda. Worth noting for later — that trade deal is mostly toys and holiday goods, not the footwear and apparel that actually matters for Nike, so don't expect it to show up in Nike's numbers Thursday.

On the economic data: this morning brings home prices, consumer confidence, and job openings, but honestly none of it is the main event — that's tomorrow's inflation print, the first one since the Fed's September hike. Consumer confidence missed expectations last month, which would normally argue for the Fed easing up, except this hiking cycle is being driven by oil prices, not the labor market, so a soft jobs number today doesn't necessarily save anyone from a hike in late October.

Speaking of which — Nike reports earnings Thursday after the close. The Street's now solidly around forty-four cents a share on eleven and a third billion in revenue. The number to actually watch is the tariff line — Nike's already guided to roughly a billion dollars of tariff costs this year and raised its margin hit once already; if they raise it again Thursday, that's the real story, not the headline beat or miss.

In tech: Nvidia's Jensen Huang rolled out a new AI agent safety platform Sunday night — basically a sandbox-and-monitor system so companies can rein in AI agents that go off script, running right on Nvidia's own networking chips. Anthropic, Arm, Microsoft, Oracle and SpaceX are all backing it. OpenAI notably isn't — worth watching whether that's a snub or just a "we'll wait and see." And SpaceX's Starship finally made it to orbit for the first time yesterday, dropping off twenty-six Starlink satellites — genuinely the rocket's first money-making flight — before coming home early after just one lap around the planet. Nobody's said exactly why it cut the mission short yet.

Overseas: a rough night in Ukraine — two dozen towns in the Sumy region hit with bombs and drones, a market strike in Kyiv killed two people, and Ukraine's air defense knocked down more than a hundred drones and several ballistic missiles just since yesterday evening, plus a fire from strikes on Odesa's infrastructure overnight. On the Saudi side, nothing new to report since Saturday's intercepted Houthi strike — that front's gone quiet for now.

And in the Gulf — a detail that hadn't been reported before: a cargo ship called the Cape Dao got hit by two torpedoes off Oman's coast last week, killing a twenty-six-year-old Indian crew member and forcing the rest of the crew to evacuate. Nobody's claimed it — not Iran, not the US. Combine that with the previously-undisclosed strike that injured eight US Marines two weeks ago, and a disputed claim that Iran seized a second American underwater drone, and you get a pattern: there's a quieter, less-disclosed shadow war running underneath all the public diplomacy, and it isn't slowing down just because the talking is speeding up.
