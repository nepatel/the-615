Now the lead. Two weeks ago, a polling firm calling itself Wedgewood Polls admitted its numbers were almost entirely fabricated, after an aggregator called FiftyPlusOne noticed the data looked statistically off and confronted them directly. Wedgewood basically said the whole thing was an experiment, to see how easily a made-up polling shop could get picked up by the press and by campaign trackers. And it worked, for a while — the New York Times had folded more than a dozen of their polls into its own tracker, and pulled every single one once the fabrication came out. This is actually the second time this has happened this cycle; a different fake outfit ran basically the same stunt earlier in the year, on primary-season polling. A Michigan state senator has said the fake numbers actively hurt her own campaign, and a former Senate candidate has made a similar claim about her race — though neither of those is independently confirmed, so treat them as allegations, not established fact.

Here's the thing, though — none of that changes what the real, surviving polling actually says. The genuine aggregate, as of late October, still has Democrats up on the generic ballot by something like eight or nine points, and a separate Reuters poll put the gap even wider, around nine points among likely voters. Trump's approval is sitting at a record low for his term — call it mid-thirties approve, low sixties disapprove, depending on the pollster. So the real numbers didn't move. What moved is confidence in the numbers themselves, right before an election where voters and markets alike are going to want to trust them.

Early voting backs up that this is a genuinely engaged electorate, scandal aside: more than thirty million ballots already cast nationally, running ahead of both the last two midterm cycles at this same point. But it's lumpy, not uniform — Texas is running something like three-quarters ahead of its twenty-twenty-two pace, Ohio's up solidly too, while Iowa is actually down a touch. So read it as energized in specific places, not as one even national wave.

And here's the read on all of it: the gap between the model-based forecasts and the betting-market odds still hasn't closed in the final stretch. The models give Democrats a very strong shot at the House and a solid edge in the Senate; the prediction markets are more cautious, especially on the Senate, where they've got it close to a coin flip. That's a real gap, not noise, and it's been sitting there for most of the past week without converging, which tells you the market still isn't convinced the polling-based forecasts are right. The fake-polling scandal gives both sides a fresh reason to distrust the other's inputs right as the election happens, and markets generally hate pricing a close binary outcome under that kind of uncertainty.

For this reader, the thing to actually watch Tuesday isn't just who wins — it's what combination of outcomes you get. A Democratic House, regardless of the Senate, brings shutdown and debt-ceiling brinkmanship back as the default negotiating posture heading into next year. A Senate flip specifically slows the pace of Fed and judicial confirmations the administration's been pushing through on narrow margins. And remember, the Fed itself has already tried to stay out of this — when the Fed held rates steady at its meeting six days before the election, the chair went out of his way to say, on the record, that politics had nothing to do with the timing. Whether that holds up as credible through Tuesday and into December's meeting is itself something investors are going to be watching, on top of the election outcome itself. Watch Tuesday night's results, watch whether any more fabricated polls get clawed back from trackers before voters even go to the polls, and watch Monday's market open for the first hint of pre-election positioning.
