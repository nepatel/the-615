The 6:15, for Monday, October the nineteenth. Here's what moved overnight: Riyadh's main airport finally reopened over the weekend after more than a week shut down by Houthi missile strikes, but the bigger story for your morning is that the oil shock from this war just showed up directly in the inflation numbers.

Quick lap around the board, all rounded: Brent's up around a hundred and nine dollars, WTI's near ninety-eight, and that spread has narrowed; the ten-year's a little above five percent; the dollar index is basically flat; S&P futures are up about half a percent and Nasdaq futures are up over one percent; the VIX dropped sharply overnight, though that's a single-source read we haven't been able to double-check; gold's near forty-one eighty; gas at the pump is running about four twenty-nine a gallon nationally; Asia closed mixed to higher, Europe's opening lower; the Strait of Hormuz is still being called closed to commercial shipping by the insurance-and-risk trackers, with fresh, unconfirmed Iranian mine claims on tankers overnight; the Yankees' season is long over; the Knicks tip off their title defense tomorrow night; and Northwestern won on the road Friday to go five and one.

Now the lead. King Khalid International Airport in Riyadh reopened this past Saturday, nine days after the missile barrage earlier this month that killed at least a dozen people and wounded around three hundred. But international flights haven't bounced back — Lufthansa, KLM, Air France, and a handful of Gulf and Asian carriers are still keeping their cancellations in place. And the attacks haven't stopped either: Saudi authorities say a Houthi projectile hit a residential neighborhood in Najran on Sunday, injuring six people, while the coalition says it intercepted a couple of drones near Riyadh and the Eastern Province. The Gulf Cooperation Council put out a statement Sunday backing Saudi Arabia and framing an attack on any one of its six members as an attack on all of them. Egypt said it would help too, though Egypt's own parliament actually put off the vote that would authorize that support. So the promised "firm" Saudi response still hasn't arrived, and regional officials keep describing Saudi air-defense interceptor stocks as stretched thin.

That's the security side. Here's where it gets interesting for your portfolio: September's CPI report, which wasn't out yet when we went to press last Wednesday, came in hot — prices up six tenths of a percent on the month, three point seven percent year over year, with core inflation at two point six percent. And look at what drove it: energy prices jumped five percent on the month and better than twenty percent over the year, gasoline alone up over nine percent on the month and better than a third over the year. That is a straight line from the Houthi-driven oil spike into the number the Fed actually watches. And then the very next day, producer prices came in even hotter than expected, retail sales beat too — largely on gas-station receipts — and jobless claims were right in line, so there's no labor-market weakness offsetting any of this.

Here's the read: two hot inflation prints in a row, both substantially energy-driven, land just over a week before the Fed's next meeting, and they don't change anything — betting markets still have the odds of an October rate cut sitting in the high teens to twenties, which lines up with a Fed that already leaned hawkish back in June. What started as a shipping and insurance story in the strait has turned into a direct input into US inflation, and that's tightening the Fed's room to maneuver on anything else that goes wrong between now and year-end. Meanwhile the tape doesn't fully agree with itself this morning — US equity futures are constructive and that volatility gauge is down sharply, even as European stocks are opening lower across the board. And worth remembering: the president said a couple weeks back that prices would come tumbling down once this war wound down. Instead prices went up, and the war hasn't wound down at all.

On the economy: the claims and consensus detail beyond what's in the lead — claims in at a hundred and ninety-nine thousand, right on consensus, continuing claims around one point seven million. We're also carrying, for the first time, some color on that June Fed vote: reportedly nine to three to hold rates, with all three dissenters wanting to go up, not down — that's a single analyst's characterization, not the Fed's own transcript, so take it with a grain of salt. Put together: hot CPI, hotter producer prices, steady claims, strong retail sales — that's an economy still running warm even as energy costs climb.

On geopolitics: beyond that Gulf Cooperation Council statement, Houthi media is claiming a Saudi airstrike killed nine people in a Yemeni town called Saada — that's their claim alone, nobody's independently confirmed it. Egypt's parliament punting its vote, rather than voting it down or passing it, leaves open just how far Saudi Arabia's friends in the region are actually willing to go. And OPEC-plus's meeting on November first just got more important — they've held output flat two months running, and the December decision is genuinely live now, especially with Brent sitting near a hundred and nine dollars.

On Iran and the strait: two separate mine claims from Iran overnight, neither one independently confirmed by a major wire. The maritime monitors say they're increasingly struggling to even track these incidents because targeted ships have mostly stopped reporting in. On diplomacy, the president says a decision on Iran is coming very soon, without saying which way, while still ruling out nuclear weapons or ground troops. Iran's foreign ministry is denying it directs the Houthis at all and says it's willing to mediate. None of that changes the standing pledge to hold off on any new US strikes until after the November third midterms.
