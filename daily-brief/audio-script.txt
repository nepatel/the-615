The 6:15, for Saturday, October the seventeenth. Here's what moved overnight: a trio of hot inflation reports collided with another leg up in oil prices this week, and together they've put a Federal Reserve interest rate hike back on the table for later this month — a very different story than the rate-cut talk markets were running on over the summer.

Quick numbers before we get into it. Brent crude jumped about four percent on Friday to just under one hundred ten dollars a barrel, continuing a climb tied to the Saudi-Houthi war and attacks near the Strait of Hormuz. The ten-year Treasury yield is up around five point one percent, way up from the mid-four percent range back in August. Stocks had a rough week — the S&P down about two percent, the Nasdaq down more than four and a half percent, hit hardest in chip and AI infrastructure names, while the Dow actually eked out a small gain, cushioned by energy stocks. Gas is running about four dollars thirty cents a gallon nationally. Looking ahead, Tuesday brings Netflix and Tesla earnings after the close, plus the NBA season opener, with the Knicks hosting Philadelphia at the Garden. And the big one: the Fed's October twenty-seventh and twenty-eighth meeting, where a hike is now a live possibility.

Here's the lead story. September's inflation data came out this week, and all three major reports ran hot. Consumer prices, out Wednesday, rose three point seven percent from a year earlier, with the monthly gain at six-tenths of a percent — both above expectations, and both up from August's three point four percent. Core inflation, which strips out food and energy, came in at two point six percent year-over-year. Then Thursday got worse: producer prices, the wholesale measure, jumped one point three percent for the month and six point three percent from a year ago — way above the roughly half-a-percent economists expected. Retail sales also beat, up eight-tenths of a percent for September, though if you strip out cars and gas, the underlying number was basically flat — not something you can call statistically significant. And weekly jobless claims stayed low, around a hundred ninety-nine thousand, so the labor market isn't showing any cracks. In other words: this isn't a weak economy overheating. It's prices running hot while hiring stays calm.

Why does this matter so much? Because of where that heat is coming from. The producer price jump looks like it's being driven by energy and trade costs — which makes sense, because oil has been spiking for weeks now on the back of the Hormuz shipping crisis and the Saudi-Houthi air war. That's a supply shock working its way through the pipeline, and it's got further to run into next month's numbers. Meanwhile, the soft retail sales core reading suggests consumers aren't actually spending more once you account for gas and vehicles — so you've got a split picture: hot prices, but not necessarily a hot underlying economy.

And this is landing on a Fed that is already in a hiking posture, not a cutting one. They raised rates a quarter point back on September sixteenth, to a range of three and three-quarters to four percent, and had already penciled in one more hike by year end. After this week's data, the odds of an October hike spiked as high as almost fifty percent during the day, before settling back down to somewhere in the fifteen to seventeen percent range after Fed Chair Kevin Warsh spoke Thursday in Bangkok. He wouldn't commit either way — he said, quote, "I don't prejudge meetings," and talked about a synchronized global tightening cycle and warned that the AI buildout's economic transition could get, quote, "very bumpy." Goldman Sachs's economists read that as the Fed pausing in October, then delivering one more hike in December.

Markets did not like any of this. Wednesday's inflation report alone knocked the S&P down over one percent and the Nasdaq down almost two percent. By Friday's close, the S&P stood at around seventy-six fifty, the Nasdaq composite near twenty-six thousand one forty, and the week's losses were concentrated hard in chip and AI infrastructure stocks — CoreWeave and Micron among the worst hit — while the Dow actually finished the week higher, helped by the energy rally. And that ten-year yield I mentioned, pushing past five percent, is doing exactly what you'd expect from a hiking Fed facing sticky inflation — not what you'd expect from a central bank getting ready to cut.

What to watch: next Thursday's jobless claims number, which if it stays low keeps the case for a December hike alive no matter what the Fed does later this month. And whether the Hormuz and Saudi fighting gets worse before the Fed meets — because that's the engine behind this month's energy-driven inflation, and it's not something the Fed can do anything about.

On politics: Trump is pushing Saudi Arabia to accept Israeli help against the Houthis, and there's talk he thinks a Saudi-Israel normalization deal could be getting closer, even though Riyadh is still insisting on Palestinian statehood as a precondition. That's on top of his earlier comments about possibly joining the Saudi fight directly. None of it touches his pledge to hold off on new Iran strikes until after the midterms on November third.

On the economy, beyond what I covered in the lead: that retail sales control group — the cleaner read on underlying consumer demand — was up just two-tenths of a percent, not really distinguishable from flat. August's retail sales got revised up to one point three percent. And claims are still historically low. So again: this is a prices problem, not a growth problem.

On business: bank earnings were a mixed bag this week. Morgan Stanley beat big, with revenue over twenty billion and earnings well above estimates, driven by a huge jump in equities trading. BlackRock beat too, with record assets under management near fifteen and a half trillion dollars. Bank of America missed, though — net income fell to six point nine billion from eight point three billion a year ago, after taking a one point two billion dollar regulatory settlement charge.

On technology: this week's selloff was concentrated in chips and AI infrastructure names, with worries about Chinese semiconductor competition and how much of the AI buildout is being financed with debt. That's really the same story as the Fed — expensive, long-duration growth stocks are the most exposed when rate-hike risk comes back.

On geopolitics: Riyadh's main airport remains closed after this month's deadly Houthi strikes, with no confirmed reopening as of this morning. Saudi Arabia is investigating its own air defense failures and has promised a firm response. And separately, the fragile Russia-Ukraine energy truce that Trump announced last weekend already looks shaky — Ukraine struck a major Russian refinery after accusing Moscow of continuing to hit its power grid.

And on Iran and Hormuz: the latest Houthi strikes actually hit Yemen's own government in Aden, not Saudi Arabia — a reminder they're fighting on two fronts. There's also an unconfirmed report of a tanker hit near the Saudi coast, and a separate, unverified Iranian claim about a tanker collision that we're not able to confirm independently.

Quick sports update. The Yankees' season is over — they're in the offseason with no free agent moves to report. The Knicks wrapped up their preseason and open the regular season, and their NBA Cup title defense, Tuesday night at home against Philadelphia. And Northwestern, ranked twenty-fifth and sitting at four and one, is on the road at Michigan State today — no result yet as of this recording.
