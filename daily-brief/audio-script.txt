The 6:15, for Saturday, October 24th. Here's what moved overnight: oil closed at the highest level of this whole war, and Wall Street sold off hard into the weekend, as a brand new, still-unverified Iranian claim about mined tankers landed on top of Thursday's fight over whether Iran is faking attacks altogether.

Quick scan of the board: it's the weekend, so markets are closed until Monday, but Friday's close was ugly — Brent crude near one seventeen a barrel, WTI near one oh three, both up sharply; the Dow down about six hundred fifty points, a little over one percent, the S&P down about half a percent, the Nasdaq down a bit less; the VIX, the fear gauge, up over two percent and creeping toward twenty; gold actually fell again, which still doesn't fit the usual panic story; Treasury yields crept back up across the board after Thursday's flight to safety; gas prices ticked up for a third straight day; the Fed meets Tuesday and Wednesday with cut odds still thin, around a fifth to a quarter; OPEC-plus meets a week from Sunday; and the midterms are now just over a week away. In sports: the Yankees' season has been over since early October; the Knicks lost their second straight to open the year, falling in Boston; Northwestern, ranked twenty-fourth, hosts Rutgers at noon; and the Brewers took Game One of the World Series from the Rays, three to two, so they lead the series one game to nothing, with Game Two tonight in Milwaukee.

Now the lead. Friday's calm did not hold. Brent crude settled at a hundred sixteen dollars and seventy-two cents a barrel, up a little over three percent — that is the highest closing price of the entire seven-month war, and two separate price feeds agree on that number, which matters, because this brief has spent weeks flagging how much those feeds disagree. WTI settled around a hundred three and a quarter, also up sharply. The gap between the two benchmarks blew out to roughly thirteen and a half dollars, the widest this brief has ever tracked. That's a reversal from Friday morning, when oil had actually eased back a bit from Thursday's spike — by the close, that calm was gone.

Stocks followed oil down for the whole session, not just the open. The Dow lost about six hundred fifty points, a little over one percent, to land just above fifty-two thousand two hundred. The S&P fell about half a percent, the Nasdaq a more modest two-tenths of a percent — its second straight losing week. The VIX jumped over two percent to just under twenty, putting it on track for its first weekly close above twenty in a while. That's notably worse than what Friday morning's futures had implied — the selling got worse as the day went on, it didn't ease up.

Bonds told a different story. The two, ten, and thirty-year yields all climbed back up by about four basis points into Friday's close, clawing back part of Thursday's drop — the drop this brief flagged as odd, since it came on a stronger-than-expected jobs report, the kind of data that usually pushes yields up, not down. So the safety trade from Thursday was already fading in the bond market before any new news even hit Friday night.

And that new news: Iran's Revolutionary Guard navy says two supertankers were struck and disabled by mines overnight in what it calls its mined corridor. Nobody — no maritime authority, no flag state, no independent monitor — has confirmed this. It lands directly on top of Thursday's bigger claim, the seven-tanker claim that President Trump called fabricated outright, accusing Iran of making up hits to drive oil prices higher ahead of the midterms. That dispute hasn't moved an inch since yesterday — nobody's corroborated it, nobody's walked it back, and neither Tehran nor Washington has said anything new to settle it either way. Meanwhile, the tracker this brief watches for tankers going dark on ship-tracking systems jumped to seventy-two percent of screened vessels in the last twenty-four hours, way up from Friday's fifty-four percent and well above the usual seven-day run rate — but even the tracker itself cautions that a lot of that jump could just be ordinary signal loss, not boats deliberately hiding, so don't treat it as confirmed evasion just yet.

So here's where it sits: if Iran's claims are basically true, Friday's market move — the new oil high, the deeper stock selloff, yields drifting back up — was the market correctly pricing in a real escalation. If they're basically fabricated, like the president says, then Friday's close may have priced in a story that doesn't hold up, and some of Friday's move could unwind just as fast as it built. One thing argues against a clean fear trade either way: gold actually fell again, down about one and a quarter percent, even as everything else leaned toward risk-off. That's now happened twice in a row, and it still doesn't add up cleanly — normally gold is the thing that goes up when everyone else is nervous. Worth watching into next week: does anyone independently confirm or debunk the new mine claim; does the tanker dark-rate stay elevated or snap back toward normal; does the ship-transit count finally update past its six-day-old reading; and does Monday's open in Asia extend Friday's selloff or reverse it, the way Friday morning briefly reversed Thursday's panic before the afternoon undid that.

On Iran and the strait more broadly: the ship-transit count tracked by the IMF stayed stuck at just four vessels a day, against a normal baseline of eighty-five — that reading is now six days old without an update, the longest stretch of staleness this brief has seen. The war-risk insurance premium, still running around thirty-two times pre-crisis levels, is now over two weeks old as an estimate, so treat that as background color, not a live price. The crisis-tracking indices were little changed — overall pressure stayed at an extreme reading, flat on the day, while the escalation outlook actually ticked down slightly, its first dip after two straight increases. Betting markets on a return to normal shipping were unchanged: still under one percent by the end of this month, six percent by the end of November, twelve percent by the end of December — still pricing a long crisis no matter what either side claimed this week. And diplomatically, nothing has moved — Iran's foreign minister's offer to reopen the strait is still just sitting there, unanswered.

Sports, quickly: Yankees, season's been over since early October. Knicks dropped to zero and two after losing in Boston Friday night, Jayson Tatum scoring thirty-six. Northwestern, ranked twenty-fourth, hosts Rutgers at noon today. And the Brewers beat the Rays three to two in Game One of the World Series, so Milwaukee leads the series one game to none, with Game Two tonight back in Milwaukee.
