The 6:15, for Wednesday, September 30th. Here's what moved overnight: Trump rejected Iran's Hormuz plan on camera, calling it unacceptable — but oil sold off anyway, dropping roughly nine percent from Tuesday's levels even though nothing physical in the Strait actually changed.

Quick tour of the board: Brent's trading around ninety-seven, WTI near ninety, both down sharply overnight; the ten-year's sitting just above five and a quarter percent, near its highest since 2007; the thirty-year hit a fresh high going back to 2002; the dollar's a touch softer; S&P futures are basically flat to slightly higher; the VIX ticked up to sixteen; gold's bouncing back toward forty-two hundred; Asia closed higher, Europe's mixed this morning; gas prices actually came down a little overnight; and the Hormuz counters — vessel transits, tanker holdings, war-risk insurance — are all essentially unchanged from where they've been, still near zero traffic through the Strait. On the sports side, the Yankees lost game one of their wild-card series to the Red Sox last night.

Here's the lead. Trump gave his rejection of Iran's seven-day Hormuz plan straight to the cameras Monday: quote, I'd like to make a deal too, but that deal would not be acceptable, end quote. That's a harder line than what we heard from Waltz over the weekend, and it landed right as Iran's foreign minister, Araghchi, was expecting an official written response through Qatari mediators — a response that still hasn't come. As of Tuesday evening, Araghchi said he was still waiting on that formal answer, and Iran's security chief said Trump is, quote, trapped in a quagmire where he can neither negotiate nor fight. So both sides are talking tough in public while the actual diplomatic channel stays behind closed doors in Doha.

And here's what's strange: oil did not treat this as an escalation. Brent and WTI are both down eight, nine percent from where this show had them Tuesday, even though nothing in the physical picture changed — transit counts are still near zero, the tanker-holding count is unchanged, war-risk insurance premiums are unchanged. So the market is unwinding risk it had already priced in, not reacting to new information. Two ways to read that: either traders think Trump's tough talk is negotiating posture rather than a real policy shift — which would line up with reports that he's privately willing to grant sanctions relief once Iran shows concrete progress — or this drop has nothing to do with Iran at all, and crude had just run too far ahead of the actual disruption and is now correcting.

What didn't move with oil: the long end of the bond market. The thirty-year closed Tuesday at its highest yield since 2002, and the ten-year's holding in a band it's occupied since mid-September. If falling energy prices were really driving inflation expectations down, you'd expect bonds to rally along with oil. They're not, which suggests what's really going on with long rates is about Treasury supply and term premium — how much the market wants to be paid to hold long-duration debt — rather than about energy costs. October alone brings a big slate of new seven- and ten-year Treasury supply that the market has to absorb no matter what crude does. Meanwhile stocks are treating cheaper oil as good news — futures are green, gold's bouncing — while the VIX ticked up a little, which sits a bit oddly next to an otherwise calm tape.

All of this lands just hours before the August inflation report — the Fed's preferred gauge — due at eight-thirty this morning, the last one before the Fed's next meeting in late October, where prediction markets still put the odds of another rate hike at around two in three. Consensus going in is for a slightly cooler core reading than July's. A hot number against falling oil prices would be the most hawkish combination on the table — it would mean whatever disinflation the oil market thinks it's seeing isn't showing up in the actual data yet.

On politics: China's pause on rare-earth export controls is set to expire November tenth — five weeks before the broader trade truce with the US needs renewing in January. Beijing's shown little sign of loosening the underlying licensing regime in the meantime, and added two more American companies to its restricted list back in June. So there's a rare-earth-specific flashpoint on the calendar well before the bigger trade truce comes up again — a sequencing problem that echoes the one stuck between the US and Iran.

On the economy: that Fed rate hike two weeks ago that took rates to a range of three-and-three-quarters to four percent passed unanimously, and the Fed's own projections show most members expecting one more hike before year-end. The Fed chair's tone since then has stayed hawkish. That's the backdrop this morning's inflation number lands against.

On business: corporate bond markets are not showing any stress at all — investment-grade spreads are about as tight as they've been since the late nineties, and even high-yield spreads are historically tight outside of the lowest-rated credits. So you've got a thirty-year Treasury yield at a two-decade-plus high, but corporate borrowers aren't paying up for it — which tells you this rate move is about government bond supply, not about companies getting riskier. Nike and Micron both report earnings this week and will be an early test of whether that calm in credit markets matches what's actually happening in company results.

On technology: Micron reports today after the close, and Wall Street's expecting roughly thirty-one dollars a share on about fifty-one billion in revenue — a huge jump from a year ago, driven by AI-related memory chip demand. That's the cleanest read this week on whether all the AI spending everyone's been talking about is actually showing up as real chip demand.

On geopolitics: Ukraine's president says Russia launched nearly five hundred attack drones and more than forty missiles overnight in a barrage lasting over twelve hours, hitting Kyiv, and Zaporizhzhia, Khmelnytskyi, Sumy, and Odesa regions — a bread factory and a tire plant among the sites hit. That's a bigger barrage than what we reported here yesterday, though some of the reporting on the exact timing overlaps with what we already told you, so treat the fine details as still being sorted out. Zelenskyy's again calling on the US, Europe, and the G7 and G20 to respond.

And a bit more on Iran: separately from the diplomatic story, the economic squeeze on Iran keeps tightening. Iranian oil loadings in August were down more than eighty percent from a year ago because of the naval blockade, and Iran's own oil company may have to cut production further if the blockade holds. Floating storage of Iranian crude has fallen by two-thirds since mid-July, and dozens of sanctioned tankers are just sitting idle, unable to get back to port. That's real pressure building on Tehran the longer this standoff drags on — which cuts against the idea that Iran can just wait this out for free.

Sports, quickly: the Yankees dropped game one of the wild-card series to Boston, three to one, at Yankee Stadium — Boston's Garrett Crochet was dominant, and a two-run pinch hit put it away. The Knicks are in training camp, no roster moves to report, preseason opens at Philadelphia next Monday. And Northwestern is two and one, coming off a loss at Indiana, hosting Penn State Friday night in the season debut of the rebuilt Ryan Field.
