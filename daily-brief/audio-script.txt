The six fifteen, for Tuesday, August twenty fifth. Here's what moved overnight: Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent finally rolled out the Iran sanctions package he'd been teasing for days as an "economic D-Day" — and markets promptly read it as a lot less scary than the buildup suggested, because he didn't actually put a deadline on it.

Quick lay of the land before we get into it. Oil eased back after last week's run-up — Brent settled just above ninety two Monday, WTI in the mid eighties — while the ten year sits around four and seven tenths percent, the dollar's basically flat, stock futures are a touch higher this morning, gold's holding near a three month high, and gas at the pump is right around four dollars and ten cents a gallon nationally. In the strait, we're on day one seventy seven of the closure — transit counts from every tracker we watch are now more than a week stale, there's a fresh unclaimed strike on a tanker off Oman overnight, and Iran's still holding that seized ship near Qeshm Island. On the transactions side: the Yankees made no new moves, Josh Hart's extension with the Knicks is still unsigned, and Northwestern confirmed its season opener will be played at a temporary stadium while Ryan Field finishes construction.

Okay, the lead. Bessent stood up Monday and formally announced what the administration's calling "Operation Economic Outcast" — the sanctions campaign he'd branded economic D-Day. The mechanics: Treasury hit close to sixty entities and vessels directly — people and ships tied to Iran's nuclear and missile programs, cyber operations, oil smuggling — and then, separately, it widened the net for what's called secondary sanctions. That's the tool where Washington penalizes third countries for continuing to do business with Iran. They added five new categories to that net: digital assets, tech, gold, aviation, and shipping. Bessent's own words were that the goal is to, quote, sever every economic lifeline that keeps this regime standing until Iran stands alone.

Here's the thing that actually mattered to markets, though: what Bessent didn't do. He would not put a deadline on any of it. The countries most exposed are reportedly China, India, Turkey, Iraq, and the UAE — and the UAE, of all of them, had already cut its own economic ties to Iran more than a week earlier after those missile strikes on its territory. Bessent's language was, quote, I'm not going to set a timeline, but we do not have infinite patience here — he called it a warning shot, said everyone's getting a chance to remedy bad behavior. And that's the read: a campaign that had been built up for weeks as a knockout blow arrived instead as basically a notice period, with no enforcement clock actually running.

Oil confirms that read perfectly. Brent had been climbing all last week on anticipation of this announcement, and then gave it back Monday — down about two and a half percent to settle just above ninety two. WTI fell a similar amount. That's a classic sell the news pattern — the market had already priced this as a big deal, and then the actual announcement came out softer than expected. Stocks shrugged it off too, for the same reason — the Dow was actually up a bit Monday. The bigger story for equities wasn't Iran at all, it was chip stocks selling off ahead of Nvidia's earnings Wednesday, which knocked the Nasdaq down the better part of a percent.

Tehran's response was pure theater, consistent with everything they've said for weeks — their finance minister said they've got a two year plan and they're fully prepared, echoing the same lines their foreign minister's been running since last week about how U.S. pressure campaigns always fail.

But here's the part worth actually sitting with: the list of countries named. China is Iran's biggest trading partner and its biggest oil customer. Naming Beijing alongside India, Turkey, and Iraq as sanctions targets — even without a deadline — sets up a fight Washington can't easily win against any of those four without risking a separate trade war, and that's on top of a reciprocal tariff dispute that's now opening up with Canada too. And having the UAE on that same at-risk list, when they'd already zeroed out their own Iran trade voluntarily, tells you something: secondary sanctions is a blunt instrument here. The actual pressure on Iran right now isn't coming from Washington's paperwork — it's coming from the seizures and strikes happening in the strait itself, which kept happening all weekend regardless of anything Bessent said Monday.

Quick hits around the rest of the board. On politics: Lisa Cook's deadline to respond to Trump's latest attempt to remove her from the Fed lands tomorrow, Wednesday. Worth noting this runs on a totally separate track from the Supreme Court's own review of Trump's removal authority, which won't get argued until January — so tomorrow really just tests whether Trump tries to remove her again before the Court even rules on whether he can.

He's called it, quote, a blank piece of paper — hasn't decided whether to go big picture or give traditional guidance. That's a real source of volatility risk into the weekend.

On business and tech: Monday's stock session split by index — Dow up, S&P down slightly, Nasdaq down the most — as chip stocks got sold ahead of Wednesday's Nvidia report. Consensus is for revenue around ninety two billion dollars, which would be roughly ninety five percent growth year over year — huge in absolute terms, but actually a deceleration from the triple digit growth rates Nvidia's been posting. The size of Monday's pre-earnings selloff tells you positioning has gotten defensive going in, which cuts both ways — less of a sell the news risk if they beat, more downside risk if they just meet expectations. Marvell reports Thursday as the week's other chip data point. On geopolitics: Russia and Ukraine had their heaviest exchange in days overnight Sunday into Monday. Ukraine hit dozens of targets inside Russia, including a fighter jet and a defense plant — but tragically, debris from one downed drone killed two children at a childcare center in Krasnodar. Russia hit back with missiles and well over a hundred drones across ten Ukrainian regions. Putin said Ukraine's strikes on economic targets had, quote, opened Pandora's box — an escalation in his rhetoric from just promising more destructive retaliation before.

And on Iran and the strait specifically: Oman's foreign minister is in Tehran today, which is actually notable — Oman's really the last consistent diplomatic channel anyone still has open with Iran. That visit comes right after Iran's own strait authority warned that any ship violating its new transit rules faces detention or seizure — which is exactly what happened to that tanker Amara a week ago, and which may be what this weekend's unclaimed strike on another tanker off Oman was also meant to signal.
