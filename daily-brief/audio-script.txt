The 6:15, for Saturday, November 28th. Here's what moved overnight: growth got revised up sharply, and inflation got revised up with it, five business days before a Fed meeting where a hike is already all but locked in.

Quick sweep of the board before we get into it. Oil's down hard on the month, both Brent and WTI off in the high teens percentage-wise, sitting in the low nineties and low eighties a barrel; the ten-year's sitting around five percent, the VIX near nineteen, the dollar index around one-oh-four, though none of those have been refreshed since the middle of last week so take them as a baseline, not today's number; nobody could confirm an official close for Wednesday or for Friday's half day, so treat any number you saw floating around for the S&P or for Nvidia this week as unconfirmed; gas prices keep falling, down to about three fifty-three a gallon nationally; the Hormuz situation is unchanged, no fresh attack or transit data since the middle of last week; and on the transactions side, the Yankees are quiet, the Knicks' last confirmed score is still that Toronto win from the seventeenth, and Northwestern's season is over at six and five with no bowl word yet.

Now the lead. Wednesday morning, 8:30 Eastern, two numbers landed within seconds of each other from the same government agency, and together they tell you more about where this economy and this Fed are headed than almost anything else this month. First: the second look at third-quarter GDP came in at four point three percent annualized. That's not a small revision — it's nine-tenths of a point above the first estimate of three point four percent, and it's roughly double the second quarter's two point two percent. The government said the upgrade came mostly from stronger investment — especially software spending — and from stronger consumer spending on goods, partially offset by weaker spending on services, mainly health care. Underlying private demand, which strips out some of the noise, actually rose five point eight percent, also revised up. Corporate profits did rise in the quarter, but by a lot less than the second quarter's jump.

Here's the second number, landing the same morning: the Fed's preferred inflation gauge, the PCE price index, for October. Headline inflation came in at three point eight percent year over year — that's up from three point six in September. Core, which strips out food and energy, came in at three point three, up from three point one. So in the same hour, you got confirmation that the economy grew faster than anyone thought, and confirmation that inflation is accelerating, not cooling. I want to flag one wrinkle here for anyone watching the headlines: the GDP report itself also contains an inflation number — a different one, the GDP price index — and that one was flat at two point one percent. That is not the same thing as the three point eight percent headline PCE number, and if you see someone quoting the lower figure as if it's today's inflation rate, that's the wrong one.

Why this matters: going into this week, the market had already priced a December rate hike as better than ninety percent likely. Nothing in Wednesday's data gives the Fed a reason to back off that. If anything, a growth number this strong paired with inflation accelerating is exactly the kind of combination that takes a dovish surprise off the table. One thing worth separating out, though — the strength in this GDP report leaned heavily on software and intellectual-property investment, which looks a lot like AI-related capital spending doing the heavy lifting, rather than ordinary household demand picking up broadly. That's a read, not a fact from the report itself, but it's worth keeping in mind before you treat four point three percent as uniformly good news. And two other data points that were supposed to land that same morning — weekly jobless claims and October durable goods orders — simply couldn't be confirmed this run, so don't assume either one fits neatly into this story yet. The one claims number we do have is older: the week ending November 14th came in at two hundred three thousand, a touch above expectations, but that's last week's news, not this week's.

What to watch from here: whether a cleaner read on rate-futures pricing, once it's available, shows the odds of a hike pushing even higher on the back of this data; whether the durable-goods and claims numbers, once they surface, back up the investment-led story or complicate it; and how Sunday's OPEC-plus decision lands into an oil market that's already down sharply on the month.

Over to business. Best Buy's third-quarter numbers, out Monday, were a clean beat and a meaningful raise — bigger, in percentage terms, than Walmart's beat the week before. Revenue came in just over ten-point-two billion, up from under nine-point-seven billion a year ago. Adjusted earnings per share came in at a dollar sixty-two, up sixteen percent from a year ago, and ahead of the roughly dollar-forty-nine consensus estimate, though that consensus number itself comes from secondary sources this run, not a fully verified read. Comparable sales were up about six percent, with online sales here in the U.S. up nearly eleven. Management raised full-year guidance meaningfully. What we don't have is how the stock actually traded on the news — that reaction couldn't be confirmed this run. Between Best Buy and Walmart, both big retailers are now reporting accelerating sales heading into the holiday quarter, against a backdrop of falling gas prices that's clearly a tailwind, even if neither company said so directly.

On technology: Nvidia's week since Monday's reinstatement rally was choppy rather than one clean direction. The last confirmed closing price was around one-fifty-one a share, and scattered snapshots from Friday morning put it in roughly the same range — so the honest answer is we don't have a clean read on the stock's net move for the week. What is firmer now is Jensen Huang's congressional calendar: December first, closed-door sessions before two House committees; December second, Nvidia's general counsel alone before the same panels; December third, Huang's first public testimony, as the sole witness before the Senate Banking Committee. And one correction to flag from last issue: the company some reports linked to a chip-diversion investigation — Megaspeed — still has not actually been added to the government's trade blacklist, according to one newspaper's reporting, even as the Justice Department has opened a separate criminal investigation into that firm. That's the opposite of what Treasury's own comments had suggested was coming.

On Iran and the strait: nothing new and dated turned up this run. The last confirmed incident is still that tanker strike near Khasab, Oman, from the middle of last week, and the diplomatic terms on the table — a full halt to enrichment, a verifiable end to any weapons pursuit, unconditional reopening of the strait — haven't moved. Given there's nothing fresh, we're keeping this one short rather than restating last issue's numbers as if they were new.
