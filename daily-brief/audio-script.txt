The 6:15, for Wednesday, August twelfth. Here's what moved overnight: CoreWeave posted a blowout quarter and jumped double digits after hours, while Alphabet dropped on a DeepMind shakeup and a scary new capex number — the AI trade is starting to split into winners and the people paying for it.

Quick lay of the land before the lead. Oil's basically flat overnight after Tuesday's spike — Brent's hovering just under ninety, WTI in the low eighties. The ten-year's at four point seven percent, drifting a touch lower since yesterday, and the whole curve eased a few basis points with it. The dollar's flat. Stock futures are modestly higher, Nasdaq futures leading with a gain approaching three-quarters of a percent. Gold jumped more than a point overnight to around forty-four hundred an ounce. Tokyo closed up about a percent, Hong Kong down about the same. National average gas ticked up to just over four dollars a gallon. On Hormuz: the closure's in its hundred sixty-fourth day, transit counts are still down around ninety percent from normal, the Navy disabled another blockade-running tanker overnight with two missile strikes, and war-risk insurance is still running thirty times what it cost before the war. On transactions: Northwestern's football program got a thirty-five million dollar gift, and the Yankees and Knicks had nothing new.

Here's the lead. CoreWeave reported second-quarter numbers Tuesday night that beat on everything that matters — revenue up about a hundred twelve percent from a year ago, comfortably ahead of what Wall Street expected, and a smaller loss per share than analysts had penciled in. The stock jumped somewhere around thirteen to fourteen percent in after-hours trading. Their backlog of contracted work is now above a hundred billion dollars, more than triple where it was a year ago, and they raised their full-year revenue guidance well above consensus. They also said Anthropic and Meta signed on as new customers this quarter. On paper, that's about as clean a validation as you can get for the thesis that demand for AI computing capacity is real and still growing.

But dig one layer down and the same quarter that proved the demand story also showed the cost of feeding it. CoreWeave's net loss under standard accounting actually widened sharply from a year ago, mostly because of surging interest payments on the debt they've taken on to build all this infrastructure. And that's exactly the problem Nvidia is trying to solve with the huge financing plan it announced earlier this week — non-binding agreements with six of Wall Street's biggest asset managers to help fund half a trillion dollars of AI buildout, essentially trying to get someone else to hold that interest-rate risk instead of the compute companies themselves. CoreWeave's results are the clearest evidence yet of why that market needs to exist: the growth is real, but the financing bill is eating into it anyway.

Alphabet is the mirror image. The stock fell as much as three and a half percent Tuesday on two things landing at once. First, a leadership shakeup at DeepMind — CEO Demis Hassabis stepped down to become chairman, and one of their top AI scientists, Jeff Dean, is leaving to start his own venture. Second, and probably the bigger deal for investors, Alphabet raised its full-year capital spending forecast to somewhere between one hundred ninety-five and two hundred five billion dollars, with quarterly spending now double what it was a year ago — enough to push their free cash flow negative for the first time ever. They also closed a twenty-five-billion-dollar bond sale the same week to help pay for it all. Put simply, the market is starting to treat hyperscaler AI spending less like a normal expense line and more like a credit story — the exact same question CoreWeave's own interest bill just made real from the other side of the trade.

The broader market reflected that split without resolving it. The Dow, S&P, and Nasdaq all closed lower Tuesday, driven as much by Alphabet's slide and Hormuz-linked oil strength as by anything systemic. Energy stocks outperformed again; airlines gave back ground on higher fuel costs — a pattern that's repeated through most of this war.

The read-through comes tonight, when Cisco reports earnings after the close. Options traders are pricing in one of the bigger post-earnings swings Cisco's had in a while, and analysts are looking for strong order growth tied to AI infrastructure spending, offset by some margin pressure from pricier components. If Cisco's numbers echo CoreWeave's, it reinforces that infrastructure demand is outrunning everyone's comfort with how it's being financed — a dynamic today's inflation report, landing after this broadcast, only complicates further. A hotter number tightens financial conditions right as hyperscalers are borrowing record amounts to fund all this.

On to the sections, quickly. Politics: the Senate passed a bill extending sanctions on Iran and Russia before leaving for recess, and a matching bill was introduced in the House, though the House itself is still out and hasn't scheduled a vote. Separately, Senate Democrats opened an investigation into deportation actions against military families. And there were special primaries Tuesday in South Carolina and Alabama.

Economics: today's the big one — July inflation data lands at eight-thirty this morning, after this recording. The consensus is for headline inflation around three and a half percent year over year and core inflation around two and a half, continuing to cool. But the Fed's last meeting saw three officials dissent in favor of a rate hike — the most hawkish split in about a decade — so this print is genuinely live for markets, not a formality.

Business: General Motors confirmed it's selling its stake in an Indiana battery joint venture to Samsung, which is turning it into its first standalone North American manufacturing plant. Read that as GM trimming a lower-return EV bet at the same moment the rest of corporate America is leveraging up for AI. Applied Materials reports Thursday, the next data point on chip-equipment demand.

Technology: Microsoft is reportedly planning to unveil its next AI chip in September and is chasing a huge order with its manufacturing partner for 2027 — though that's not confirmed by either company. Google's said to be pursuing something similar with its own chips. Both are bets that chip-packaging capacity, not chip design, becomes the real bottleneck — same tension as the lead story, just one level down in the stack.

Geopolitics: analysts spent the day picking apart Monday's Iranian military reshuffle — some read it as hardliners tightening their grip, others think it could actually make a deal easier by clarifying who's in charge. Also newly reported: multiple outlets say Trump secretly switched planes leaving a NATO summit last month after a credible assassination threat tied to Iran — old news that only surfaced this week, but a real data point on how seriously Washington's taking the personal risk here.

Iran and Hormuz: the sharpest overnight escalation — a Navy helicopter fired two missiles into a tanker that was running the blockade toward an Iranian port, the third vessel the U.S. has disabled since mid-July. Separately, and just outside the strait, a Houthi attack on a ship in the Bab el-Mandeb killed six crew members — the first shipping deaths tied to the Houthis in over a year. And yet oil barely moved on either headline, which tells you the market had already priced this kind of escalation in. One correction from yesterday: the claim that six insurance clubs pulled war-risk coverage entirely turns out to overstate a narrower move back in March — worth setting the record straight.

Sports, fast. Yankees beat Seattle four to one Tuesday night; their exact record is genuinely unclear right now because sources disagree, but they're trailing Tampa Bay in the East and holding a wild-card spot; they're back at it again tonight. Knicks are in the off-season — no news, though Josh Hart's extension talks are heating up. Northwestern's in preseason camp, and their football program just got a thirty-five million dollar boost from a major donor.
