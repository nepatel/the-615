The 6:15, for Monday, August 31st. Here's what moved overnight: the U.S. and Iran traded fire directly for the first time in over a month, and oil is back above ninety dollars.

Quick board, before the lead. Oil jumped a few percent overnight after the exchange, with Brent back near ninety and WTI in the mid-eighties; the two-year's sitting around four and a third percent, the ten-year just under four point seven five; stock futures dipped modestly and then pared back; gold quotes are a mess this morning and disagree with each other by more than a hundred and fifty dollars; Asia closed mixed, Europe's hovering flat with energy stocks up; gas is around four-oh-eight a gallon nationally, on pace for the most expensive August ever; the strait's been closed a hundred and eighty-three days now, with a tanker also hit near Oman overnight, separate from the military exchange; the Yankees swept the Red Sox all weekend; and Josh Hart is finalizing a four-year extension with the Knicks.

Now the lead. U.S. Central Command said Sunday its forces struck two Iranian rocket launchers on Larak Island, right inside the Strait of Hormuz, after spotting Revolutionary Guard personnel getting ready to launch rockets carrying sea mines into the strait. That's the first direct American strike on Iranian positions in more than a month. Iran's Revolutionary Guards said the strike killed and wounded several of their people, didn't give a number, and vowed to punish those responsible. The U.S. military pushed back hard on Iran's framing, calling its own action limited and precise, aimed at stopping an imminent minelaying threat to commercial ships. Iran hit back within hours — ballistic and anti-ship missiles from several cities, plus drones, aimed at two American bases in Jordan. A U.S. official says nearly all of it was intercepted, no serious damage reported so far.

Oil moved first and hardest. Brent, which had been sitting in the high eighties, jumped a few percent to back above ninety dollars a barrel in early trading — different trackers give slightly different numbers, and there's no clean settlement yet this early in the day. W-T-I moved up into the mid-eighties. Stock futures dipped — the S&P and Nasdaq both fell a bit right after the news broke, then clawed back most of that by the time New York trading got close. Volatility futures are running well above Friday's closing level, even though the spot index hasn't caught up yet.

Here's why this matters beyond the overnight headline. Just Friday, Fed Chair Warsh gave his first Jackson Hole speech and came out hawkish — he said inflation, not jobs, is the Fed's main worry right now, and traders responded by pushing the odds of a September rate hike up to around three in five, way up from about a third the day before. Now you've got an oil shock landing right on top of that. Higher energy costs feed straight into the same inflation numbers Warsh just said he's worried about — but a serious disruption in the Gulf is also a hit to global growth, which the Fed is simultaneously trying to cool down. So this is actually the first real test of how this Fed treats an oil-driven inflation problem versus a demand-driven one. Hike into a supply shock, and you risk looking like you're fighting the wrong fight. Stand pat, and it looks exactly like the flexible, data-watching approach Warsh just spent Friday defending. To be clear — nothing so far suggests this becomes a bigger, sustained conflict. It looks like one strike and one retaliation, with no American casualties reported. But markets had spent five months pricing a slow bleed in Hormuz traffic, not a fresh military exchange, and this morning they're still figuring out which one they're looking at.

On top of all that, a British maritime warning said a crude tanker got hit by an unidentified projectile late Saturday night near Khasab, in Oman — a separate incident from the military strikes, still unclaimed by anyone. So the quiet stretch we flagged Saturday is over.

Quickly on the rest of the board. Tuesday also brings the manufacturing survey for August, and Friday brings the big one — the August jobs report, expected to show around a hundred and ten thousand jobs added, with unemployment holding steady around four point two percent. That report, plus next week's inflation print, are the two numbers that'll really decide whether that September rate-hike call sticks. Two more big earnings prints this week — Palo Alto Networks and Dell report Tuesday, Broadcom reports Wednesday — all being watched as the next test of whether the AI spending boom that showed up in Nvidia and Marvell's numbers last week keeps broadening out. One trade estimate has Broadcom sitting on a sixty-billion-dollar order backlog heading into that report — take that with a grain of salt, it's a single source, not something the company's confirmed. Also worth flagging: cybersecurity spending is the other theme desks are watching this week, after CrowdStrike's strong quarter — Palo Alto's report Tuesday will be the read on whether that strength is company-specific or spread across the whole sector.

Overseas, a Russian strike on a warehouse just outside Kyiv late Friday turned into one of the deadliest single attacks of the year — the death toll's up to thirty-eight, with a few people still missing and dozens hurt, after the building, which was reportedly storing ammunition, set off a chain of explosions that tore through more than a hundred nearby homes. There were more casualties across the weekend too — a toddler killed in Kyiv Saturday, a teenager and an adult killed by Russian bombs in the east on Sunday. Ukraine, for its part, hit one of Russia's biggest oil refineries again overnight Saturday, plus a drone site and an airbase — both sides are still going after fuel and logistics, and now, with Friday's strike, dense civilian areas too.

Back to the strait. Beyond the tanker hit near Khasab, the numbers there are still messy and mostly stale — the fastest-moving trackers haven't updated since before the weekend. One tracker's dark-fleet count — ships hiding their location — jumped to eighty-eight in the last day, though that's from a different, bigger sample than what we quoted last issue, so it's not really an apples-to-apples comparison. War-risk insurance for a single big tanker transit is still running around ten million dollars, roughly forty times what it cost before the crisis — and notably, that number didn't move even after Sunday's exchange, which tells you insurers had already priced in something close to worst-case. Separately, worth knowing: overall Gulf oil exports are actually only down by around half from last year, not by the ninety-plus percent the strait traffic numbers alone would suggest — producers have been routing more oil around the strait entirely, by pipeline, rather than through it. And there's still no fresh word on that detained tanker near Qeshm Island, or on who was behind last week's still-unclaimed strike on a Greek-owned tanker off Oman — both of those threads stay open.

Sports, quick. Yankees swept the Red Sox all weekend, outscoring them twenty-five to seven across the three games, and now sit at seventy-eight and fifty-nine, holding the top wild card spot in the American League. The Knicks are in the off-season, but Josh Hart is finalizing a four-year, eighty-one-million-dollar extension, so that saga's basically over. Northwestern opens their season Saturday against South Dakota State.
