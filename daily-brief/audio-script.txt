The 6:15, for Sunday, October 18th. Here's what moved overnight: a credit crack in the AI buildout, not a flight to safety, drove the worst stock session since summer, while oil kept climbing on the Saudi-Houthi air war.

Quick scan of the board: Brent settled near one-ten a barrel, up almost four percent, with WTI close behind near ninety-nine; the ten-year's sitting around five-oh-nine percent and didn't budge on Friday's selloff; the S&P fell about one percent and the Nasdaq dropped over two percent while the VIX spiked above twenty for the first time in months; Asia and Europe actually closed higher Friday on their own session; gas at the pump is a little over four twenty-nine a gallon; the Strait of Hormuz is still being called closed by war-risk insurers even as the U.S. says ships are still moving through, with a handful of fresh, mostly unconfirmed strike claims overnight; and in sports, the Yankees' season is over, the Knicks wrapped a rough one-and-four preseason and open the regular season Tuesday, and Northwestern won again, now five and one.

Here's the lead. Friday was the worst day for stocks since the summer — the S&P down about one percent, the Nasdaq down over two percent, and the VIX, Wall Street's fear gauge, jumping more than twenty percent to its highest level in about four months. The trigger wasn't some new geopolitical shock — it was credit stress at CoreWeave, one of the big "neocloud" companies that borrows heavily to build out data centers for AI. CoreWeave has piled up more than fifty billion dollars in debt to fund that buildout, and this week the market started pricing that debt like it might not get paid back on schedule — the bonds traded at distressed, almost junk-level yields, and the stock has been cut by somewhere between fifteen and twenty-five percent on the week, depending which count you use. Other AI-infrastructure names — Nebius, IREN, Oracle, DigitalOcean — got dragged down with it.

Here's why that matters more than one bad stock chart: the entire AI buildout has been financed on the assumption that money stays cheap and hyperscaler demand keeps growing forever. When a major AI lessor's own cost of borrowing spikes into distress, that's the market's first real test of whether the economics behind this whole buildout actually work once lenders — not just excited stock buyers — have to sign off on it. On top of that, when volatility jumps this fast, it mechanically forces certain big funds to sell stocks whether they want to or not, which is probably part of what's behind some unconfirmed chatter about a hedge fund blowing up — there's one specific rumor floating around about a fund called VARA, but nobody's actually confirmed that, so treat it as just a rumor for now.

And here's the part that should really get your attention: this did not look like a normal panic. In a normal flight to safety, bond yields fall as everyone rushes into Treasurys. That didn't happen — the ten-year stayed right around five percent, and the thirty-year held above five and a half, both right where they've been sitting near the highs of this cycle. Meanwhile oil kept rising on the Saudi situation. So you've got stocks falling, bonds not rallying, and oil going up, all at the same time — that's a stagflation-flavored combination, not a classic risk-off trade. The market's effectively saying: we've got an AI-credit problem stacked on top of a hot inflation problem, and that's a worse combination than either one alone. Worth watching this week: whether the stress in CoreWeave's bonds spreads into the broader high-yield market, and what Tesla and Netflix say about AI spending when they report earnings Wednesday and Tuesday.

On the economy: September's inflation numbers, which weren't out when we last went to press, are now in, and they're hot across the board. Headline consumer prices rose six-tenths of a percent on the month and are up three point seven percent over the year, with gasoline doing most of the damage. Wholesale prices, the producer price index, ran even hotter — up over one percent on the month. Retail sales were up solidly too, though strip out gas stations and the underlying number was much softer. Put simply: this is an energy-driven inflation story, and it's exactly why bonds didn't rally Friday — the market's treating this as a supply problem, not weak demand.

On business: bank earnings were mixed, and almost beside the point given everything else going on. Bank of America actually missed, with profits down from a year ago after eating a one-point-two-billion-dollar fine from regulators over money-laundering and sanctions failures — the third-biggest penalty of its kind on record, and apparently it knocked about seventeen cents a share straight off their earnings. Morgan Stanley beat nicely on strong wealth management, with revenue up meaningfully from a year ago. BlackRock posted a record fifteen and a half trillion dollars under management, inflows up double digits. Goldman and Citigroup both beat too, with Citigroup's profit up around forty percent from last year. So it's a genuinely split picture in the banks — mostly good, one real miss — overshadowed entirely by the AI-credit story.

On technology, not much new to report. Rocket Lab pushed back its Neutron rocket's first launch to next year, which is the only confirmed item we've got in that lane right now — no new frontier AI model, no Starship or New Glenn news, nothing on a big venture funding round. That's now five issues running with nothing fresh there, even though venture money overall is still flowing at a record pace globally.

On geopolitics, the Saudi-Houthi air war that's been the big story for two weeks is still going, and it's still spreading. The Houthis hit an airport inside Yemen itself on Friday, Saudi forces kept intercepting incoming fire, and the U.S. is helping with intelligence and refueling but still hasn't joined the fighting directly, though Trump says he's looking at it. New wrinkle: Egypt's president is now asking his own parliament to consider authorizing help defending Saudi Arabia, which would pull in yet another country.

On Iran and the strait: Iran's navy claims a couple of tankers hit mines this week, but no outside source has backed that up, and it's the same dispute as before over whether these are actual mines or something else entirely. The numbers on how many ships are going dark, avoiding tracking signals, in and around the strait are still elevated, though even the people tracking it admit some of that could just be normal signal dropout rather than evasion. On diplomacy, Iran's seven-day plan to dial back its stockpile of highly enriched uranium in exchange for reopening the strait is still sitting with Washington, with no confirmed response yet — that's unchanged from where things stood a few days ago.

In sports: the Yankees' season ended weeks ago, no change there. The Knicks had a bumpy preseason, finishing one win and four losses, but they open the real season Tuesday at home against Philadelphia. And Northwestern beat Michigan State on the road, now five and one on the year, riding a three-game winning streak, with Rutgers coming to town next Saturday.
