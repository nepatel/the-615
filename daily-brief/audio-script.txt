Now the lead. Thursday night closed out three straight days of mega-cap tech earnings, and Amazon's print kept alive the same divide we've been watching all week. The headline good news: Amazon Web Services grew thirty-nine percent year over year, its fastest growth in about five years, beating expectations, and C-E-O Andy Jassy told investors to expect AWS growth above forty percent in both twenty twenty-seven and twenty twenty-eight. That's a genuinely strong number. But total company revenue came in just a hair below what Wall Street expected, earnings per share missed by about four percent, and -- most importantly -- the guidance for the current quarter came in soft. The stock, which had already closed down over four percent Thursday ahead of the print, fell further in premarket trading Friday morning, down somewhere in the range of six to nine percent depending on which snapshot you look at.

Here's the read: this completes a pattern we flagged earlier in the week. Microsoft beat on everything, spent less on AI infrastructure than it had guided to, and the stock rose. Alphabet beat on revenue but more than doubled its capital spending while its core search business slowed down, and it fell about ten and a half percent. Amazon sits in between -- its cloud business accelerated and beat, which is the Microsoft story, but a soft guide for the holiday quarter produced a stock reaction closer to Alphabet's. The takeaway across all three: the market isn't rewarding revenue beats on their own anymore. It's pricing each of these companies on whether their spending plans and their guidance actually line up. And if you line up the three cloud businesses side by side, the growth-rate comparison gets a little misleading -- Amazon's cloud arm is growing thirty-nine percent off a run-rate near a hundred eighty-four billion dollars a year, Microsoft's Azure is growing forty-eight percent off a smaller base, and Google's cloud business is growing over a hundred percent off the smallest base of the three. Bigger businesses just grow slower in percentage terms, so don't read too much into which one posted the biggest headline number.

And this happened against a macro backdrop that, on its own, looked fine. The government's first estimate of third-quarter economic growth came in at three and four-tenths percent annualized -- stronger than expected, and a big step up from the second quarter. Weekly unemployment claims fell to a level near a fifty-seven-year low. Neither of those good numbers helped stock futures this morning -- which tells you this selloff is about Amazon and tech earnings specifically, not a reassessment of the broader economy. With all three major cloud companies now reported, the quarter's real lesson is sector-wide: spending discipline gets rewarded, spending uncertainty gets punished, no matter how good the underlying growth actually is. Watch the regular session open today to see if this morning's selloff holds, and keep an eye on Tuesday's midterm elections, now just four days away, as the next big catalyst.
