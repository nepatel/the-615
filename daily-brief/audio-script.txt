Here's the lead. Anthropic priced its initial public offering late Wednesday at eighty-three dollars a share — above the seventy-to-seventy-eight-dollar range it had been targeting — which works out to a valuation around one and a half trillion dollars once you count employee stock. The company's raising something like seventy-nine billion dollars, maybe closer to ninety billion if the underwriters exercise their option to sell more shares. Shares start trading this morning at nine-thirty on the Nasdaq under the ticker A-N-T, so by the time you're reading this, the stock will already be open.

To put that in context: this tops the seventy-five billion dollars SpaceX raised back in June, which makes Anthropic's IPO the biggest primary stock sale on record. And it's not happening in isolation — there's a separate twenty-six-billion-dollar private placement already locked in from Nvidia, Microsoft, Google, AMD and Broadcom, the same companies that are both Anthropic's biggest customers and, for a couple of them, its chip suppliers. So this deal ties Anthropic's public stock price directly to the rest of the AI trade in a way it wasn't before.

The company's growing fast — about seventeen billion dollars in revenue last quarter, up from just one point four billion a year ago — but it's not profitable yet, and it's carrying about six billion dollars in debt against almost a trillion dollars in chip and data-center commitments stretching out to twenty forty-five.

Why this matters right now: this pricing lands just over a week after Jensen Huang's resignation threw Nvidia's own earnings call into chaos, and two days after Nvidia's actual results beat expectations anyway. So you've got a fresh, very public test of what investors think AI companies are worth, landing the same week the chip side of that trade is dealing with a leadership vacuum. One analyst from Renaissance Capital says this deal is so central to the AI story that it's basically a referendum — he thinks a jump to a hundred dollars today would be a solid result, and a move toward one-forty would mean AI enthusiasm is fully back. An IPO researcher from the University of Florida pushed back, saying the market's pricing in very lofty expectations that could be hard for Anthropic's management to actually deliver on.

Worth remembering: valuation guesses for Anthropic ran as high as two trillion dollars earlier this year, before the broader AI-stock pullback brought them down. So the one-point-five-trillion number tells you as much about where the market's reset its AI expectations generally as it does about Anthropic specifically. Senator Elizabeth Warren took a shot at the deal on social media, saying it doesn't answer for the risk to ordinary Americans' retirement accounts — worth noting that Anthropic's own filing admits its technology could pose, quote, catastrophic or existential risks to society if it's not developed responsibly.

What to watch: where the stock actually trades today versus that eighty-three-dollar price, and whether a pop or a stumble spills over into Nvidia, AMD and Broadcom, which are all financially tied into this deal. One more thing on Anthropic: Dario and Daniela Amodei started the company back in twenty twenty-one after leaving OpenAI, so a lot of coverage this morning is framing this listing as the first real public-market scoreboard between the two labs.

A few other things worth knowing. In politics, Kansas's governor's race is finally resolved — Democrat Cindy Holscher's win was confirmed and her Republican opponent, Ty Masterson, conceded, after a state audit backed up her lead. She's inaugurated in January.

On the economy, the story this week is the Federal Reserve turning more hawkish, not less. Minutes from the Fed's late-October meeting, out Wednesday, showed that holding rates steady was a close call for some officials, with two of them actually wanting to raise rates instead. That follows Fed governor Christopher Waller saying he'd back a half-point hike in December if inflation stays hot — and inflation has stayed hot, with October producer prices up six and a half percent from a year ago, the hottest pace since late twenty twenty-two. Retail sales, at least, came in exactly as expected, up two-tenths of a percent.

In business, Nvidia's results, reported Tuesday on Colette Kress's first call as interim CEO, beat across the board — revenue more than doubled from a year ago to around a hundred sixteen billion dollars, data-center revenue hit a record too, and the company added a hundred fifty billion dollars to its stock buyback with no expiration date. The stock reportedly rose after hours, and the board chair used the call to say flat out that Jensen Huang will not be coming back. Walmart reports its own results this morning, which will be our best read yet on how the American consumer is holding up heading into the holidays.

In tech, Jensen Huang finally has a firm date for his congressional testimony — a closed-door interview December first, alongside Nvidia's general counsel, about what that Chinese businesswoman he was involved with might have had access to. Venture money is still flowing hard into AI, too — an infrastructure startup called Mirendil just raised a billion dollars at something like a one-point-two-billion-dollar valuation.
