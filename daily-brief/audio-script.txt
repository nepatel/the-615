Okay, the lead. The UAE said yesterday it's suspending all trade and financial transactions with Iran until further notice — and this is a genuinely big deal, because these two countries have historically been close trading partners, something like twenty-seven to thirty billion dollars a year, with the UAE actually Iran's largest non-oil trading partner after China. The trigger was Tuesday night: UAE air defenses detected two Iranian ballistic missiles headed toward its waters, one falling short in international waters, the other landing inside UAE territory. Iran denies it fired them at all, calling it a false-flag operation, and no group has claimed responsibility — so we've got two governments with dueling accounts and no independent verification either way.

A few hours after the UAE's move, Trump escalated on social media, promising what he called the most crushing economic operation ever against Iran, and threatening serious consequences for any country whose banks or businesses give Tehran what he called a lifeline. That builds on sanctions and a naval blockade that were already in place; the details of anything new haven't been published yet. Iran's foreign minister called it economic terrorism and a distraction from America's own problems.

Here's why markets care: the UAE has been one of the last Gulf states still moving real non-Iranian oil volume through Hormuz, with the bulk of it now rerouted through that Omani corridor. A country that just cut financial ties with Iran is more likely to tolerate the U.S. Navy getting more aggressive about defending that route — or more likely to get hit by Iranian retaliation aimed at the route itself. Iran's own oil exports were already collapsing under existing sanctions — Kpler has them down to something like a hundred fifty-six thousand barrels a day in August, from close to nine hundred thousand in July — so this is a regime with less and less to lose from getting cut off further, which is its own kind of danger. And on the supply side, U.S. crude inventories came in a couple percent below their five-year average this week, so there's less domestic cushion than usual heading into a shock that has nothing to do with U.S. production.

Now, the twist: bonds are not confirming the hawkish, inflationary read you'd expect from an oil shock, because there's a separate story dominating rates this week. Wednesday's Fed minutes from the July meeting were more hawkish than the actual vote suggested — three regional Fed presidents dissented in favor of a hike, and other officials worried policy might not be tight enough yet. Chair Warsh also floated cutting the Fed's meeting calendar from eight times a year down to six. That should be pushing yields up. Instead, the Treasury also announced Wednesday it's doubling the size of its long-bond buyback program, a move aimed at steadying a bond market that had pushed the ten-year to its highest level in twenty months earlier in the week — and that buyback news did the work, pulling yields back down on the day. So you've got oil spiking on geopolitics, yields falling on a liquidity operation, and stocks sitting flat in between, waiting to see which force wins. The next real test is next Wednesday — Nvidia earnings, the PCE inflation print, and Lisa Cook's deadline, all landing the same day.

On politics: that funding gap between the House and Senate short-term spending bills is still unresolved, with the House not back until September. And Lisa Cook's own deadline to respond to the White House's removal effort is now six days away, landing right in the middle of next week's big data dump — so the Fed-independence question is now sitting directly on top of the rate debate, not off to the side of it.

On the economy: outside of the Fed minutes and the Treasury buyback, September rate-hike odds are back up to around thirty-five percent, having more than recovered off the lows from a couple weeks ago. Target's beat was juiced by a tariff-refund benefit worth about a dollar sixty-five a share — strip that out and earnings were still up a healthy amount, but it's not a clean read. Quickly on the wider world: Russia and Ukraine kept trading heavy strikes overnight with no real change in the picture, and nothing new to report on the Trump-Xi summit or the Gaza track since yesterday's issue — the Gulf story is dominating everything else this morning.

And back on Hormuz specifically: Trump repeated his threat to bomb Oman if it, quote, gets in the way of a shipping deal — the second time he's said that since May, aimed at a country that's never been at war with the U.S. The chief engineer of a Greek-managed bulker that was hit by an unidentified projectile off Oman over the weekend has died of his injuries; nobody's claimed responsibility. And the broader casualty and strike counts across this war are just too disputed between U.S. and Iranian sources to give you a clean number today.
