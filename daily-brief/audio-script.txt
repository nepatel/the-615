The 6:15, for Sunday, November 22nd. Here's what moved overnight: Wall Street's AI trade finally cracked. The S&P 500 and the Dow both closed in correction territory Friday, and the Nasdaq wiped out its entire gain for the year, as chip and AI stocks led a brutal week-long slide.

Here's the board, in one breath: Best Buy reports earnings Monday, the October inflation reading the Fed cares most about lands Tuesday ahead of Thanksgiving, OPEC-plus meets the Sunday after to set next year's output, and Jensen Huang testifies to Congress behind closed doors December first ahead of the Fed's own meeting the following week; oil's sitting in the low nineties for Brent and low eighties for WTI, both down on the week; the ten-year's still hovering just above five percent; gas prices keep falling, down to about three sixty-four a gallon nationally; the Hormuz situation is now at roughly two hundred sixty-six days of effective closure with no new tanker-transit data since our last issue, though Iran's Revolutionary Guard claims a fourth tanker was mined and the US says it struck an Iranian air-defense site and shot down drones aimed at Bahrain; and in sports, the Yankees made no new moves, the Knicks are eight and six, and Northwestern lost a heartbreaker at Minnesota to finish six and five.

Now the lead. Friday was ugly, and it was the culmination of something that's been building for two weeks. The S&P 500 fell three-point-seven percent to around six thousand nine hundred ten — its seventh straight losing day, and more than ten percent below its October record high, which is the textbook definition of a correction. The Dow dropped about one-point-nine percent, giving back an early gain. But the Nasdaq took it worst — down more than five percent on the day alone, which was enough to erase the entire index's gain for 2026. For the week, the S&P lost something like eight-and-a-half percent and the Nasdaq ten percent — the worst week for both in well over a year.

This wasn't a broad-based selloff. It was almost entirely concentrated in chips and AI infrastructure — the exact group that's been carrying this market all year. The semiconductor index fell more than ten percent on Friday alone. Nvidia itself dropped somewhere around thirteen to fourteen percent, which puts its decline since Jensen Huang's resignation two weeks ago past ten percent, on top of a stock that was already down roughly a quarter over the prior month. Names like Corning and Bloom Energy, which aren't chipmakers but sell into the data-center buildout, got hit just as hard — which tells you the market isn't just nervous about semiconductors specifically, it's nervous about the whole capital-spending cycle behind this AI boom, and whether the revenue to justify it actually shows up on schedule.

And get this — Anthropic, which had just gone public Thursday and closed up seventeen percent from its IPO price, gave almost all of that back on just its second day of trading, falling over nine percent. The newest, most-hyped AI listing on the market became a casualty of the very selloff it IPO'd into, within forty-eight hours of ringing the bell.

There's also a new wrinkle specific to Nvidia: the Wall Street Journal reported that the SEC has opened a civil investigation into whether Nvidia's disclosures misled investors. We don't know the full scope, and Nvidia hasn't been accused of anything — an investigation isn't a finding — but it's clearly connected to the same Huang situation that's already sending him to Congress in December, and it lands on a stock that was already the single biggest driver of this whole selloff. And this wasn't just a US story — Japan's Nikkei and South Korea's Kospi were each down more than five percent in their own Friday sessions, so the selling moved around the world well before New York even opened for the day.

Step back, and this is really the second big data point in nine days on how the market is re-pricing the AI trade. First it was Anthropic's IPO putting a trillion-and-a-half-dollar price tag on pure growth. Now it's the broader chip-and-infrastructure complex getting asked whether all that spending can actually pay for itself. And those two things are connected — the same handful of giant companies, Nvidia, Microsoft, Google, AMD, Broadcom, are financing Anthropic's buildout AND absorbing this week's stock-market hit directly, which means there's no clean way to treat this as two separate stories. Nobody serious is calling this a bubble popping outright — most of the commentary we found described it as a valuation reset inside a market that's still fundamentally intact, not a reversal of the AI thesis itself. But watch Monday closely: does the selling extend into Asia and Europe, does the SEC inquiry produce anything new before Huang's testimony, and does Walmart's actually-pretty-good quarter give the market anything else to hold onto, or does it just get buried under the same selling pressure.

On that note — Walmart's numbers, which we were waiting on in our last issue, came in solid. Revenue up about five percent to just under one hundred ninety billion dollars, beating expectations, and they raised their full-year guidance for the holiday quarter. E-commerce was up sharply both in the US and globally, even with some noise from Flipkart's sale timing in India dragging the growth rate down a bit this quarter, which should reverse into next quarter's numbers. It's honestly a pretty good, boring, reassuring read on the American consumer heading into the holidays — it's just gotten totally overshadowed by the AI selloff, which tells you something about where investor attention actually is right now.

On Iran and the strait: the most significant thing since our last issue is a cluster of claims from Wednesday — Iran's Revolutionary Guard says a fourth tanker hit a mine and exploded, and on the American side, Central Command says the US struck an Iranian air-defense site and shot down several one-way attack drones headed for Bahrain. Take all of that with real caution — it's each side's own account, nothing's independently confirmed. The actual shipping-transit numbers out of the tanker trackers, meanwhile, haven't updated since our last issue, so we're not going to pretend we have fresher data than we do. But put together with everything we've reported the past couple weeks, the picture is the same as it's been: persistent, grinding, low-level conflict, with no real sign of the ceasefire talks going anywhere, and no sign of it blowing up into something bigger either. The diplomats we quoted last issue — Secretary Rubio saying the US isn't close to a breakthrough, Iran's own security chief denying any talks are even happening — stand unchallenged this week.

Quick sports: the Yankees made no new moves this week. The Knicks beat Toronto and sit at eight and six. And Northwestern lost a tough one at Minnesota, twenty to seventeen, on a field goal as time expired — they finish the season six and five.

That's The 6:15. Trains are running.
