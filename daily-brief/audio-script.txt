The 6:15, for Sunday, November 8th. Here's what moved overnight: stocks got hit with their worst single day since last spring on Thursday, wiping out something like three trillion dollars in market value, before Friday's blowout jobs report helped claw back about a third of the damage.

Quick scan of the board, in rounded numbers. There's no FOMC meeting this coming week, the Fed's holding steady until its December ninth decision; October's CPI lands Tuesday morning, and October's PPI follows next Friday. On Monday it's Veterans Day, so the bond market's closed but stocks trade as usual. Friday's close, after the rebound: the S&P around seven thousand three hundred seventy-seven, up about a third of a percent; the Nasdaq near twenty-four thousand five hundred eighty, up a bit more than one percent; the Dow around fifty-one thousand two hundred eighty-five, down under half a percent, still dragged by chip stocks. The fear gauge eased to the mid-twenties after spiking as much as seventy percent higher during Thursday's panic. Oil is genuinely messy this week — different sources have Brent anywhere from the mid-nineties to just under a hundred dollars, and crude didn't settle cleanly, so take any single oil number with a grain of salt right now. The ten-year yield sits a bit under four point nine percent, though one other data feed shows a wildly different number we couldn't reconcile. Gold's a bit above four thousand seventy dollars. Gas prices keep drifting down, just over four dollars nationally. And a quick note on the Yankees, Knicks, and Northwestern coming up.

Now the lead. Thursday was a crowded trade unwinding hard, dressed up with a macro excuse. The Dow dropped more than eighteen hundred points, down three and a half percent; the S&P fell four and a half percent; the Nasdaq fell five and a half percent — the worst day for stocks since last April, and something like three trillion dollars in market value gone in a single session. The chip-heavy Philadelphia Semiconductor Index fell twelve percent. Nvidia dropped about nine percent. Micron and AMD each fell more than fifteen percent at one point before paring those losses. And some of the AI-infrastructure names — Nebius, Iren, CoreWeave — each fell double digits. The volatility index spiked as much as seventy percent higher at one point during the day. What triggered it, as best as multiple outlets described it, was a sudden repricing of worry about AI capital spending — whether the hundreds of billions of dollars going into AI infrastructure this year will actually pay off. There wasn't one single clean trigger — no specific downgrade or disclosure anyone's pointed to — so call this a sentiment shock more than a single news event.

Friday supplied the rescue, and it came from data, not from sentiment. October's jobs report, delayed earlier this year by the shutdown, finally came out Friday morning, and it was a clean beat. Payrolls grew by about a hundred sixty-five thousand jobs, well above what economists were expecting — most estimates were down in the fifty-nine to seventy thousand range. The unemployment rate actually fell, to four point one percent, better than expected. The two previous months got revised up by a combined eighty-four thousand jobs. And wages grew faster than inflation. Stocks rallied on that data and on a one-day truce in the AI trade — the S&P and Nasdaq both closed higher Friday, though the Dow still lagged because of continued weakness in chip stocks. Nvidia, AMD, and Intel all bounced back Friday, each up a healthy chunk. Even with that bounce, though, the week closed in the red across the board — the S&P down around two and a half percent, the Nasdaq down more, the Dow down just under a percent.

Here's the read. This was a positioning shock, not a growth scare — the economic data that helped stocks recover Friday describes an economy adding jobs faster than expected, with unemployment falling and wages keeping pace with inflation. That's not the kind of backdrop that normally justifies an eighteen-hundred-point Dow selloff two days earlier. What actually moved Thursday was crowded positioning in chips, memory, and AI-infrastructure names, finally cracking under capital-spending anxiety this program has been tracking for weeks now, across Alphabet's, Microsoft's, Intel's, and Amazon's spending guidance. Friday's jobs data didn't resolve that anxiety — it just gave index buyers a reason to step back in, a day after the crowded trade had already been flushed out. For investors, the next real test is Tuesday's inflation data — that's what tells us whether this stays a one-week flush or turns into something bigger. Watch Monday's open to see if Friday's calm holds.

A few other threads. On politics: the timeline for a new House Speaker is later than you might think — the House Democratic caucus votes internally on November eighteenth to nominate Hakeem Jeffries, but the actual binding vote that elects a speaker doesn't happen until the new Congress convenes on January third. Kansas and Ohio's governor races are still uncalled.

On business: a correction from last issue — Caterpillar's earnings actually came out back on October twenty-ninth, not this past week as we'd flagged, and they were strong: sales up seventeen percent, with a record order backlog. Qualcomm's earnings this week were genuinely murky — revenue beat expectations but fell from a year ago, and different outlets can't agree on whether profit per share actually beat or missed. Airbnb, though, had a clean beat — revenue up nineteen percent, with guidance raised.

On technology: a data-center operator called Iren posted around two hundred million dollars in AI-related revenue against a quarterly loss of two hundred sixty-three million — a concrete example of the spending-versus-payoff gap behind Thursday's selloff. And one single, uncorroborated report put Anthropic's expected IPO valuation around one to one and a third trillion dollars, down from around two trillion a month ago — take that one with real caution, it's one source.

On Iran and the Strait of Hormuz: Iran's military claimed several tanker mine strikes and a drone shootdown earlier this week, but independent maritime trackers only confirmed one actual incident — a tanker hit by unidentified projectiles off Oman. A separate strike on a different cargo ship was logged a day earlier. Nothing new has been confirmed in the last several days, though that's more "unclear" than "calm," since neither side's claims from earlier in the week were ever fully verified. A key tracking metric — the share of tankers going dark on ship-tracking systems — dropped sharply this week, but the tracking site itself says that drop might just be background noise, not a real signal.

And now, sports. The Yankees' season is over, but there's a decision pending: they gave reliever David Bednar a qualifying offer worth twenty-three million dollars, and he has until November seventeenth to decide whether to accept it. The Knicks had a good week — they beat Chicago at home by thirty, and beat Cleveland on the road, and sit at five wins and four losses. And Northwestern's football team lost at home to a ranked Iowa team, twenty-four to ten, dropping them to three wins and seven losses — their starting quarterback missed the game hurt.
