Here's the lead. Two clocks are running on this war, and this weekend they're reading differently. On land, there's a real pause -- one tracker puts it at ten days since Iran's last attack on a host state, the last one a missile barrage more than a week and a half ago, no drone attacks in two weeks. That's genuine, and it's new. But at sea, nothing has stopped. Just this past week there were two more tanker incidents inside the strait -- one unexplained hit on a vessel exiting the strait, and Iran's Revolutionary Guard claiming they struck another ship for an unauthorized crossing. Those are two separate ledgers, and a quiet week on the missile count doesn't mean a quiet week in the strait. Don't confuse the two.

Into that gap comes a claim worth treating carefully rather than believing outright. Iran's foreign minister said this weekend that Tehran and Oman have agreed a plan to reopen the strait to normal traffic. As of this morning, no Omani official has backed that up. This is different from the bigger multilateral meeting Saudi Arabia postponed back on the thirteenth, which still has no new date. If the Iran-Oman claim is real, worth remembering what it would actually mean: earlier versions of this framework described a temporary corridor that starts and ends in Iranian waters, with Iran deciding who gets through and fining violators. That's Iran controlling access to a strait that carries something like a fifth of the world's oil, not some neutral authority. None of that is confirmed for this specific claim -- it's just context for what "reopening" would really mean if it holds up.

And the market is still pricing the quiet version of this story. Friday's close had the VIX under fifteen, stocks pushing higher, oil lower on the week -- that's a tape that's been treating the strait as background noise for a while now, and nothing this weekend changes that read. That's the same setup as before: five weeks until the next Fed decision at the end of October, and the market's betting that Saudi and Omani workaround supply keeps flowing even though the shooting in the strait hasn't stopped, and even though nobody can agree on how much oil is actually moving through it day to day. I checked four different transit counts this morning -- Reuters, a firm called Windward, the IMF's tracker, and the Energy Secretary's own number -- and none of them match, not even close, some counting vessels, some counting barrels. A ten-day pause in one kind of attack is real information. It's just not, on this week's evidence, the information the market is actually pricing.
