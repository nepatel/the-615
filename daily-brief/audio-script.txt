Now the lead. Overnight, the escalation cycle that's defined the Hormuz crisis since Saturday's naval clash took its sharpest turn yet, inside about a day and a half. On Tuesday, US Central Command said it had destroyed five Iranian oil tankers — four in the Gulf of Oman, one near Kharg Island — after Iran's Revolutionary Guard twice tried to target a US Navy warship with ballistic missiles over the previous two days. American forces reportedly told the tankers' crews to abandon ship before striking, and there were no US casualties. Then, in the early hours of this morning Eastern time, Iran hit back directly at a US-linked target on land for the first time this week: Iran's Revolutionary Guard claims its ballistic missiles struck a hangar housing American surveillance and strike drones at an airbase in Jordan. Jordan's military says it intercepted ten of thirteen missiles fired, with the rest landing on open ground and no casualties reported. A US official called the attack ineffective. That account is still single-sourced and developing — Central Command hasn't confirmed it as of this taping.

Here's why that matters more than it might sound. Up to now, the retaliatory logic in this war has run ship to ship — Iran hits a tanker, the US hits a tanker back. A strike on a Jordanian airbase changes the shape of the conflict: it pulls a US partner directly into the crossfire, and it meaningfully raises the odds of an incident that produces American or allied casualties, which would be a different kind of event entirely from anything since the strait's closure back in February. Markets are already treating Tuesday's tanker strikes and this morning's Jordan attack as one continuous risk event rather than two separate stories, and that's the right way to read it.

Brent crude has now traded through ninety-nine dollars for the first time since this latest leg of the crisis started accelerating in late August — it was in the high eighties just three weeks ago.
