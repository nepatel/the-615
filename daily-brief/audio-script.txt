The 6:15, for Wednesday, November 4th. Here's what moved overnight: Democrats swept both the House and the Senate in the midterms, and markets are reading it as relief, not gridlock risk.

Quick board, for the record: stocks closed mixed Tuesday but futures are up around three quarters of a percent this morning on the election result, the VIX dropped eleven percent overnight to the high teens, the dollar's weaker, gold's jumped to around four thousand an ounce, Brent is somewhere in the low-to-mid hundreds with sources disagreing by a couple bucks, the ten-year's at about four point nine five percent, gas is a little over four dollars and ten cents a gallon nationally and still falling, Hormuz is still closed with the dark-tanker screen basically flat around seventy percent, and on the calendar: Qualcomm reports tonight, jobs report Friday, CPI a week from Tuesday.

Now the lead. The call came in two stages overnight. The AP called the Senate for Democrats late Tuesday — a seven-seat swing, flipping Ohio, Iowa, South Carolina, Maine, North Carolina, Kansas, and Texas. The House call followed early this morning, completing a clean sweep of both chambers. Democrats only needed a net gain of four Senate seats and three House seats — they cleared both with room to spare, enough that the networks called it before several individual races were even fully counted. Hakeem Jeffries is now positioned to become the first Black speaker in the House's history, pending the formal vote. The coverage is framing this as a rebuke of the administration, and it's tangled up with two things we've been tracking for weeks — persistent anger over prices, and the Iran war, including Monday night's strikes on Qeshm Island, which landed hours before polls opened despite the president's own pledge not to hit Iran before the vote.

Here's the part worth sitting with, though: a Democratic sweep is, on paper, the more disruptive outcome for the administration's agenda — and risk assets rallied anyway. The VIX fell eleven percent overnight, down to the high teens from Monday's low twenties. S&P and Nasdaq futures are both up around three quarters of a percent, Dow futures are up over a percent. That's a meaningful overnight move for an index that had already closed mixed Tuesday — the Dow actually finished the cash session down almost four tenths of a percent, so this is very much a reaction to the result landing, not a continuation of Tuesday's trade. Read on that: markets are pricing divided government as a check on unpredictability, not as gridlock — the same way investors usually read a midterm, but with extra weight this time because so much of this year's volatility has been policy-driven rather than cyclical. The dollar sold off to its weakest since late September, and gold caught the other side of that trade, rallying a couple-and-a-half to three percent into the low four thousands — which is an unusual combination, risk-off into a weaker dollar at the same time stocks are rallying. Reads like investors buying insurance against political uncertainty even while they're adding risk.

Oil's the one market still digesting the strikes rather than the election. Quotes this morning disagree by close to two dollars within the same hour, which tells you the market is still searching for a level after Monday's seven percent plunge on de-escalation hope ran straight into the re-escalation risk from the Qeshm strikes. And the dark-tanker signal out of the strait is basically flat day over day — not the second straight jump that would confirm a trend, just sitting at an elevated plateau. So here's the split for the investor listening on the way in: election risk is resolved and priced — that's what the rally in stocks and the drop in vol are telling you. Hormuz risk is not resolved. It's sitting exactly where Monday night left it, and it's still the variable that can move crude and the inflation path from here. Watch for any Iranian response to the strikes, whether today's oil session actually settles cleanly, and Friday's jobs report — the first hard data point the new Congress has to react to.

On politics beyond that: those seven flipped Senate seats span both the races we've tracked all cycle and a few states markets weren't pricing as competitive, which is part of why the margin surprised people, relative to the tighter betting odds we had going into last night. Governor races aren't confirmed yet this morning — exit coverage flagged Kansas and Ohio as newly competitive late, but nothing's verified. And that fake-polling story from last month is probably just going to fade now that the election's decided rather than get any kind of formal resolution.

They've also got a new CFO starting, Cecile Guegan. Qualcomm reports after the close tonight; Caterpillar's expected to report this week too, but neither is confirmed yet.

On technology: that AMD number is really the story — a data-center segment growing ninety-seven percent year over year is the clearest sign yet this week that AI infrastructure spending is holding up, even with chip stocks in Asia still jumpy earlier in the week before this morning's broad rally on the election overrode that.
