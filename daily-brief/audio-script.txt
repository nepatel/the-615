The 6:15, for Saturday, August fifteenth. Here's what moved overnight: the bond market just sent Washington a warning. Thirty-year government debt sold this week at the highest interest rate in a quarter century — and it happened in the same week inflation data actually came in cooler than expected. That mismatch is today's story.

Quickly, the state of the world before we get into it: oil is sitting in the high eighties for Brent and low eighties for WTI, both up around one and a half percent on the day but roughly flat to a little lower for the week; the two-year Treasury yield is around four point two percent, the ten-year is about four point seven, and the thirty-year jumped past five point two percent at auction this week, its highest since two thousand one; the dollar index is flat just under a hundred; gold is up around forty-three hundred dollars an ounce; stocks are near records, with the S&P up about seven-tenths of a percent Friday and the so-called fear index, the VIX, actually falling; retail gas is just over four dollars a gallon, the highest ever for an August; the Strait of Hormuz has now been effectively closed for a hundred and sixty-seven days, with three separate drone attacks on tankers in just the last two days; and the Yankees, Knicks, and Northwestern all had no roster moves to report.

Okay, the lead. On Thursday, the Treasury sold twenty-five billion dollars of new thirty-year bonds, and they cleared at just above five point two percent — the highest yield on long government debt since two thousand one. It wasn't a disaster of an auction, demand was roughly average, but it was still soft enough to mark the third auction in a row where buyers demanded a little more than the market expected going in. And the way traders are reading that is pretty blunt: the bond market is telling the Treasury Secretary that financing the government's still-growing deficit at the long end is just going to cost more now — a quarter-century high, in fact — and that's true even though the Federal Reserve probably isn't about to keep raising rates.

Here's why that's strange, and worth sitting with. The rest of the curve moved the opposite way this week. July's inflation report came in soft — prices up just a tenth of a percent for the month, three and a half percent for the year, with the core rate cooling to two and a half. Traders responded exactly like you'd expect: they cut the odds of a September rate hike roughly in half, down to about forty percent. The two-year yield, which tracks Fed expectations closely, dropped on that news. But the very next day, the thirty-year sold off into a twenty-five-year-high yield, and the ten-year barely budged. So you've got the short end pricing a friendlier Fed, and the long end pricing something else entirely — that's a steepening curve, not everything moving together.

Two things explain that split, and neither one is really about the Fed's next meeting. First, the very same day as that soft inflation report, the producer price report told a different story — wholesale prices were flat headline, but the core measure, stripping out food, energy, and trade, jumped four-tenths of a percent, four times June's pace, driven by a big jump in financial services fees. That's a services-inflation signal, and it flows straight into the next big inflation print the Fed actually targets, due in eleven days, right before the September meeting. Second, oil is adding its own pressure to that same pipeline. Brent settled Friday just under ninety dollars, up for the day but still a touch lower than where it started the week — recovering from a surprise stockpile build mid-week — but the bigger point is that it's staying elevated on a crisis premium, with three confirmed drone strikes on tankers tied to the UAE's national oil company in just the last two days. The market isn't pricing Hormuz as resolving. It's pricing it as a standing risk.

And here's the part that should make you pause: stocks completely shrugged this off. The S&P hit a fresh high Friday, and the VIX actually fell. The dollar didn't move either. It's really only the rate-sensitive parts of the market — mortgages, in particular, already sitting at a one-year high — that are treating this as real news. So you've got equities pricing calm, and the long end of the bond market pricing fiscal stress and inflation risk. Those two views can't both be right for very long. And landing this same week that the Pentagon confirmed it's swapping out an aircraft carrier that's been stretched to its breaking point after a record deployment, and Washington and Tehran are both escalating demands for reparations from each other — that's not exactly the backdrop that makes you want to bet on the calm read. For the new Fed chair, Kevin Warsh, only a few months into the job, this is a real problem: even if he holds rates steady in September because inflation cooled, the bond market is telling him the government's own borrowing costs are rising anyway, regardless of what he decides.

On to the rest. In Washington, the Senate passed a funding bill this month that keeps the government running through early December — past the midterms — but the House still hasn't voted on it and isn't back from recess until September, so that risk is delayed, not resolved. The only election on the calendar in the next ten days is Alaska's primary on Tuesday.

On the economy, beyond the inflation and bond story, retail sales actually dropped six-tenths of a percent in July, the worst month in over a year, though the three-month trend is still solidly positive, so call it a pullback, not a collapse. Jobless claims ticked up a little but remain historically low.

In business, it was a strong week of earnings with a weird pattern: CoreWeave, Cisco, and Applied Materials all beat expectations on the actual numbers — Cisco even posting record orders — but two of the three stocks fell anyway because guidance wasn't good enough for a market pricing these AI infrastructure names for perfection. Nvidia's push to line up half a trillion dollars in Wall Street financing for AI data centers is still just a handshake deal, not signed contracts. And Databricks raised five billion dollars at a hundred-ninety-billion-dollar valuation, the biggest venture round of the month — proof private money is still chasing this trade even as public markets get pickier.

In technology, two more serious open AI models shipped this week alone, part of a release pace that's basically monthly now. The bigger story isn't any one model — it's how all this AI infrastructure spending actually gets financed, whether that's Nvidia's Wall Street push or private rounds like Databricks'.

Geopolitically, President Trump said Friday that, quote, pretty soon he'll be declaring the Strait of Hormuz a US territory. Iran's response was blunt — they said it can't be seized by tweet or by aircraft carrier. Neither oil nor stocks moved on the comment, so treat it as rhetoric for now, not policy. Separately, the Navy confirmed it's relieving the carrier Abraham Lincoln, which has been deployed more than two hundred fifty days without a port call, after reports of a serious mental health crisis among the crew.

On Hormuz itself: attacks got worse this week, not better, with three tanker strikes in forty-eight hours. Iran's maritime authority says the strait stays closed until its conditions are met. A Pakistani-mediated deal for a shared shipping lane was described as close ten days ago, but nothing's been signed. And insurers say war-risk premiums — now ten to fourteen million dollars for a single big tanker crossing — won't come down for a year or more, even after any ceasefire.

Quick sports: the Yankees lost three-one at Toronto Thursday and sit at sixty-eight and fifty-three, second in the AL East. The Knicks and Northwestern are both in the off-season, with Northwestern opening its year on September fifth.

That's The 6:15. Trains are running.
