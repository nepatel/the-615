Okay, the lead. Back in June, the U.S. and Iran signed a memorandum in Islamabad giving themselves sixty days to reach a real, final deal. That clock ran out Monday with nothing to show for it. Reporters asked Trump if he'd extend the deadline. He said no, said he's in no rush, and then said something that raised eyebrows — that his administration has a direct backchannel going with Iran's Revolutionary Guard Corps. The Guard Corps immediately called that delusional, and separately, Iran's foreign ministry shot down a different rumor going around about a secret meeting in Erbil. So, denied twice, from two different corners of the Iranian government.

Then Trump turned his fire on Oman — the country that's actually been brokering the more technical piece of this, a shipping-lane arrangement with Iran. In a Fox interview he said, quote, if Oman gets in the way, we'll bomb the you-know-what out of them. That's the second time this year he's threatened a Gulf country that has never been in conflict with the U.S. and has actually helped free detained Americans in the past. Oman's government didn't respond publicly, though plenty of Omanis pushed back online. Reading between the lines here — going after the mediator, not Tehran, suggests the White House doesn't love the shape of the emerging Iran-Oman deal itself: inbound ships through Iranian waters, outbound through Omani waters, no fees during a transition period. That's not really what Washington's been asking for.

And then it escalated again. Tuesday evening, the UAE says its air defenses picked up and intercepted two ballistic missiles fired from Iran — one landed outside their territorial waters, one inside. Sirens went off in several emirates for about ten minutes before the all-clear. Nobody was hurt, but this is the first time in months Iran has been tied to a missile launch directly at the UAE, not just the tanker attacks we've been tracking.

Markets are treating all of this as durable risk, not just a headline. Brent's up near ninety-two dollars, a fourth day of gains in a row, now more than a quarter above where it was before this whole crisis started. That's the backdrop heading into two big events today: a twenty-year bond auction, and at two p-m Eastern, the Fed releases its July meeting minutes. Everyone wants to know how hard three regional Fed presidents pushed for a rate hike that the committee ultimately didn't do — the most divided vote in about a decade.

Stocks are feeling the rate story more directly than the oil story right now, though the two are starting to reinforce each other. The read here: higher-for-longer interest rates are repricing the most expensive, most crowded trade in the market — AI infrastructure — at the exact same moment an oil shock is showing up in headline inflation.
