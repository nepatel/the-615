The 6:15, for Sunday, October 25th. Here's what moved overnight: nothing traded, because it's the weekend — but Iran's newest tanker-mine claim from Saturday still hasn't been confirmed by anybody outside Tehran, and the dashboard this brief uses to track evasive shipping in the strait just changed how it counts things, right in the middle of a crisis.

Quick scan of the board: markets are closed, so Friday's numbers are still the last real prices — Brent crude around one seventeen a barrel, WTI near one oh three, both at war-highs; the Dow, S&P, and Nasdaq all sitting where they closed Friday, down on the week; the fear gauge just under twenty; gold a little above four thousand; the dollar index barely softer. Tonight is Game Three of the World Series in Tampa, with the Brewers up two games to none after winning Game Two Saturday, five to four. Northwestern blew out Rutgers forty-two to fourteen Saturday, they're six and one now and bowl eligible. The Knicks didn't play Saturday, still oh and two. The Fed meets Tuesday and Wednesday, a hold is heavily favored. Inflation and growth data land Thursday, OPEC-plus meets a week from today, and the midterms are now just over a week out.

Now the lead. Since nobody's traded since Friday afternoon, Friday's close is still the number that matters: Brent settled at a hundred sixteen dollars and seventy-two cents, the highest close of the seven-month war; WTI at a hundred three and a quarter; the gap between those two, which had been blowing out all week, sits around thirteen and a half dollars; the ten-year yield at five percent even, the two-year just under four and three-quarters, the thirty-year above five and a quarter. The dollar index and gold both closed Friday roughly where this brief left them — the dollar a touch softer, gold still drifting down even on a nervous tape, which is the same odd pattern this brief flagged twice already this week. None of that has moved since, because there's been no session to move it. That's really the whole story this morning — the market's shut, but the claims keep coming.

And a new one did come. Overnight Friday into Saturday, Iran's Revolutionary Guard navy said two supertankers were struck and disabled by mines in the strait. As of this morning, more than a day later, nobody — not the UK's maritime monitoring office, not any flag state, nobody independent — has backed that claim up. That's not unusual lately; most of these mine and strike claims from Tehran this past week haven't been matched to a specific outside confirmation. It just piles onto Thursday's bigger, still-unresolved dispute, where President Trump called an earlier seven-tanker claim fabricated outright, and nobody's settled that one either.

Here's the part that's actually new, though, and it's not about the war, it's about how we're measuring it: the tracker this brief has used for weeks to show what share of tankers are going dark on ship-tracking systems — that exact metric just disappeared from the page. In its place is a different measure, a count of high-risk and moderate-risk vessels out of everything the site is watching in the Gulf, and the site itself is now flagging that its own ship-tracking feed is stale. So think about what that means: it's not that the evasive-shipping problem got better or worse over the weekend, it's that the yardstick changed. Friday's reading and today's reading aren't the same thing anymore, and that's worth remembering the next time someone quotes a scary-sounding percentage from this tracker — ask what it's actually counting.

The two broader risk readings this brief does track consistently barely moved. Overall crisis pressure held at an extreme level, unchanged. The escalation outlook ticked up one point after dipping one point Friday — basically flat, in other words. The war-risk insurance premium, the one that's supposed to reprice fastest when something real happens at sea, is still stuck on an estimate from more than two and a half weeks ago. Think about that for a second — if real mine strikes were happening the way Iran claims, you'd expect insurers to be scrambling to reprice risk. Instead, that number hasn't moved in eighteen days, which says something about how thin actual insurance activity in the strait has gotten, war or no war.

So here's the read for this morning: a closed market can't test a claim the way a trading session can. Friday's selloff already priced in one unconfirmed story this week. Saturday's claim is sitting out there with nothing to accept it or knock it down before markets actually open again. The first real test is Monday evening, our time, when Tokyo and Hong Kong open for the week — that's the first chance for any of this to actually move a price. Worth watching: does anybody corroborate or debunk the new mine claim before then; does the ship-transit count, which has been stuck on the same six-day-old reading for a week now, finally update; and does the tracker site bring back a real dark-tanker comparison or stick with its new format.

On Iran and the strait a little more broadly: diplomatically, nothing's changed. Iran's foreign minister said again, about a week ago, that Tehran's ready to talk "based on mutual respect and credible guarantees" — that's a softer tone than September's seven-day reopening plan, which the president rejected outright, and Washington never disclosed any follow-up response to that one. Betting markets on a return to normal shipping haven't budged either — still under one percent by the end of this month, six percent by the end of November, twelve percent by the end of December. OPEC-plus meets exactly a week from today to set December production, after three straight months holding output flat; so far nobody in that group has said a word publicly about this week's mine claims.

One more thing worth flagging before we get to the week ahead: the Fed decision Tuesday and Wednesday. Outside trackers that try to estimate what traders are pricing in don't agree with each other right now — some put the odds of anything other than a hold in the high single digits, others in the mid-teens — and this brief couldn't get a clean, current read straight from the exchange itself this morning, so treat any specific percentage you hear this week with some skepticism. The safe takeaway is just that a hold is the heavy favorite, and almost nobody's pricing in a cut.

And the World Series: Milwaukee beat Tampa Bay five to four Saturday night, so the Brewers lead the series two games to none heading into Game Three tonight down in Tampa.
