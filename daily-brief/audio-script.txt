The 6:15, for Monday, September 21st. Here's what moved overnight: oil is down for a fourth straight session, its worst stretch since June, even after the Houthis fired a missile at Riyadh for the first time — the market's betting on Saudi supply recovering and a friendlier US-China summit this week, and that bet is running into a Gulf security picture that just got harder, not easier.

Here's the lead. Brent crude is down for a fourth day in a row now — the longest losing streak since June — as traders weigh two things pulling in the same direction. First, signs that Saudi Arabia's oil output is recovering after weeks of Houthi missile and drone strikes on its energy infrastructure. Second, real optimism heading into Thursday, when Trump hosts Xi Jinping in Washington, with preparatory talks over the weekend in New York covering a new US-China dialogue on AI safety, tariff cuts on some non-sensitive goods, and — the actual point of the whole summit — whether the two sides extend a trade truce that otherwise expires in November.

But "Saudi supply recovering" doesn't mean the region got safer. Saturday, Saudi Arabia said the Houthis fired a ballistic missile directly at Riyadh — the first time they've targeted the Saudi capital itself in this round of fighting. It was intercepted, and Saudi air defenses also stopped attempted strikes on four other cities the same day. That's a Yemen-based escalation, a different theater from the Strait of Hormuz itself, but it's the same Iran-aligned axis the oil market is trying to price — and it argues against treating this as a de-risking story.

Iran's own language on Hormuz hardened at the same time. Officials are now saying the strait won't fully reopen until the US meets its commitments under an interim peace deal that has already lapsed — which is a harder, more conditional line than the unconfirmed claim we flagged yesterday that Oman had agreed to a broader reopening. There is a real, narrower Iran-Oman transit corridor from back in August, but that was never a general reopening, and conflating the two overstates how close to normal this actually is.

So here's the gap: the tape is pricing an easier supply picture and a friendlier US-China backdrop, while the actual security picture on the ground got harder over the weekend, not easier. That's the same disconnect we've flagged in prior issues — markets and the missile count pointing in opposite directions — just with a fresh data point on each side today. And there's a second wildcard sitting inside Thursday's summit itself — Xi is arriving with genuine leverage, China's exports up twenty-five percent year over year last month, on pace for another trillion-dollar trade surplus, which means Beijing has less incentive to make concessions than Washington does, and Trump goes in constrained by that same costly Iran conflict. Taiwan is also reportedly on the agenda, even though it isn't the headline topic. For anyone with Gulf-linked energy or shipping exposure, Thursday in Washington is still the real catalyst this week — but Saturday's missile is a reminder the tail risk hasn't actually shrunk.

On politics: Trump signed the Russia and Iran sanctions bill into law Friday — it's now named for the late Senator Lindsey Graham. It gives the president tariff authority against major buyers of Russian energy, with most of it kicking in within thirty days, by October eighteenth. The awkward part: China is one of the larger indirect buyers of Russian energy this bill could reach, in the same week Washington wants a trade concession from Beijing. Separately, the White House's ban on CNN, Politico, and MS NOW staying in effect, with no update this week.

On economics: it's a light data day. The homebuilder sentiment index is due mid-morning, expected to hold roughly flat from August; a miss there would tie directly to how expensive thirty-year financing has gotten, and a hold would say builders are shrugging it off. The Treasury's auctioning short-term bills today too — routine, but one more read on demand at the short end, a day after new confirmation that the two-year's sitting near four and three quarters percent.

On business: the real story is the summit prep itself — that New York meeting over the weekend between the Treasury Secretary and China's Vice Premier covering trade, AI, critical minerals, and the Iran war, with earnings season also picking up midweek — dozens of reports Tuesday through Thursday — so markets have two storylines to juggle at once by Thursday.

On geopolitics: Ukraine launched its largest drone attack yet on Moscow on Sunday, timed to the last day of Russia's parliamentary election — an election Putin's party won easily, north of fifty-seven percent of the vote, with no anti-war party allowed on the ballot. Two people died in the Moscow region, two more in Russian-held Kherson including an election official, and a Moscow oil refinery was hit again. Russia fired well over a hundred drones back into Ukraine the same day.

On Iran and Hormuz: beyond the lead, there are really three separate tracks moving independently right now — a stalled multilateral meeting in Oman that's been postponed with no new date, the narrow bilateral transit corridor that's already operating, and Iran's broader, newly-hardened conditions for a real reopening. Treating those as one storyline gets it wrong. And tanker war-risk insurance is trending toward the top of its range, which lines up with the harder Iranian stance more than with any real reopening.
