Here's the lead. The market spent yesterday pricing in a deal that hasn't actually been made yet. Brent's down six days running, and the trigger was Trump telling reporters his team -- Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner -- had a very good, three-hour sit-down with Iran's foreign minister on the sidelines of the UN General Assembly, with another meeting already being planned. That's notable timing, because Trump's own speech to the UN a few hours earlier was combative, he repeated a threat to annihilate Iran. So this reads less like a breakthrough and more like both sides keeping a back channel open no matter what they're saying in public.

The sharper piece of news came separately. A senior Iranian official told Reuters yesterday that Iran can reopen the strait within seven days, if the US eases military pressure and lifts its blockade on Iranian ports. That's the first time Tehran's put an actual number of days on this, instead of just talking about it in the abstract. But the condition is doing all the work here -- the US blockade is still on, nothing suggests Washington's agreed to ease it, and right now the strait is running at something like one percent of normal traffic. Two tankers got hit inside it as recently as Monday. So a seven-day offer tells you something about what Iran wants, not that the clock has actually started.

There's a political wrinkle too. Iran's president, Pezeshkian, flew into New York and speaks to the General Assembly this morning, after hardliners back home leaned on him to skip the trip entirely, or treat the UN stage as what one hardline paper called a courtroom for American crimes. That he went anyway, with a delegation that's now meeting American envoys, is being read as hardliners making a little room for diplomacy -- though that's an interpretation, not a confirmed fact, and it's worth treating it that way until we see what actually gets said today.

And here's the part that complicates a simple story. The dollar's at its highest since late July, and gold's falling -- normally that's exactly what you'd expect if geopolitical risk were draining out of the market alongside oil. But it's also exactly what a hawkish Federal Reserve does on its own, and right now the odds of another rate hike next month are genuinely split, somewhere between fifty-four and sixty percent depending which tracker you check. Meanwhile stocks split the difference too -- the Nasdaq hit a record Tuesday, up nearly three percent on the week, while the Dow actually slipped. That looks more like a market trading an AI and rate story than one trading Hormuz. Bottom line: six days of falling oil is real, and it is connected to Iran. But none of yesterday's numbers actually prove the strait is any closer to reopening than it was three days ago.
