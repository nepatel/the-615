Now the lead. Iran's foreign minister and Oman's foreign minister put out a joint statement Tuesday floating what they're calling an interim framework to get shipping moving again through Hormuz. The idea is a temporary corridor, about seven miles wide, plus a mine-clearance effort to make the water safe to sail again. Here's the catch worth noting: ships coming into the Gulf would sail entirely through Iranian waters under this plan, and only the outbound route would touch Omani waters too — so Iran keeps physical control of the corridor that Oman is brokering. The two sides say they'll keep talking for another thirty to sixty days about a permanent version of this, who administers the strait long-term, and how security and information-sharing would work.

Oil markets took this as a big deal — Brent's now down for a third straight day, giving back the gains built up over the past couple weeks on fears of a wider blockade. But here's the thing: that read is running ahead of the facts. Iran's own deputy foreign minister said, on the very same day, that this Oman agreement does not mean the strait is reopening — his words were that it remains, quote, militarily closed. And the shipping data backs him up — transit counts are still stuck in the single digits a day, versus a normal pre-crisis pace of something like eighty or ninety ships. So what actually happened Tuesday is narrower than the oil price move suggests: Iran agreed to talk about a corridor it would still control, with a month or two before anything substantive gets decided.

The bigger picture here is about sequencing. This diplomatic opening from Oman is landing in the same week as Monday's big Treasury sanctions push against Iran, where the Treasury Secretary pointedly declined to set any deadline for compliance. So you've now got two tracks running at once — Washington's sanctions pressure, and Oman's diplomacy — and Oman's the one Gulf country that's kept a real channel to Tehran open this whole crisis, which is exactly why it's the one that can produce something Washington's sanctions alone couldn't. Whether this actually goes anywhere in the next month or two, or just gives Iran a talking point while the strait stays closed on the water, is the thing to watch — not today's oil price pop, which is unwinding a risk premium that was already getting ahead of itself.
