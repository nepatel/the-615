The 6:15, for Monday, September 7th — Labor Day. Here's what moved overnight: Friday's jobs report came in almost three times what economists expected, and it just about erased the dovish turn the Fed took less than a week ago.

Quick rundown of the board before we get into it. Brent's pushed up near ninety-seven dollars on a weekend flare-up in the Gulf, the ten-year's back up around four point eight percent, the Dow lost around a quarter of one percent Friday on the jobs number, the VIX is sitting in the mid-teens, gas is a touch over four fifteen a gallon nationally, markets here are closed today for Labor Day though Asia and Europe are trading, the Strait of Hormuz is on day one ninety of effective closure, OPEC and its partners agreed over the weekend to just hold output steady into October, and on the sports side, the Yankees dropped the weekend series to the Padres and sit three and a half games behind Tampa Bay, the Knicks still haven't signed Josh Hart to an extension, and Northwestern opened its season with a win over South Dakota State.

Now the lead. Friday's August jobs report landed the opposite of what Thursday's market had braced for. Payrolls jumped by a hundred sixty-two thousand — nearly triple the fifty-three thousand economists were expecting — and unemployment held at four point one percent. On top of that, the report revised the two prior months up by a combined fifty-five thousand jobs, including flipping July from a shocking decline into a solid gain. Wages were still moderate, up three tenths of a percent on the month and around three percent for the year, so this wasn't an inflationary wage story — it was purely a strength-of-hiring story. And that mattered, because just one day earlier, Fed Governor Waller had broken publicly with the hawks, saying he'd lean toward holding rates steady in September if inflation kept cooling. Odds of a September hike had collapsed to basically a coin flip on that. The jobs report reversed a good chunk of that move — hike odds bounced back up to around sixty percent. Waller himself came out again and said the report looked consistent with a fully healthy labor market, not the kind of weakness that would've pushed him toward easing — a noticeably different tone than the day before.

Markets moved the way you'd expect: stocks dipped, with the Dow, S&P, and Nasdaq all a bit lower Friday; and yields jumped, with the ten-year up a few basis points and the two-year hitting its highest level since early last year.

Here's the thing to watch, though. Waller never said jobs would be the number that changes his mind — he pointed specifically to Thursday's inflation report as decisive. A strong jobs report doesn't contradict that framework directly. But it does hand more ammunition to the hawks on the committee, which means Thursday's inflation print now carries even more weight than it already did. A hot number pretty much locks in a hike. A soft number doesn't settle it anymore on its own, because Friday's strength is sitting on the other side of the scale. One wrinkle worth flagging: a chunk of the report's weakness was actually in tech-adjacent jobs — information-sector employment fell by twenty-three thousand, tied to cuts in data processing and computing infrastructure — which is a reminder that a strong headline number can still be hiding some softer spots underneath.

Two other things happened over the weekend. Nvidia confirmed it's buying Hugging Face for right around thirteen billion dollars, mostly to shareholders with some retention money for staff — its second-biggest deal ever, expected to close sometime in the first half of next year. And separately — this is probably the bigger story of the weekend if you're thinking about risk rather than just markets — Iran and the U.S. had their first direct military exchange of this whole war. More on that in a minute. Markets have barely priced it so far, with oil up about one percent, but it's a meaningful shift.

On politics: a new poll found a big majority of voters in swing midterm districts don't want new AI data centers built near them, which campaigns are already picking up on. The president said his political operation plans to spend four to five hundred million dollars on the midterms. And two court rulings over the weekend — one blocking a mail-ballot rule change, one striking down a new Missouri congressional map — both preserve more of the current electoral landscape than Republicans had been pushing for.

On the economy: the Fed's target range is still three and a half to three and three quarters percent; a hike this month would take it up a quarter point. Friday's report was the last full jobs read before the meeting, so from here it's all about Thursday's inflation print — that and next week's jobless claims are really the last two data points left before the Fed votes.

On business: Nvidia's Hugging Face deal — that platform has something like eighteen million developers and over three million models on it, and Nvidia says it won't require its own chips to use the service. Separately, Amazon's fight with the FTC turned into an actual lawsuit — the agency and twenty-two states allege Amazon quietly turned what was supposed to be a fair ad auction into something that overcharged advertisers by more than twenty billion dollars since 2019. Amazon's calling the whole thing misguided.

On technology: OpenAI's newest model, GPT-6 Astra, is now fully available through the API and to its top-tier subscribers, with a wider rollout still coming. And Anthropic still looks on track to put out its IPO paperwork sometime this week, aiming for a public listing late this month or in early October — reports suggest the company actually turned its first quarterly profit this year, which would help justify the size of that offering.

On geopolitics: Russia hit eight regions of Ukraine overnight Friday with ballistic missiles and more than a hundred and fifty drones, mostly targeting factories that supply Ukraine's defense industry. That happened even as American diplomats were shuttling between Moscow and Kyiv, and both sides agreed to a brief pause on hitting each other's capitals to make room for those talks. Nothing came of the diplomacy though — Zelensky said over the weekend that he expects the war to keep going.

And now the big one: on Iran and the strait. Saturday, Iran's Revolutionary Guard fired two ballistic missiles directly at a U.S. aircraft carrier and a destroyer near the strait — the first time in this entire war that Iran has targeted American Navy ships instead of commercial tankers. The Pentagon says both ships dodged the missiles with no one hurt, and the U.S. hit back the same day, disabling two Iranian tankers and hitting a third. The admiral in charge of U.S. forces in the region put it bluntly: shoot at two of our ships, and we'll take out three of yours. There's also an unconfirmed Iranian claim about a separate attack early this morning on an unmanned American vessel — that one's single-sourced and disputed, so treat it skeptically for now. The strait itself is still effectively closed, day one ninety and counting, but Saturday's exchange is the clearest sign yet that this conflict is shifting from a tanker war into something closer to direct confrontation with the U.S. Navy.

Sports: the Yankees lost the rubber game to the Padres Sunday, dropping the weekend series, and now sit three and a half games back of Tampa Bay, though they still hold the top wild card spot. The Knicks still haven't gotten a Josh Hart extension done. And Northwestern opened its season with a thirty-four to eighteen win over South Dakota State.
