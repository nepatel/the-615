The 6:15, for Thursday, October 22nd. Here's what moved overnight: Iran's Revolutionary Guard navy says it hit seven ships in the Persian Gulf in the last twenty-four hours — the biggest single claim of the entire crisis — and markets are treating it as real whether or not it's confirmed. Oil jumped, stocks sold off from Tokyo to London, and US futures are pointing to a rough open.

Quickly, the board: weekly jobless claims land this morning; the Fed meets Tuesday and Wednesday next week with rate-cut odds still thin, and we learned overnight that July's meeting actually held with three dissents arguing for a hike, not a cut, so this is a hawkish committee walking into a week oil just spiked; inflation data and the next growth reading both land the day after the Fed decision; OPEC Plus meets in ten days; the midterms are eleven days out; Brent and WTI are both up more than two percent this morning, Brent near one-twelve, WTI near ninety-nine; the ten-year's around five-and-an-eighth percent; the dollar's a touch softer; S&P futures are down about one percent, Nasdaq futures down almost two; the VIX jumped more than ten percent to about eighteen-and-a-half; gold, oddly, fell instead of rising; gas ticked up for the first time in a couple weeks, just under four twenty-seven a gallon; Asia closed down across the board — Tokyo off two-and-a-half percent, Hong Kong down three, Shanghai down one-and-a-half, with Europe following lower at midday; the strait's still closed to commercial shipping, day two-thirty-five, and more tankers are going dark than last issue; and in sports, the Yankees' season is over, the Knicks are oh-and-one, and Northwestern is five-and-one.

Now the lead. Iran's IRGC says its forces hit seven vessels across the Gulf overnight — four of them simultaneously, set on fire during oil transfers in the north-central Gulf, because Tehran considers the route they were using unauthorized. Three more, west of the Strait of Hormuz, reportedly hit mines and were left disabled. That's the largest number Iran has claimed in a single day since this whole thing started back in February. But here's the catch: Iran gave no ship names, no flags, no casualty numbers, nothing independently checkable. The US military is pushing back specifically on the mine part of the story, saying the lanes are clear and that missiles and drones, not mines, are what's actually been hitting ships. What the British maritime authority can actually confirm is smaller: one tanker hit by an unknown projectile southeast of Al Faw in Iraq on Tuesday evening, no injuries, no damage. Iran also warned Washington that if the US keeps moving ships through routes it doesn't approve, that could trigger strikes on American bases in the region — language we haven't heard quite this explicit before.

Markets didn't wait for confirmation. Brent and WTI both jumped more than two percent. And here's something worth noting: the two oil price feeds we track had been disagreeing on direction as of yesterday — one up, one down, at the same moment — and this morning they've converged; they still don't agree on the exact number, but at least they agree which way it's moving. Asia took it hard: Tokyo down two-and-a-half percent, Hong Kong down three, Shanghai down one-and-a-half, and China's broader tech-heavy index is now twenty percent below its June peak, which is technically a bear market. Europe was following lower by midday, and US futures are ugly — S&P futures down about one percent, Nasdaq futures down almost two, the VIX up more than ten percent. Even bitcoin gave up ground, sliding from above eighty-four thousand dollars toward eighty-two. And the one thing that doesn't fit the pattern: gold actually fell, when you'd expect it to rise on a risk-off morning like this. Nobody's got a clean explanation for that one yet.

It's not purely a Hormuz story, either. Part of the tech selloff in Asia got blamed on Tesla and Meta pulling in opposite directions on spending. Tesla beat on earnings — forty-eight cents a share, better than expected — but cut next year's capital spending plan to around twenty billion dollars, and operating income fell more than half. Meta, the same week, raised the low end of its own spending guidance. One pulling back, one leaning in — and that contrast is making investors nervous about whether all this AI spending is really being driven by demand, or just by momentum.

And the timing matters. The Fed meets next week already leaning hawkish, and a sustained oil spike heading into the inflation numbers makes any rate cut harder to justify, regardless of what today's jobless claims show. This claim also lands eleven days before the midterms and ten days before OPEC Plus meets again — and OPEC just held output flat earlier this month. A spike like this raises the political cost of holding steady a second time, though nobody inside OPEC has signaled anything yet. The watch list: does anyone independently confirm Iran's seven hits; does Brent hold above one-ten through the New York open; what claims says at eight-thirty; and whether OPEC says anything before its meeting.

On the economy: nothing else new this morning beyond what's in the lead — claims at eight-thirty is the only US data point before the Fed meets. The read worth remembering is that a hawkish Fed combined with a fresh oil shock is a bad combination for anyone hoping for a cut next week.

On business: Tesla's actual numbers, beyond the beat — revenue was basically flat year over year, net income fell forty-three percent, but free cash flow actually turned positive by about one-and-a-half billion dollars, better than expected, and margins improved from the summer. The stock gave back its earlier gains after hours, in the two-to-three percent range depending who's counting. Coca-Cola, Boeing, and GE Vernova show up on one calendar for next week, but we couldn't verify those dates against the official sources, so hold that loosely.

On technology: the real story is that capital-spending divergence between Tesla and Meta, which is feeding doubts about whether AI spending is sustainable. Bitcoin's slide fits the same risk-off mood. And still nothing to report on a new frontier AI model, a rocket launch, or a big venture round — seventh straight day with nothing confirmed there.

On Iran and the strait specifically: two things worth flagging beyond the lead. First, a new number from the International Maritime Organization — a hundred nineteen confirmed security incidents across the wider Middle East since this war started, and twenty-four sailors killed. That's a different count than the one we cited last time, covering a wider area over a longer stretch, so don't try to do math between the two. Second, more tankers are going dark than before — over half the ones we track lost their tracking signal in the last day, worse than our last reading. Diplomatically, nothing's moved; Iran's reopening offer is still sitting with no public US response that we could find.

Sports, quickly. The Yankees' season ended weeks ago, swept by Tampa Bay. The Knicks are oh-and-one after losing their opener at home to Philadelphia; they're on the road at Boston tomorrow night. Northwestern is five-and-one and hosts Rutgers Saturday. And the World Series starts tomorrow — the Rays at the Brewers, eight o'clock Eastern on Fox, Game 1 of a matchup between two franchises that have never won it all.
