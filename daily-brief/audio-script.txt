The 6:15, for Saturday, August 22nd. Here's what moved overnight: the Fed's own minutes just made room for another rate hike, right as Walmart posted its weakest comparable sales in more than six years — and Brent crude closed out the week near ninety-four dollars as the UAE cut off all trade with Iran.

Quick lap around the board. Iran sanctions are expected out of Treasury Monday, Fed Governor Lisa Cook's deadline to respond to her removal letter is Wednesday, the same day Nvidia, Salesforce, CrowdStrike and HP all report earnings alongside the government's PCE inflation, GDP, and durable goods numbers; Jackson Hole opens Thursday with new Fed Chair Kevin Warsh's first keynote Friday; Brent settled Friday just under ninety-four dollars and WTI in the high eighties, both the highest since late July; the two-year yield actually eased a touch on the week while the ten and thirty year rose, a steepening move; the S&P, Dow, and Nasdaq all closed up Friday but still logged a second straight losing week; gold pushed above forty-six hundred dollars an ounce, the highest since mid-May; and the Strait of Hormuz is now closed for its hundred seventy fifth day, with dark-tanker activity and war-risk insurance both climbing again.

Here's the lead. Wednesday's Fed minutes did something the market hadn't priced in: they showed real support for raising rates, not cutting them, and that support went well beyond the three regional Fed presidents — from Cleveland, Minneapolis, and Dallas — who formally dissented in favor of a hike. There was reportedly no discussion anywhere in those minutes of cutting rates at all. That's a sharp reversal from where this debate stood at the start of the year. And that single document moved the bond market more than any data point this week: short-term yields actually eased a little, but the ten and thirty year both moved higher, a steepening pattern that reads as the market pricing in a Fed that holds short rates up for longer while long-term inflation and fiscal worries build underneath. Gold caught that same bid, jumping to its highest level since mid-May.

Then Walmart landed right in the middle of that setup. They beat on both revenue and earnings — revenue up almost six percent, earnings well ahead of estimates — but same-store sales in the U.S. slowed to just two point six percent, the weakest pace in more than six years, and their full-year guidance came in below what Wall Street wanted. The stock fell more than nine percent in a single day, a brutal reaction to what was, on the surface, a beat. Target and TJX told a better story, still winning on value shoppers; Lowe's said big-ticket discretionary spending is getting harder to find; Home Depot held its outlook steady. Taken together, this week's five-name retail scorecard is the freshest read on the American consumer since July's retail sales miss, and it's a split verdict, not a clean one. Put it together, and the pattern from recent weeks — where soft economic data got read as good news because it meant the Fed would ease — that pattern just broke. The Fed is leaning hawkish exactly when the consumer is showing cracks, and that tension is now hanging directly over Kevin Warsh's first Jackson Hole speech as chair, next Friday.

And then there's oil, layered right on top. Brent and WTI both hit their highest levels since late July, because the Hormuz crisis just kept getting worse. The sixty-day deadline for a U.S.-Iran deal expired with nothing signed. President Trump threatened, again, to bomb Oman if it gets in the way of a deal he says he's negotiating on his own. And the UAE — a close U.S. partner that had stayed commercially engaged with Iran through this entire war — cut off all trade and financial dealings with Tehran after intercepting two Iranian ballistic missiles headed its way. A sustained push toward ninety-five-dollar Brent feeds directly into the same inflation story the Fed just leaned into. That's the thread connecting everything this week: a hawkish Fed, a cracking consumer, and an oil market pricing in a war with no end in sight, all crashing into Jackson Hole next week.

On politics: primaries in Alaska, Florida, and Wyoming reshuffled several races heading into the midterms — Mary Peltola is now taking on incumbent Senator Dan Sullivan in Alaska in what's rated a true toss-up, and Trump-endorsed Byron Donalds is heavily favored in the Florida governor's race. The House and Senate still haven't reconciled their government funding bills — one runs to early December, the other to mid-December — and that gap sits unresolved with the House out until September. And Lisa Cook's deadline to respond to the White House's renewed effort to remove her from the Fed is now just days away.

On business and technology: Tesla had its best week since May, up around five percent, after Nevada regulators approved its application to run a driverless robotaxi network in Las Vegas; Boeing's engineers voted to authorize a strike, though no walkout date is set yet; and Databricks quietly closed a five billion dollar funding round at a hundred ninety billion dollar valuation, the biggest venture deal of the month, even though it happened a week and a half ago. In AI, OpenAI shipped a cybersecurity-specialized model built specifically for finding software vulnerabilities and building exploit chains — the first frontier model explicitly built for that kind of offensive security work. And multiple outlets now confirm what was a single rumor last issue: Microsoft is lining up over three hundred thousand of its own AI chips from TSMC for next year, aiming to cut its reliance on Nvidia — though Nvidia still controls most of the advanced packaging capacity those chips need, which could slow the whole plan down.

On geopolitics: Russia carried out its worst single-day strike on Ukrainian civilians in weeks, a double-tap drone attack on a shopping center in President Zelensky's hometown that killed at least sixteen people and wounded well over a hundred, many of them children — a second drone hit the same spot half an hour later, right as rescuers arrived. Zelensky called it cynical and vile. Separately, the Xi-Trump summit in Washington is still on the calendar for late September, with export controls still the sticking point.

On Iran and Hormuz — beyond everything already covered in the lead — the bigger story is that the UAE, which had stayed commercially engaged with Iran through this entire war, just walked away from it entirely, suspending all trade and financial dealings after those missile intercepts. Iran and Oman have separately finalized the map for a safe shipping corridor through the strait, but Tehran is clear that map alone doesn't reopen anything. Iran's Parliament Speaker laid out exactly what it would take: a full end to the naval blockade, lifted oil sanctions, and unfrozen assets. Treasury Secretary Bessent says the toughest sanctions yet are coming Monday, and Iran's military has already promised a crushing response to any new threats.

Quick sports update. The Yankees beat Toronto three to one Friday night behind a two-run homer from rookie Spencer Jones, their fifth straight win — they're seventy-three and fifty-five, second in the AL East, three games back of Tampa Bay. The Knicks are in the off-season with Josh Hart's contract extension still unsigned. And Northwestern is idle until their opener September fifth against South Dakota State.
