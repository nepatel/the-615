Here's the lead. On a day with literally no trading, the story that actually moved is regulatory, not financial. Remember how last issue we flagged an unconfirmed report that Commerce had added the company Megaspeed to its trade blacklist, the same day as all the Huang drama? Turns out that was wrong — or at least, that's not what happened. The Financial Times is now reporting that Commerce actually held off on adding Megaspeed, despite four other agencies — State, Energy, Treasury, and Defense — all separately pushing for it to go on the list. That's notable because Commerce hasn't added a single company to that blacklist in over a year now, the longest stretch in eighteen years, and separately, its own investigation into Megaspeed's alleged chip smuggling reportedly got shifted over to the Justice Department after Nvidia itself made contact. Quick reminder on who Megaspeed even is, for anyone just tuning in: it's the Singapore-based firm at the center of the chip-diversion allegations, and it's also the company linked to Alice Huang, the executive whose relationship with Jensen Huang is what triggered this whole scandal in the first place, amid suspicions she's tied to Chinese intelligence. So this isn't a side issue — it's arguably the crux of the whole investigation, and it's the one piece of it where a US agency had a clear lever to pull and chose not to pull it, over the objections of four other parts of the government.

Why this matters: Jensen Huang and Nvidia's general counsel, Tim Teter, are about to walk into closed-door, transcribed interviews with House committees — Foreign Affairs, Intelligence, and the China Select Committee — on December first and second, Huang first, Teter the next day. Then Huang testifies publicly, alone, as the only witness before the Senate Banking Committee on December third. That's a deliberately staged sequence: get the closed-door, detailed version on the record privately first, then put Huang in front of cameras. The Trump administration spent last weekend personally vouching for Huang's national security standing to get him reinstated as CEO, with the president telling reporters he'd personally made the case to Nvidia's board. Now Huang's heading into hearings where the obvious question is: if this is all about national security, why did the one agency actually responsible for the blacklist — Commerce — leave the company at the center of the spying allegations off of it, overruling four other agencies in the process? That's a much harder story for the administration to control than a stock price. Expect Senator Elizabeth Warren, who's already said the reinstatement doesn't let Nvidia off the hook on export enforcement, to make this Megaspeed non-listing exhibit A in that December third hearing — it gives her a concrete, specific agency failure to point to, instead of just the messier personnel story around Huang himself.

None of this moves Nvidia's stock today, obviously, since markets are closed. But it's the frame going into Friday's thin half-session and next week's reopening, and it's worth sitting with over turkey: a reinstatement that was sold as a clean national-security exoneration now has a loose thread attached to it, a month before Huang has to answer for it in public. Watch for three things — whether Commerce or BIS says anything on the record about the Financial Times reporting before December third; whether any detail leaks out of those closed-door interviews ahead of the public hearing; and, more mundanely, just getting a real, confirmed stock price for Nvidia again once trading actually resumes, since we genuinely don't have one past Monday's close.
