The 6:15, for Sunday, September 6th. Here's what moved overnight: Iran fired ballistic missiles at a U.S. carrier group Saturday, and the U.S. hit back by sinking or disabling three more Iranian tankers.

Quick rundown of the board before we get into it. Brent's up around ninety-six, its best week since mid-July, though nobody's confirmed an exact settle. The ten-year's back up near four and three-quarters percent, the dollar firmed, stocks gave back Thursday's rally after a hot jobs number, the VIX is sitting in the mid-teens, gas is a bit above four dollars fifteen a gallon nationally. The Strait of Hormuz is still closed, day one-eighty-nine or one-ninety depending which tracker you believe, with only a handful of ships crossing a day. On sports: the Yankees split with San Diego and are sitting at eighty and sixty-one, second in the AL East but holding the top wild card; the Knicks still haven't locked up Josh Hart's extension; and Northwestern opened its season with a win.

Now the lead. Friday's jobs report flipped the week on its head. Payrolls rose a hundred sixty-two thousand in August — about triple what Wall Street expected — and the prior two months got revised up a combined fifty-five thousand, so July's scary drop now actually looks like a small gain. Unemployment held steady at four point one percent. Markets read that as good-news-is-bad-news: it puts more pressure on the Fed to stay tight. The odds of a rate hike at this month's meeting, which had collapsed to basically a coin flip Thursday after one Fed governor broke ranks and turned dovish, jumped right back up to around sixty percent by Friday's close. Yields rose, and stocks gave back Thursday's gains — the Dow fell about a quarter of a percent, the S&P and Nasdaq similar. So in three trading days, the market went from pricing a two-thirds chance of a hike, to a coin flip, to sixty percent again. That fight inside the Fed is very much alive, and it all comes down to the inflation report due in five days, which the dovish governor himself named as the thing that would change his mind.

Then Saturday, the war caught up with the talk. Iran fired multiple ballistic missiles at a U.S. Navy carrier and a destroyer in the Gulf. Both ships dodged them — no American casualties, according to Central Command. Washington hit back within hours, disabling or destroying three more Iranian oil tankers — one off Kharg Island, one near Jask, one in the Gulf of Oman, whose crew had to abandon ship. The admiral running Central Command put it bluntly: shoot at two of our ships, and we'll take out three of yours. The Defense Secretary said the same thing in blunter terms. Iran's military command threatened attacks "more severe than before," and separately claimed — without U.S. confirmation — that it hit three more tankers and three other U.S.-linked vessels overnight.

Here's what makes this land hard: less than a day before all that happened, the President had called this six-week war "small potatoes," and the Vice President said he wouldn't even call it a war. Nobody in the administration has yet squared that talk with a carrier group actually dodging live missiles. Oil was already up around nine percent for the week heading into Friday's close, its best week since mid-July, purely on the earlier round of strikes — and Saturday's escalation happened after that price was set, so none of it is priced in yet. With OPEC-plus meeting today to set next quarter's output, and that decisive inflation report landing Thursday, a Monday spike in oil would hit at almost the worst possible moment for the Fed's math. That's the setup heading into the week: a jobs number that just turned the Fed more hawkish, a war the administration calls basically over that the military is actively fighting, and an inflation report eight days out that both camps inside the Fed have now said is the thing that decides everything.

A quick word on how murky the on-the-ground numbers still are, because it matters for how much weight to put on any of this. Nobody publishes an official running count of strikes since this war started back in February — the two trackers that try both run into the hundreds, but neither is a government source. Ship traffic through the strait is down to a handful of vessels a day against a normal baseline in the dozens, though the two firms that track it by satellite don't agree with each other, and don't agree with what U.S. officials are claiming either — Washington's numbers run much higher, because they're counting small craft the commercial trackers can't see. And war-risk insurance for a single supertanker passage is now quoted around forty times the pre-war rate, roughly ten million dollars a trip, though that figure traces back to one tracking site rather than a named insurer. All of which is to say: the direction is unambiguous — this is worse, not better, than the picture painted as recently as Friday — but precise numbers should be treated with real caution this morning.

On politics: a federal judge in Boston blocked a new Postal Service rule that would have required election officials to get advance approval before mailing ballots, calling it unworkable since ballots are already going out. Melania Trump and more than forty-five Republican lawmakers are skipping the party's midterm convention in Dallas next week, right after a new poll put the President's approval at thirty-three percent, a low point for this term. New Hampshire's Democratic Senate primary, coming up Tuesday, is reportedly tightening.

On the economy: with the jobs report already behind us, the next big thing is the August inflation report Thursday morning — expectations call for a moderate reading, though at least one economist thinks it comes in hotter. The Treasury sells new three- and ten-year debt this week too.

On business: Tesla's low-key robotaxi relaunch in Austin left a lot of questions unanswered and the stock dropped nearly six percent Friday; regulators opened a safety inquiry the same day. Blue Owl Capital is planning to spin off a data-center real estate company. Nigeria cleared its biggest IPO ever, a refinery deal worth about a billion six.

On technology: OpenAI says its newest model is now available to all its paying users after a rocky rollout it apologized for. Microsoft put out a new speech-recognition model it says is the best and cheapest around. Crusoe, the AI cloud company, raised over three billion dollars at a thirty-billion-dollar valuation. And an Abu Dhabi AI firm is looking at giving Americans majority ownership just to keep its access to Nvidia's chips.

On geopolitics beyond Iran: American envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner met Putin in Moscow for hours on Saturday — the first Moscow trip of this new diplomatic push — and both sides agreed to pause strikes on each other's capitals for the duration. Those same envoys showed up in Kyiv on Sunday with what the White House calls a concrete proposal — more details promised in the coming weeks — while Zelensky repeated that Ukraine won't give up territory or its NATO ambitions. But the pause didn't hold everywhere: a Russian missile strike killed five people well outside the capital zone on Saturday, and Ukrainian officials counted well over eight hundred strikes on dozens of towns in one southern region alone, with fighting still concentrated around Pokrovsk. On a smaller note, international inspectors did broker a local ceasefire at the Zaporizhzhia nuclear plant so technicians could fix damaged power lines. Separately, Israel struck Hezbollah targets in southern Lebanon over the weekend after alleging drone launches, killing at least four people — a new and separate flare-up from Gaza or Iran.

Sports scores: the Yankees lost three-two Friday, then won five-one Saturday, both against San Diego — they're eighty and sixty-one now, second in the AL East but holding the top wild card spot, with the series finale still to be played. The Knicks still haven't signed Josh Hart to an extension. And Northwestern opened its football season with a thirty-four to eighteen win over South Dakota State.
