Lead

INFLATION · RATES · OIL

Two straight hot inflation prints, and the bond market shrugged — Treasury yields fell through both

September CPI and PPI both ran hotter than consensus this week, and both say the same thing about why: a war-driven energy shock, not demand, is doing the damage. CPI, released Wednesday, rose 3.7% year-over-year against a 3.6% consensus, with the 0.6% monthly gain matching estimates; energy prices jumped 5.2% on the month and are up 20.4% year-over-year, and gasoline alone accounted for more than half of the headline's monthly rise, per the BLS. [fact] PPI, released Thursday, accelerated sharply: final demand rose 1.3% month-over-month and 6.3% year-over-year — up hard from August's already-hot 0.4%/5.4% reading — while the core measure (less food and energy) rose 0.7% monthly and 5.0% year-over-year. [fact, BLS primary source] Equities did not like the CPI print: the S&P 500 closed Wednesday at 7,718.94, down 1.11%, the Nasdaq fell 1.88%, and eight of eleven S&P sectors declined, led by a 2.6% drop in information technology [fact, single-sourced via secondary market blogs, exact index levels not cross-confirmed by a wire service].

The mechanism tying the two prints together is the Houthi campaign against Saudi oil infrastructure covered in this brief's last two issues: strikes on Aramco's Dhahran headquarters and on Riyadh's main airport have pushed crude and refined-product costs higher globally, and that's showing up directly in CPI's energy line and in the kind of diesel-and-goods surge that drove August's PPI print before this week's acceleration. [read] What's not behaving the way a textbook would predict is the bond market. Both prints came in hot and accelerating, which should push yields up on reduced rate-cut expectations or a growth/inflation risk premium — instead, the entire curve has fallen for three consecutive sessions: the 10-year eased from 5.21% Tuesday to 5.19% on CPI day to 5.10% on PPI day; the 2-year from 4.79% to 4.82% to 4.77%; the 30-year from 5.56% to 5.52% to 5.43% (Treasury.gov). [fact]

Read: the bond market appears to be treating this inflation overshoot as a transient, supply-side shock tied to a war rather than a demand problem the Fed needs to fight — consistent with prediction-market odds of an October 27-28 rate cut sitting unmoved in the 15%-22% range through both releases, which is itself a signal that almost no one expects the Fed to react to these prints in either direction. [read] The more straightforward read is simpler: a direct-hit campaign against the world's largest oil exporter's capital and headquarters complex is also a risk-off event, and duration demand from that fear can swamp an inflation-driven selloff in the same two sessions. [read] Those two reads aren't mutually exclusive, and they point the same direction for now: both argue against a Fed reaction, just for different reasons — one because the inflation is transient, the other because growth risk is rising faster than price risk. The distinction matters for how long the rally in duration lasts. If the "transient shock" framing is right, yields should snap back once the Houthi campaign cools and energy pass-through fades from the data, probably within one or two more CPI cycles. If it's really a growth/war scare, the rally has further to run, and equities — already down hard on CPI day — have more room to fall than the one-day move so far suggests.

Oil itself isn't confirming further escalation this morning — Brent at $105.72 and WTI at $95.84 are both down slightly and off this week's highs — which argues for the risk-off/transient-shock read over a renewed supply-shortage one, at least as of this morning's price action. The Brent-WTI spread has also narrowed to roughly $9.88 from the ~$11 level flagged two issues ago, which on its own would normally read as the Gulf-specific war premium easing relative to the broader crude complex — though a single morning's narrowing, pre-settle, isn't enough to call that a trend. Watch: whether Saudi Arabia's promised "firm" response to the Houthis widens the war in a way that forces the "transient" framing to break, whether the curve keeps falling or stabilizes once today's settle prices are in, and whether October's CPI print, when it lands next month, still shows energy doing all the work.

Politics

Midterm positioning firmed modestly this week without a single decisive data point. Polling averages reviewed Oct. 13 show Democrats favored to retake the House and within range on the Senate — a shift analysts say would have looked unlikely at the start of this cycle — but the same coverage flags a degraded polling environment this cycle: a proliferation of low-quality and AI-generated polls, at least one fraudulent pollster exposed, and thinning state-level polling as local outlets cut survey budgets. [fact/flag, single-outlet framing of the averages] All 435 House seats, 35 of 100 Senate seats and 39 governorships are on the Nov. 3 ballot. Voters are polling as most focused on the Iran war, immigration, tariffs, gas prices and affordability generally — a list that puts this week's energy-driven CPI print directly in the midterm conversation. [fact] The party controlling the White House traditionally loses ground at the midterm; Republicans hold narrow majorities in both chambers heading in, which is the baseline the Oct. 13 polling average is measured against. Iran & Hormuz

Beyond the Board's strike tally, this week's two most significant new Hormuz data points both carry named methodology, as this brief requires. Kpler's ship-tracking puts crude export volumes through the strait at 8.5 million barrels per day for the week ended Oct. 12 — about 40% below the pre-war baseline — the freshest throughput read available this run, replacing the now-stale PortWatch transit count carried in prior issues. [NEW, fact] Separately, CNN's analysis flags Oct. 11 as the single worst day of the war for shipping incidents: six commercial vessels reportedly struck in and around the strait in 24 hours, the highest count since fighting began. [NEW, fact] The IMO's running monthly tally — more than 20 vessels struck in Hormuz, the Persian Gulf and the Gulf of Oman since Oct. 1, two sailors missing, over a dozen injured — provides the casualty context neither Kpler's flow data nor CNN's single-day count capture; none of the three should be averaged or differenced against the others, as they measure different things over different windows. [fact]

The Oct. 14 container-ship sinking remains a live dispute over mechanism, not just source count: Iran's military says the vessel struck a mine, while maritime security firm Marisks says it has no independent evidence to support that account, leaving the cause of the sinking unresolved as of this writing. [fact/flag] No update on Araghchi's promised "definitive" response to Washington's nuclear counterproposal — flagged as overdue in issue No. 25 — was located this run; it remains a gap, not a resolution. [flag] Stale background not refreshed this run and therefore not repeated in full: Breakwave's hull-value war-risk indications (last published Sept. 8) and Signal Ocean's VLCC-positioning data (last published mid-July) — both over a month old and carried forward only as context in prior issues.

Net effect on the physical oil market: Kpler's 8.5m bpd read, if sustained, is a genuine supply reduction rather than a pricing anomaly — roughly 5.5m-6m bpd of flow missing versus a normal ~14m bpd Hormuz baseline — and it sits alongside, not in place of, the shipping-incident risk the IMO and CNN figures capture. A trader pricing Gulf crude off Kpler's number alone would be pricing a real barrel shortfall; one pricing off the incident count alone would be pricing insurance and diversion risk on top of that shortfall. Both are live in the market simultaneously, which is this brief's standing reason for refusing to net them into one figure. [read]
