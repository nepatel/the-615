Lead

HORMUZ

Iran and Oman float an interim Hormuz corridor — and Tehran immediately says that isn’t the same as reopening.

Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi and his Omani counterpart, Badr al-Busaidi, used a joint statement Tuesday to disclose an “interim framework” aimed at resuming shipping through the Strait of Hormuz — the most concrete diplomatic movement of the five-month crisis. The mechanism: a temporary joint maritime corridor roughly seven nautical miles wide, paired with a mine-clearance project to restore safe navigation. Under the proposal, inbound traffic entering the Gulf from the Gulf of Oman would run entirely through Iranian waters, while outbound traffic would cross both Iranian and Omani waters — a routing choice that keeps Tehran, not a neutral third party, in physical control of the corridor Muscat is brokering. The two sides agreed to continue technical talks over the next 30 to 60 days toward a permanent corridor, a framework for administering the strait, and a mechanism for information exchange, traffic management and maritime security services, and said they would also pursue parallel talks with other Gulf states bordering the waterway.

Oil’s reaction was immediate and unambiguous: Brent fell for a third consecutive session, down 2.68% to $86.20 and WTI down 2.71% to $80.13 as of Wednesday’s pre-open, extending Monday’s and Tuesday’s declines as the crisis-premium unwind that began with Monday’s Bessent sanctions rollout (see prior issue) continued on top of Tuesday’s Iran-Oman news. (Read: markets are pricing this as the first credible step toward de-escalating the physical blockade, not just another round of the rhetorical sparring that has characterized most of the past five months.)

But the read and the fact diverge here, and the gap is the story. Iran’s deputy foreign minister said Tuesday, in the same news cycle as the joint statement, that the Oman understanding does not mean the strait is reopening — it remains, in his words, “militarily closed.” That is a direct, same-day hedge from Tehran’s own government against the interpretation oil markets are trading on. Nothing in the transit data supports a reopening yet either: single-day counts remain in the low single digits against an 85-vessel-per-day pre-crisis baseline (see Hormuz counters), and Lloyd’s List’s slower monthly aggregation still has August running near 12 transits a day versus a roughly 130-per-day pre-war norm. What actually changed Tuesday is narrower than the oil-price move suggests — Iran agreed to talk about a corridor under its own control, with a 30-to-60-day runway before any of the substantive questions (permanent administration, security services, who polices compliance) get resolved.

The second-order read is about sequencing, not substance. This diplomatic opening lands the same week as Monday’s Treasury sanctions rollout (“Operation Economic Outcast,” detailed in the prior issue) and Bessent’s explicit refusal to set an enforcement deadline for the countries most exposed to secondary sanctions. Read together, Washington’s coercive track and Oman’s diplomatic channel are now running in parallel rather than in sequence — and Oman, notably, is the one Gulf state that has maintained a functioning line to Tehran throughout the crisis, which is precisely why it is the one able to produce a document Washington could not extract through sanctions alone. Whether that channel produces something durable in the next 30-60 days, or simply gives Iran a talking point to manage the sanctions pressure while the “militarily closed” status persists on the water, is the question the next several issues will need to track — not this week’s oil-price pop, which is already reversing gains built on a geopolitical premium that predates any actual change in enforcement.

Politics

Lisa Cook’s deadline to respond to Trump’s renewed removal effort lands today, per Deputy Chief of Staff Dan Scavino’s Aug. 5 letter requiring a response through the Director of Presidential Personnel by Aug. 26. Her attorneys, Abbe Lowell and Norm Eisen, have already signaled the substance of her response: the mortgage-fraud allegations are “as baseless now as they were a year ago,” and there is “no valid cause for removing Governor Cook” under Supreme Court precedent. (Continuity, unchanged stance.) No outcome — a removal action, a further extension, or no action at all — was confirmed as of press time. (Flag.) This letter was drafted specifically to satisfy the notice-and-opportunity-to-respond requirement the Supreme Court set in June when it blocked Trump’s first removal attempt; the Court has since agreed to hear the underlying removal-authority question on the merits, with argument set for January, so today’s deadline runs on a separate, faster track than that eventual ruling. Massachusetts holds its state primary Sept. 1 (mail-ballot request deadline was Aug. 25); South Carolina votes Sept. 9 and Delaware Sept. 15. The House-Senate government funding gap — the Senate’s continuing resolution runs to Dec. 11, the House’s own version only to Dec. 4 — remains unresolved and unchanged since the last issue.

Business

Dick’s Sporting Goods posted its worst single-day stock move on record Tuesday, falling roughly 29% after missing both lines — EPS of $3.53 versus a $3.76 consensus, revenue of $5.59B versus $5.65B — and cutting full-year GAAP EPS guidance to $10.94–$11.94 from $13.27–$14.27. The miss was concentrated in the newly acquired Foot Locker business, where comparable sales fell 3.6% amid heavy athletic-footwear discounting and bloated inventory; the core Dick’s banner still grew comps 4.9%, helped by 2026 FIFA World Cup-related demand. (Read: this is the clearest evidence yet that the Foot Locker acquisition is a drag rather than an accretive bolt-on, at least near-term.) Geopolitics

Russia and Ukraine traded another heavy overnight exchange. Russia launched Iskander-M ballistic missiles, Kh-31P anti-radar missiles and roughly 150 drones at targets across Sumy, Kharkiv, Zaporizhzhia, Dnipropetrovsk, Kherson, Kyiv, Odesa, Chernihiv, Mykolaiv and Poltava, plus occupied Donetsk. Ukraine struck two Russian oil refineries in response: the Afipsky refinery in Krasnodar, where falling drone debris killed two people and injured two more and sparked a fire, and the Novoshakhtinsky refinery in Rostov, which suspended processing after the attack. Both strikes extend the energy-infrastructure exchange that Putin, on Sunday, characterized as Ukraine opening “Pandora’s box” (see prior issue) — a framing that has not shifted since. Xi’s Sept. 24 Washington summit remains on the calendar with export controls the unresolved friction point; no new developments surfaced this run.
