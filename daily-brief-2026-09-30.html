Lead

IRAN REJECTS, MARKETS DON'T

Trump calls Iran's Hormuz plan unacceptable on camera; crude sells off anyway

Trump delivered his rejection of Iran's seven-day Hormuz-reopening plan straight to cameras Monday: "I'd like to make a deal, too. But that deal would not be acceptable." [fact] That's a harder public line than Sunday's account from Waltz, and it landed a day before Araghchi's Tuesday window for an "official" written response through Qatari mediators — a window that passed without one. As of 7:20p ET Tuesday, Araghchi was still describing himself as "waiting for an official response from America through the intermediary," and Iran's security chief Rezaei said Trump is "trapped in a quagmire where he can neither negotiate nor fight." [fact] Both governments are now negotiating in public while the actual channel — Doha — stays closed to outside view; nothing here confirms whether Trump's on-camera line is policy or leverage ahead of a written answer.

Crude did not treat it as escalation. Brent traded at $97.23 (+1.12% intraday, TradingEconomics, ~6:10a ET) and WTI at $90.31 (+1.04%) this morning — both roughly 8%-9% below the $105-108 Brent/$93-94 WTI range this brief carried Tuesday, with no change in the physical Hormuz counters (transits still effectively zero, the vessel-holding count last confirmed at 233 Monday, war-risk premia unchanged in the 7.5%-10% hull-value band). [fact] That's the mechanism worth sitting with: a public rejection that reads harder than anything Waltz said last week, delivered into a market that had already priced a chunk of headline risk into a Strait that remains shut, and the price is unwinding rather than extending. Two reads, both live: either the desk is treating Trump's line as negotiating posture rather than a policy shift — consistent with Monday's Axios/CNN report that he'd privately accept sanctions relief after "concrete progress" — or the move has little to do with Iran at all, and is a standalone reversal after a crisis premium that had run ahead of the actual disruption. [read] The counters didn't move, so this is a repricing of risk, not new information about the Strait itself.

What didn't move with it: the long end of the curve. Thirty-year Treasuries closed Tuesday at 5.59% — a fresh high since 2002 — and the 10-year held in the 5.23%-5.26% band that's been a 2007-level floor since mid-September. [fact] If cheaper energy were the dominant story for inflation expectations, duration would likely be rallying alongside it; it isn't, which points to term-premium and fiscal-supply pressure carrying more weight in this move than the energy channel does — October's Treasury calendar alone calls for a recommended $44B in 7-year and $39B in 10-year supply (TBAC, Aug. 5), a load the market has to absorb regardless of where crude settles. [fact] Equities read the oil drop as the good kind of surprise — S&P futures +0.06%, gold rebounding off Monday's seven-week low — while the VIX ticked up to 16.01 from Monday's confirmed 14.87 close, a small but real two-session rise that sits oddly next to a green futures tape. [read]

All of this lands hours before the August PCE print at 8:30a ET — the last inflation read before the Oct. 27-28 FOMC, where prediction markets (Kalshi/Polymarket average, Sept. 26) still price roughly a 64% chance of a second 2026 hike. [fact] Bank consensus going in: core PCE +0.2% m/m, ~3.0% y/y, a touch softer than July's 0.27% pace. [fact, forecast] A hot print against falling energy costs would be the most hawkish combination on the table this morning — it would mean the disinflation the oil market is pricing isn't showing up in the data yet, and duration's refusal to rally would look prescient rather than stale.

Politics

China's rare-earth export-control suspension — the 12-month pause covering samarium, gadolinium, terbium, dysprosium, lutetium, scandium and yttrium — is due to expire Nov. 10, five weeks before the broader Busan trade truce's Jan. 10 renewal date. [NEW, fact] Six months into the pause, Beijing has shown "little sign" of easing the underlying licensing regime, per Benchmark Minerals reporting, and added MP Materials and USA Rare Earth to its entity list in June, barring any transfer of Chinese-origin dual-use items to either firm. [fact] The mismatched deadlines put a rare-earth-specific flashpoint back on the calendar five weeks before the broader tariff truce needs renewing — a sequencing problem distinct from, but structurally similar to, the one stalling Hormuz talks (Lead).

Business

Credit markets are not pricing the Treasury market's stress: investment-grade corporate spreads sit near 80bp over Treasuries (BBB ~100bp, AA ~50bp) — levels last this tight since 1998 — while high-yield spreads hold near 285bp, with BB-rated credit near its tightest of the cycle and CCC the one segment showing genuine widening. [fact] That combination — record-tight IG spreads against a 30-year Treasury yield at a 24-year high (Lead) — says the multi-decade move in long rates is a Treasury-supply and term-premium story, not a corporate-credit-risk one; investors are still willing to hold corporate paper at a historically thin premium to a benchmark they're demanding more yield from. [read] Thursday's Nike print and today's Micron numbers (Technology) are the first real test of whether that credit-market calm matches what shows up in actual corporate results.

[continuity, fact] Separately, the blockade's economic toll on Iran itself has deepened: crude and condensate loadings averaged just 220,000-260,000 b/d in August, versus 1.7-2.0 million b/d a year earlier — an over-80% collapse — and National Iranian Oil Co. output could fall to 1.2-1.3 million b/d if the blockade holds, per outside analyst estimates. [fact] Floating storage tied to Iranian crude fell from roughly 90 million barrels in mid-July to about 29 million by mid-September, and 27 sanctioned tankers linked to Iran's trade sit idle in ballast off Sri Lanka, unable to return to port. [fact] That erosion is the pressure both Waltz and Araghchi are negotiating against (Lead) — the longer the Strait stays effectively shut, the more it costs Tehran specifically, which cuts against reading today's stalemate as free optionality for Iran.
