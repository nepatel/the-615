Lead

A WARTIME FIRST, AND OIL TALKS BACK

Hormuz's deadliest strike of the war sends oil back above $101, unwinding Monday's divergence — and splitting the bond market into two stories at once

The Strait of Hormuz campaign recorded its worst casualty toll since the war began Feb. 28: the LR2 tanker On Peace was struck by an unidentified projectile Tuesday, injuring 12 of its 19 crew — 11 of them Indian nationals — with Oman's military airlifting 10 to hospital. It came two days after the Greek-flagged LNG carrier Maran Gas Mystras was hit near the waterline Sunday while leaving the strait; its 30-member crew was unhurt and the ship continued under its own power toward Fujairah. UKMTO has logged at least one attack a day in the strait or the Gulf of Aden since Oct. 2 and holds its threat assessment at "severe"; no attribution has been made for either strike.

Crude reversed hard on the news. Brent's December ICE contract traded at $101.83 as of 5:50a ET, up 1.24% against Monday's $100.58 settle; WTI was at $89.87, up 0.48%, with no settle yet. That is the mirror image of Monday's pattern: issue No. 23 of this brief reported both benchmarks falling even as UKMTO disclosed four separate tanker strikes, because Mideast exports were topping pre-war levels on four of the prior seven days and Saudi Aramco had just deepened its November Asian discount. The decoupling didn't survive a second consecutive day of escalation, now with the war's first double-digit casualty count — oil is pricing the headline again, not the flow data, and the flow data hasn't actually updated: straits.live's board still has transits frozen at four vessels on Oct. 4 against an 85/day baseline, lagging as it has for days. Oddly, the same dashboard's dark-tanker screen — vessels running AIS-dark to dodge the war-risk premium — came in at 76 of 195 ships screened, below its own 87.9 five-day average for the first time in recent issues, though the operator itself cautions the number is "a floor on hulls we stopped receiving, not a verified evasion tally." War-risk premia held flat at roughly $10 million per VLCC transit, about 30 times the pre-crisis rate.

The oil move dragged rates with it. The 10-year rose to 5.321% (+1.01%) and the 30-year to 5.702%, within a point of its 52-week high, both climbing across the same stretch of the morning that Brent did. That's the second-order consequence worth sitting with: Friday's payrolls miss put the Fed's Oct. 28 decision on ice — hike odds sit at 16%, barely changed from Monday's 17% — but an oil-driven pop in headline inflation expectations is now pulling the long end up even as the front end stays anchored to a dovish read on the Fed, a steepening dynamic that wasn't present Monday. December hike odds, meanwhile, firmed to 73% from roughly 65% two sessions ago — consistent with a market pricing the tightening case back in for year-end precisely because oil has stopped cooperating with the disinflation story. Equities are taking note: Tuesday's record S&P and Nasdaq closes gave way to red futures this morning (S&P -0.11%, Nasdaq 100 -0.39%) as the MSCI Asia-Pacific complex fell on tech weakness in South Korea and Hong Kong (Nikkei -0.76%, Hang Seng -0.62% at the close), with Europe following lower (FTSE -0.47%, DAX -1.18% intraday).

None of this resolves the diplomatic track. Qatari mediators met Iranian FM Araghchi in New York Monday to relay a US counter-offer to Tehran's seven-day reopening plan; Trump has called the original proposal "unacceptable," and Iran says the strait stays shut "until the US meets its conditions." No outcome from the New York channel is confirmed as of writing. The question for the rest of the week is whether Oct. 14's CPI print, rather than Friday's jobs report, ends up being the data point that decides whether the Fed's October hold survives contact with an oil market that just stopped playing along.

Iran & Hormuz

The casualty count above sits alongside a broader incident count: straits.live's chronology logged 63 total indexed events in the 24 hours to this morning, a figure the operator itself flags as single-sourced. UKMTO's southern transit corridor remains open to two-way traffic even as the agency holds its threat assessment at "severe"; the dashboard's own closure read is "closed to commercial shipping due to war-risk insurance" as of 6:17a, but that status is contested on the ground — Iran maintains the strait stays shut until the US meets its conditions, while the Financial Times reports captains are being paid $100,000 a month to transit anyway. Crisis Pressure reverted to a qualitative "extreme" reading and Escalation Forecast to "elevated," both flat over 24 hours — the second format flip in as many issues (numeric 88/100 and 58/100 in issue No. 23, qualitative bands before and now again), which this brief treats as a vendor-side inconsistency rather than a real signal.

Diplomatically, nothing has moved past what the Lead describes: Qatari mediators carried a US counter-offer to Araghchi in New York Monday; no text or outcome has been confirmed. Transits remain frozen at the straits.live board's last recorded reading of four vessels on Oct. 4 against an 85/day pre-crisis baseline — the feed still hasn't posted a newer day — while AIS-visible vessels holding position stand at 151, down from 191 Monday but within the recent 89-285 range this brief treats as noise rather than trend. Taken together: the attack rate and its severity are both rising, the physical-flow data is too stale to say whether exports are still outrunning the strike count the way they were through Monday, and this morning's oil move is a bet that the casualty headline matters more than the stale transit print — at least until straits.live's feed catches up.
