Lead

MARKETS / NVIDIA

Monday's Nvidia-led rally gives back more than half its gains in a day and a half, as a senator's rebuke and a symbolic blacklist action reframe Huang's reinstatement as unfinished business

Nvidia fell 5.86% Tuesday to $155.38, the fourth session in five to pull back from Monday's 31.48% surge on Jensen Huang's reinstatement, and extended the slide into Wednesday's premarket — down another 5.05% to $147.54 by 5:17 a.m. ET. Together the two sessions have erased more than half of Monday's gain. The broader market followed it down Tuesday: the S&P 500 slipped roughly 0.5% to about 7,201 and the Nasdaq Composite dropped 1.48% to 23,733.09, while the Dow rose 0.85% to a first-ever close above 50,000 — a rotation into defensive value names rather than a vote of confidence in the rally's durability, as chip and AI-infrastructure stocks gave back Monday's leadership. Futures extended the retreat Wednesday morning: S&P 500 futures were down 1.39%, Nasdaq-100 futures off 2.34%, and Dow futures down 0.76% in one feed, with a second feed showing smaller but directionally identical moves (flag, unreconciled feeds) — nerves ahead of Wednesday's 8:30 a.m. data cluster are one plausible driver, though this brief located no single confirmed catalyst beyond the unwind itself. The VIX — last confirmed at a stale 24.52 as of Nov. 18 — jumped to 28.80 Wednesday morning, up 9.25% on the day, the clearest same-day signal yet that Monday's move was a short-covering spike rather than a repriced consensus.

Asia absorbed the reversal harder than Wall Street did. South Korea's Kospi, which had jumped 6.32% Monday on the chip-rally spillover, sank 6.16% Wednesday — arithmetically erasing almost exactly Monday's gain in a single session. Japan's Nikkei fell a further 2.62% to 57,110.00, its second straight loss after Tuesday's 3.91% drop. India's Sensex, which has essentially no direct chip-supply-chain exposure to the Nvidia story, rose 1.82% in the same window — a reminder that this is a chip-specific unwind rather than a broad global risk-off move, even as it reads that way from inside the Nasdaq. This is precisely the "second-day fade" flagged as the thing to watch in yesterday's issue, and it is now two full sessions deep rather than one.

The reinstatement is also drawing direct Congressional pushback rather than just scrutiny. Sen. Kevin Cramer (R-N.D.), a Senate Banking Committee member who had called on Huang to resign before the board reversed itself, told CNBC Tuesday that the board — not Huang — now owns the reputational and security risk: "They can no longer say it was Jensen's fault," adding that Huang might need "a babysitter" and urging Congress to "immediately pass legislation to limit AI chip sales to China." That call lands alongside a concrete enforcement action: Commerce's Bureau of Industry and Security confirmed this week it added Singapore's Megaspeed International and six affiliates — including Alibaba's Singapore subsidiary, Apex Enterprise Solutions — to the Entity List over the risk that advanced Nvidia chips were diverted to China, resolving yesterday's flag that no primary BIS release had been located. But multiple former officials quoted across outlets say the listing omits the foreign-direct-product rule that would reach Taiwan-manufactured Nvidia chips routed through Megaspeed's affiliates, making the designation largely symbolic rather than a binding chokepoint — and Megaspeed itself denies wrongdoing.

Watch: whether Wednesday's data cluster — PCE, GDP's second estimate, jobless claims and durable goods at 8:30 a.m., new home sales at 10 a.m., the Beige Book at 2 p.m. — gives the market a reason to stabilize before Thursday's holiday closure and Friday's half session, or instead compounds the chip-led selloff; and whether a sitting Senate Banking Committee member now publicly breaking with the board's rationale complicates Huang's Dec. 1–3 Congressional calendar before it even starts.

Technology

Beyond the Lead: the Megaspeed Entity List action is the first primary-sourced confirmation this brief has had of that designation, closing out a flag carried since issue No. 42; it covers Megaspeed International and six affiliates in Singapore, Malaysia and Thailand, including Alibaba's Apex Enterprise Solutions, and requires a license for any export to them. Commerce opened its underlying investigation in 2024; Nvidia itself barred sales to Megaspeed in June 2025; the Justice Department now runs a separate criminal inquiry. One analysis reported alongside the listing expects license applications tied to the newly listed firms to be denied as a matter of policy going forward, which is the practical bite even if the foreign-direct-product gap limits the designation's reach into Taiwan-fabricated chips. Huang's three-stage Congressional calendar — Dec. 1 House Foreign Affairs plus two additional committees, Dec. 2 for GC Tim Teter, Dec. 3 for Huang's first public testimony before Senate Banking — stands unchanged from issue No. 42, but Cramer's Tuesday comments (see Politics, Lead) mean Huang now walks into that Dec. 3 hearing facing at least one committee member who has publicly rejected the board's national-security rationale for his own reinstatement, not merely the export-control record the hearing was originally framed around. No frontier AI-model release, VC mega-round, or rocket launch cleared this brief's sourcing bar as new in the prior 24-48 hours; OpenAI's reported ~$15B raise at a $1.25 trillion valuation and Thinking Machines Lab's $5B seed round were both reported in the first half of November and are not repeated here as current.

The most recent confirmed attack remains UKMTO Warning 212-26 (Nov. 18, tanker struck near Khasab, Oman, crew safe); this brief found no fresher UKMTO, Lloyd's List, PortWatch or Kpler figure despite a dedicated search this run, so last issue's transit and war-risk counters are not re-quoted as current (see Board). Oman and Qatar remain the mediation channels; no direct US-Iran meeting readout located this run. The underlying reason this brief declines to net the various Hormuz counters against each other bears repeating: UKMTO logs individual incident reports in near-real time but does not count barrels; PortWatch and Kpler estimate flows and transit counts from satellite and AIS tracking on different lag schedules and different vessel-classification rules; and war-risk premia set by insurers price forward risk rather than measure past activity. A rising transit count and a rising premium can be simultaneously true and are not in tension — they describe different markets reacting to the same conflict on different clocks.
