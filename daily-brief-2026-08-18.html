Lead

60 DAYS, NO DEAL

The US-Iran deal clock ran out Monday. Trump answered by threatening to bomb Oman.

The 60-day window Washington gave itself to convert June's ceasefire memorandum into a lasting Strait of Hormuz settlement expired Monday with no agreement and no extension. [NEW] Asked directly whether he'd extend the June 18 MoU, Trump said "No," then told Fox News's Trey Yingst that if Oman "gets in the way" of reopening the strait, "we'll bomb the s*** out of them" — a threat aimed at a longtime US partner that has spent the crisis trying to broker exactly the reopening Washington says it wants. [fact: quotes dated 8/17] [read: naming Oman, not Iran, as the obstacle is the tell — Washington's frustration has shifted from Tehran's intransigence to the mediator's inability to deliver terms Iran will actually honor, seven weeks after the MoU promised a settled outcome]

Iran's Foreign Ministry offered a sliver of the deal Trump wants credit for: spokesman Esmaeil Baghaei said Tehran and Oman had reached "an understanding...regarding the map of the transit route" — the same routing-only progress reported Friday, now confirmed from Tehran's side. [DEVELOPING] But the IRGC moved to close off the backchannel Trump has repeatedly cited, calling his claims of informal talks with the Guard "fantasies caused by delusions" and denying any US contact. [NEW] The split — a civilian ministry conceding technical progress while the military wing denies talking to Washington at all — reads as the same factional gap this brief has tracked all month, not a new rupture. [read] The Oman threat itself is a break in kind, not just degree: this war has run through months of strikes, sanctions and rhetoric aimed at Iran directly, but Trump had not previously threatened to bomb a third-party Gulf state acting as mediator. Oman has hosted the only sustained US-Iran channel of the entire crisis; making the mediator a target, even rhetorically, raises the question of who is left to carry messages between Washington and Tehran if Muscat steps back. [read]

The shipping numbers back the stalemate rather than any thaw. Two trackers converge on the same collapse: Kpler counted 5 vessels transiting Saturday and zero Sunday, against a 31-vessel prior weekend and a pre-war baseline near 130/day; MarineTraffic's daily count shows crossings falling 19.5% week-on-week, from 19 on Aug. 11 to 3 on Aug. 16. [NEW] Neither captures the last 24 hours: a vessel was struck by an unidentified projectile at 09:48 UTC Tuesday with a crew member injured, per straits.live's tracker — the latest in a strike pattern Iran hasn't paused despite, or perhaps because of, the deadline pressure. [NEW, flag: single-sourced]

Oil is the cleanest read on how the market is pricing this. Brent settled Monday at $90.87, up 2.7% on the day, and is bid to $91.02 (+0.17%, 6:18a ET) Tuesday morning — within a couple dollars of the year's highs and up roughly $8 from three weeks ago. WTI is messier post-rollover: the front-month contract shifted from September to October on Aug. 16, and the freshest quote available shows $84.13 (+0.47%, 5:41a ET), putting the Brent-WTI spread near $6.89, though Monday's session move was reported elsewhere as a smaller +0.6% to roughly $85 — treat the exact WTI level as still settling out post-roll. [flag: WTI unreconciled]

Equities did not shrug this one off the way they did the "territory of the United States" rhetoric a week ago. S&P futures were down 0.48% to 7,731.50 and Nasdaq 100 futures down a sharper 1.20% to 29,735.50 as of 6:09a ET, delayed data — a genuine risk-off move rather than the grind-higher-anyway pattern that held through last week's data misses. The VIX's Monday cash close, 15.19, up 6.60% on the day, is the clearest single number for the shift in mood: still a low absolute level, but the largest one-day jump the index has logged since this crisis began escalating again. [read: the market spent two weeks treating oil-driven inflation risk as a Fed problem for September; an actual acceleration in strikes plus a threat against a US ally reads as a supply-shock problem first, rate problem second] Gold, often the crisis hedge, actually fell 0.50% to $4,451.15 as rising Treasury yields — the 10-year secondary-market quote near 4.74%, close to a 19-month high — competed for the same safe-haven bid. Whether that yield move is inflation fear, AI-driven bond supply, or both is Wednesday's question, when the FOMC's July minutes land at 2p ET.

What to watch from here: whether Muscat responds publicly to the bombing threat, which would turn a rhetorical flare-up into an actual diplomatic rupture with a state that has had zero prior friction with Washington this war; whether the "understanding" on the transit-route map produces an actual reopening timeline or stays permanently one level below the political conditions Iran says must come first; and whether Tuesday's projectile strike turns out to be part of the same pattern as the ADNOC-fleet hits last week — concentrated on specific national flags — or reverts to the more diffuse targeting of earlier months. None of those three answers itself in this issue; each is a plain follow-up for tomorrow's Board. [read]

Politics

Alaska and Florida vote today in primaries [NEW] with direct market relevance beyond the horse race. Alaska's top-four nonpartisan primary — the state's third cycle under the format voters adopted in 2020, sending the four highest finishers to a ranked-choice November general — sets the field for a competitive Senate race between two-term Republican incumbent Dan Sullivan and Democrat and former Rep. Mary Peltola, alongside an open governor's race since term-limited Mike Dunleavy can't run again, plus most of the state legislature. Florida Republicans choose a nominee to replace term-limited Gov. Ron DeSantis, with Trump-endorsed Naples Rep. Byron Donalds favored in what's been described as a bitter intra-party fight; Wyoming also votes statewide Wednesday. [NEW] Both races carry weight beyond their states: Alaska's Senate contest is one of the cycle's genuine Republican-held toss-ups, and a Peltola win would tighten the math the GOP needs to hold its Senate majority in November, while Florida's governor's race sets the tone for how far a Trump endorsement carries in a state primary against well-funded intraparty rivals. None of these results will be known before this issue goes to press — Alaska in particular counts slowly under ranked-choice rules, so a clean read may not arrive for days; watch for what's called in tomorrow's Board. Geopolitics

Russia and Ukraine traded another heavy overnight exchange, part of what regional coverage described as one of Ukraine's largest aerial attacks of the war on the Russian side of the ledger. [NEW] Ukrainian missile and drone strikes on Russia's Belgorod region killed at least six and wounded four, including a 14-year-old, in the border village of Koloskovo; separately, Russian forces hit nine-plus Ukrainian regions with 128 attack drones per the Ukrainian air force, killing two and injuring 19, and struck port infrastructure in Odesa, damaging a Togo-flagged civilian vessel — the strike on the Danube corridor a reminder that the grain- and goods-export route matters to the war's economics even when Hormuz dominates the oil headlines. [NEW] [read] Russia has now struck Ukraine's Naftogaz facilities 13 times in the past week alone, nearly 300 times since the year began — a sustained energy-infrastructure campaign, not a new escalation spike, and one running in parallel with the Hormuz disruption rather than in response to it. [DEVELOPING] Ahead of Xi Jinping's September state visit to Washington — the follow-up to Trump's May trip to Beijing — the two sides have revived a tit-for-tat sanctions exchange, with Beijing unveiling new measures against US firms it frames as retaliation for "erroneous" American moves; the two governments are separately said to be preparing an AI-focused dialogue ahead of the summit, though how much diplomatic capital Washington has left for that agenda, mid-Hormuz-crisis, is an open question.
