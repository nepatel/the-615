Lead

Oil, Hormuz, Inflation

Brent clears $111 as the Hormuz war turns into direct US-Iran ship combat — and September's CPI shows the first print of what that costs

Brent crude traded at $111.24 as of 6:08a ET this morning (Yahoo BZ=F, +1.54% on the day), with straits.live's feed putting it marginally higher at $111.49 (+2.77% over 24 hours, 5:14a ET) — the two quotes diverging but agreeing on direction. [fact/flag] That extends a break that started Friday, when Brent touched $110 a barrel for the first time since May, per CNBC. [fact] WTI is similarly elevated: Yahoo's CL=F (December contract) shows $100.64 (+2.13%, 6:09a), though straits.live quotes WTI nearly $8 higher in the same window — a gap this brief won't try to split. [fact/flag] The mechanism is a war that has stopped being a chokepoint story and become a running fight over who controls the strait itself. UKMTO confirmed at least 12 ship attacks in the week ending Oct. 16 — more than in any month since the US-Israeli war on Iran began Feb. 28 — of which one vessel sank, two were disabled and three were damaged or had unclear outcomes. [fact] Two of those losses are now dated and attributed: the container ship M/V GFS Pride sank under tow Oct. 15 after CENTCOM said an IRGC projectile, not a mine as Tehran claimed, struck it; and Saturday, Iran's Revolutionary Guard claimed a supertanker carrying more than two million barrels caught fire after hitting a mine on an "unauthorized route," a claim no maritime authority has confirmed. [fact/flag] A separate RPG attack hit a tanker near Jubail, Saudi Arabia on Oct. 17. [fact] UKMTO itself cautions that its count understates reality: most attacked vessels refuse to report to it, and the agency says it is aware of additional incidents from other sources it cannot verify. [fact, UKMTO self-disclosed limitation] Flows have held up better than the attack rate implies — Kpler data cited by CNBC put Hormuz oil shipments at 9.4 million barrels a day for the week to Oct. 14, about 70% of the pre-war average — but one weekend crossing count found this run was far thinner, fewer than 20 commercial vessels transiting over two days, just four on the Sunday. [fact/flag, figures from different windows, not reconciled]

What's new this week is the control dispute itself. CENTCOM commander Adm. Brad Cooper said in a video message Friday that the US and regional partners — not Iran — control the strait, citing 400 ships and roughly 100 million barrels transited in the past week alone, and argued the attack pace reflects IRGC "desperation" as the naval blockade of Iran's southern ports has cost Tehran an estimated $25bn in oil revenue and $350m in seized cryptocurrency financing. [fact, single-sourced to CENTCOM] Iran's IRGC rejects that framing, says the strait is unsafe for any vessel that doesn't coordinate with its own Persian Gulf Strait Authority, and disputes the US transit numbers as exaggerated — neither side's count is independently verified. [fact/flag] Iran has also expanded its own vessel blacklist to 124 ships — up from 45 when first published in late August, through an intermediate 81, to 124 as of Oct. 16 — threatening detention, confiscation or fines for anyone who transits without coordinating with it; the latest round of 43 additions included seven more Sinokor-linked VLCCs, per Lloyd's List reporting via Seatrade Maritime. [fact, single-sourced] The same reporting counts at least 26 attacks or security incidents around the strait in the first half of October alone. [fact, single-sourced]

Read: the escalation is no longer just a shipping-and-insurance story — it is now showing up in the inflation data that drives Fed policy and, two weeks out, the midterms. September CPI, released Oct. 14 after issue No. 25 went to press, printed hot: headline inflation at 3.7% year-over-year (above the roughly 3.6% consensus) and up from August's 3.4%, with core at 2.6% y/y against a 2.5% forecast. [NEW, fact, BLS] The energy index alone rose 5.2% for the month — gasoline up 9.1% — and accounted for more than half of the headline's monthly gain. [NEW, fact, BLS] That print reflects September's crude, which was still in the $102-106 range; this month's further break above $110 suggests October's CPI, due in mid-November, is tracking hotter still, which cuts against odds for a cut at the Oct. 27-28 FOMC meeting that were already thin and inconsistent across prediction markets. [read] Set against that: AAA's national retail gas average has actually fallen to $4.2786/gal today from $4.3333 on Oct. 14 — a pump-price lag this brief won't assume survives another two weeks of $110-plus crude. [fact/read] Not every analyst reads the crude move as durable: Invezz reports Goldman Sachs is still forecasting Brent near $90 by December despite the Gulf supply risk, a call that — if right — implies today's price is overshooting the physical disruption rather than tracking it. [fact/flag, single analyst call, unconfirmed against Goldman's own research note] Watch: tonight's Netflix and Tesla earnings for an early read on consumer and ad spend against this inflation backdrop; whether Iran's Oct. 18 conditional offer to reopen the strait within six days of a deal gets any public US response; and whether Washington's stated reluctance to escalate before Nov. 3 — reinforced by a reported post-midterm strike plan that may include a large-munition option, per CNN-sourced reporting that this brief cannot independently confirm — holds through two more weeks of this.

Iran & Hormuz

On diplomacy: Iranian FM Abbas Araghchi's conditional offer — reopen the strait within six days if Washington accepts Iran's terms — was reiterated around Oct. 18, per NewsNation, but remains exactly that: conditional, with no US response located this run. [fact/flag, single-sourced] It tracks the broader seven-day reopening framework Iran first floated in late September via Qatar, which called for the US to begin easing its port blockade and halting Hormuz-linked military operations in exchange. [fact, continuity] On the military side, Iran's acting defense minister, Majid Ibn Reza, said Saturday that Iranian missiles — not US bombs — will decide the war's future, warning of costs to US "reputation, economy, and electoral calculations" if Washington escalates.
