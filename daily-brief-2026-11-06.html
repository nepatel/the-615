Lead

MARKETS

The AI trade breaks: Wall Street's worst day since April wipes out $2.5 trillion, and Asia hasn't stopped falling

Thursday was the reckoning this brief has been flagging risk for since AI-capex concentration became impossible to ignore. The S&P 500 fell 4.36% to 7,353.73, the Nasdaq Composite fell 5.52% to 24,315.00 and closed in technical correction — more than 10% below its October 6 record — and the Dow lost 1,833 points (3.44%) to 51,523.06. [fact, NEW] All three indexes posted their worst single session since April 2025. CNN put the market-value destruction at roughly $2.5 trillion across US equities; the "Magnificent Seven" alone shed more than $1 trillion. [fact, NEW] The damage was concentrated but not contained: all 11 S&P sectors closed lower, and the heaviest hits landed on the AI-infrastructure trade specifically — Nvidia closed down roughly 9.3% to $181.60 (sources range 8.3%–9.3%), erasing about $450 billion of its market value and pushing the stock negative for 2026; AMD fell sharply, with closing figures ranging 15.9%–17.3% across sources (StockTitan's official close: -17.26% to $404.40); Micron fell a reported 17.46% to $656.39; CoreWeave fell somewhere between 10% and 15% depending on the source; GE Vernova, a data-center power supplier, fell 9.1%; Tesla fell 10.8%. [fact/flag, NEW] This brief flags the source divergence on AMD/CoreWeave rather than picking one figure.

The mechanism matters more than the magnitude. Reporting converges on a single thread: investors used this week's Democratic sweep of Congress — and Democratic candidates in several states explicitly campaigning on pauses to data-center construction, Texas gubernatorial nominee Gina Hinojosa's call for a moratorium on new approvals being the most-cited example — to reprice the odds that the AI buildout keeps scaling at its current pace. [fact/read, NEW] That is a political catalyst landing on top of a valuation question that was already live: whether AI companies' capital spending is outrunning AI's actual earnings. Quilter strategist Stuart Clark's read, echoed across coverage, is the one worth sitting with for a portfolio built around mega-cap tech: concentration risk that built up over 2026's AI run means a handful of stocks moving hard enough can drag the entire index, independent of what the other 495 companies in the S&P are doing. [fact/read, NEW] President Trump posted on Truth Social blaming Democrats for the rout — a claim this brief notes without endorsing, since the proximate trigger (AI-capex and buildout-regulation fears) is distinct from broader macro conditions, which were little-changed week over week. [fact, NEW]

The cross-asset tape is the tell for how to read this. A genuine flight-to-safety selloff sends money into the dollar, into Treasuries, and into gold simultaneously. Thursday and Friday's pre-market got two of three: the dollar actually strengthened (DXY +0.46% to 102.02, reversing Tuesday's election-driven slide) and Treasuries caught a modest bid (10Y down to 4.837% from Tuesday's 4.952%, though only 5Y and 30Y moved meaningfully). Gold did not participate — it's down 0.82% to $4,079.70 alongside everything else. [fact, NEW] That combination — dollar up, gold down, equities down hard — reads less like classic risk-off hedging and more like indiscriminate de-risking: investors raising cash and covering dollar-funded leverage rather than rotating into traditional safe havens. [read] Oil fell in sympathy (Brent -0.84% to $97.46, WTI -0.86% to $89.74, both down roughly 5% from Tuesday) on growth-scare demand fears rather than any new Hormuz development — the Strait counters are essentially unchanged from issue No. 36. [fact/read, NEW]

Asia's Friday session shows this isn't over: Japan's Nikkei fell 5.96% and Korea's Kospi fell 6.02% — both heavily weighted to the same chip supply chain as Thursday's US casualties — while Hang Seng (-1.27%) and Shanghai (-1.10%) were comparatively insulated, consistent with lighter AI-capex exposure in Chinese index weights. [fact, NEW] US futures are attempting a bounce (S&P +0.25%, Nasdaq-100 +1.32% premarket), but that stabilization is fragile and untested against the session's own risk: October's jobs report lands at 8:30a ET, after this brief goes to press, with consensus clustered at a historically weak +59K to +65K. A number that confirms a cooling labor market would, in a normal week, be read as more room for the Fed to cut in December; in this week, it lands directly on top of a market already questioning whether the sector carrying most of 2026's gains can keep growing into its valuation. Watch whether Friday's cash session holds the futures bounce, or whether the AI-capex repricing has further to run.
