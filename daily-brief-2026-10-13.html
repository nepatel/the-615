Lead

Houthis hit Riyadh, Trump weighs joining Saudi fight, and the Iran talks stall over who's left to talk to

A second front opens on the Arabian Peninsula just as Washington admits its own strikes emptied the room across the table from Tehran

Houthi missiles have hit Riyadh's King Khalid International Airport on three straight days — Saturday (13 dead, including one US citizen, and more than 300 wounded), Sunday (one more dead, 11 hurt), and a claimed Monday strike plus a drone attack on a base at Khamis Mushait that Saudi-led forces say they intercepted. [fact] Separate strikes earlier in the week also hit Abha airport; officials put the week's death toll across all the attacks at 20-plus. [fact] The airport has been closed since Saturday and stayed closed through Monday's 8:48a update; Delta has pushed its planned Atlanta-Riyadh route back to March 2027. [fact] Saudi Arabia has opened an inquiry into why its air defenses failed to stop the strikes. [fact] Trump said Saturday the US "may" join Saudi Arabia's campaign against the Houthis and would decide quickly; by Monday he was calling the group's conduct "misbehaving very badly." [fact] A Houthi spokesman, Mohammed Al Bukhaiti, told AFP any US involvement would bring a "swift and painful response" against American interests in the region and named Saudi oil and airport workers as "legitimate targets." [NEW, fact, flag: single AFP attribution for the threat] Overnight into Tuesday the Houthis said Saudi aircraft struck Kamaran island and Jawf province and that they downed a Saudi drone over Saada; Riyadh did not confirm either claim. [fact, flag]

That escalation lands on top of, not instead of, the Iran war the past several issues have tracked — and Trump supplied the line connecting them. In remarks at a rally this week he said the US is struggling to negotiate with Tehran because so many regime figures have been killed in American and Israeli strikes: "We might have gone too far with the leaders because we don't know who the hell we're dealing with anymore." [NEW, fact, flag: the source's own rally location/date is internally inconsistent — text says Ohio, a photo caption says Tennessee on Oct. 10] That is, in effect, an admission that the decapitation campaign Axios reported the Pentagon was told to keep preparing (issue No. 24) has a direct cost: it degrades the other side's capacity to make a deal stick, independent of whether Washington wants one. [read] Mechanically, a Houthi front — fellow Iran-aligned actors, now explicitly threatening Saudi energy infrastructure — gives Tehran a proxy escalation option that doesn't require Iran's own depleted leadership to act directly, right as the US has tried to time-box its own Iran strikes to after Nov. 3. [read] Iran's security chief Mohsen Rezaei said Saturday the US reply to Tehran's seven-point Hormuz-reopening plan was "insufficient" — Washington accepted some conditions, left others unaddressed — and that Iran won't soften its terms. [fact] Qatar's Emir on Tuesday called for doubled efforts to secure Gulf navigation and protect energy facilities, a sign the anxiety is no longer bilateral; Iran's foreign minister Araghchi separately discussed Yemen and regional de-escalation with his Egyptian counterpart Tuesday, the first sign this run of Cairo working the Houthi angle diplomatically. [fact] None of this has altered the hardware trajectory flagged in issue No. 24 — the Theodore Roosevelt strike group is still inbound to bring the US to three simultaneous Middle East carriers, with arrival-timing estimates still split between late October and late November depending on the source — so a Houthi-focused US response would layer onto, not replace, that buildup. [continuity, fact/flag]

The market's read on all this is messy rather than clean. Brent settled Monday at $106.68, +1.86%, as the Riyadh attacks and the ongoing Hormuz tanker campaign (12 ships hit Sept. 28-Oct. 5 per Reuters/JMIC) pushed a risk premium back into the price. [fact] Premarket Tuesday it has given back more than that move, to $104.42 (-2.09% as of 6:07a ET) alongside WTI's -2.08% to $91.79. [fact] Nothing in the news flow explains the reversal cleanly — it may be profit-taking after Monday's jump, positioning ahead of today's bank earnings and tomorrow's CPI, or a read that a Houthi-focused US response is a lower-probability, lower-intensity escalation than a direct Iran campaign would be. [read, flag: mechanism not confirmed by any source] What is not in dispute is that OPEC+ is already holding output flat for a second straight month (31.01mn b/d, next review Nov. 1) and that Kpler's regional export data still shows Middle East crude flows at or above pre-war levels despite the worst strike tempo of the war — an divergence between the attack headlines and the physical-barrel data that the Iran & Hormuz section below tries to reconcile rather than paper over. [fact/read] For the investor: the Riyadh-Houthi thread is the more trade-able near-term risk precisely because it's less constrained by the midterm-timing promise Trump has made on Iran directly, and Saudi energy infrastructure — not Iranian — is the explicit threatened target.

Politics

The Nov. 3 midterms are three weeks out, and California's ballot carries the marquee economic-policy fight of the cycle: Proposition 40, a billionaire wealth tax that backers refused to withdraw despite pressure from Gov. Newsom, now faces two industry-backed countermeasures, Propositions 41 and 42, on the same ballot. [NEW, fact] Fourteen measures are on the statewide ballot in total, five from the legislature and nine by initiative. [fact] Prop 40 would levy an annual tax on net worth above a threshold aimed at the state's billionaire residents; Props 41 and 42 are industry-backed responses designed to blunt or complicate it rather than repeal it outright, and both qualified only after Prop 40's backers declined Newsom's request to withdraw. [fact] For investors, the number to watch isn't just California's own revenue math — a passed wealth tax, even one likely tied up in litigation, would be the first state-level precedent other legislatures could cite directly. Separately, the continuing resolution signed in September still funds the government through Dec. 11, pushing any shutdown fight past the election [continuity] — no new fiscal-calendar development this run.

Iran & Hormuz

Iran's security council chief Mohsen Rezaei said Saturday that Washington's response to Tehran's seven-condition plan to reopen the strait was "insufficient" — the US had accepted some conditions and left others unaddressed, he said, and Iran won't soften its position without clarity on the rest. [fact] The plan, presented at the UN General Assembly, would reopen Hormuz and resume nuclear talks within a week in exchange for the US lifting its naval blockade, easing restrictions on Iranian oil sales, and restoring a regional ceasefire; Trump rejected an earlier version of it in late September. [continuity, fact]

Beyond the Lead's numbers, a Panama-flagged crude carrier, Gem No 2, was struck by a drone roughly 25km west of Al Jazeera, UAE, this week; the fire was extinguished and all 22 crew were reported safe. [NEW, fact] A separate vessel was reportedly hit by projectiles about 95km north of Madinat ash Shamal, Qatar, with casualties reported — continuity with the Fujairah/Madinat ash Shamal spillover zone flagged in issue No. 24. [DEVELOPING, fact, flag: single-sourced, casualty count not given] The IRGC claimed a Monday strike on a supertanker transiting with its transponders off; that claim, like Saturday's mine-strike claim that shipping analyst Sal Mercogliano said the released video didn't actually support, is unconfirmed independently. [flag]

The reconciliation this issue owes on the Board's divergence: Kpler's provisional data shows regional Middle East crude exports running at a 7-day average of 18.5mn b/d as of Oct. 1 — above pre-war levels — even as the attack trackers above describe the worst strike tempo of the war. [fact] The two aren't necessarily in conflict: Kpler counts loaded cargo that clears the region by any route and excludes dark-fleet vessels with transponders off, while the strike counts measure discrete attack events concentrated in the strait itself. A high attack count can coexist with resilient aggregate flows if shippers are increasingly routing around the strait, absorbing the war-risk premium, or simply accepting the odds on any single transit — which is consistent with Lloyd's List's (undated, pre-October) estimate that dark transits made up over 80% of Hormuz passages after the ceasefire collapsed. [read] Marco Rubio's "the Strait of Hormuz is open" and the IRGC's Mohammadreza Naqdi's "closed...full control" claims describe the same waterway from two sides that each have reason to overstate their control of it; neither is treated here as the operative fact. [flag]
