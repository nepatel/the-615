Lead

MARKETS · RATES · TRADE

A hot PCE print and new tariffs on 14 countries triggered Wall Street's sharpest swing of the week — and most of it reversed by the close

Wednesday's session was the week's most violent, and its outcome is more ambiguous than Monday's reinstatement-rally headline suggested the market had settled. The Bureau of Economic Analysis's October Personal Income and Outlays report, out at 8:30 a.m. ET, showed headline PCE inflation at 3.8% year-over-year and core PCE — the Fed's preferred gauge — at 3.3%, a tenth above the roughly 3.2% consensus and up from September's reading. Personal income rose 0.7% on the month and spending rose 0.3%, both in line with recent trend. The inflation beat, however modest, was enough on its own to push December rate-hike odds to roughly 98% by one afternoon reading, up from issue No. 42's "better than 90%" snapshot five days earlier.

The inflation print landed the same morning the U.S. Trade Representative's office, under Jamieson Greer, proposed new Section 301 tariffs on 14 trading partners — China, the EU, Japan, India, South Korea, Taiwan and Vietnam among them — following an eight-month investigation into what USTR called unreasonable trade restrictions. The proposed caps range from 15% on the EU and Japan to 35% on Vietnam, with China facing an additional 20% levy that could double to 40% if Beijing is found to be breaching its existing trade truce with Washington; these are ceiling rates subject to a comment period closing Jan. 5, 2027 and hearings starting Jan. 25, meaning nothing is in effect yet. The proposals are explicitly designed to replace the IEEPA-based tariffs the Supreme Court struck down in February — a reminder that the administration's post-ruling tariff architecture is still being rebuilt in real time, not resolved.

Mechanism: the combined shock — a Fed now effectively locked into a December hike, layered onto a fresh multi-year tariff overhang on top economies — sent the S&P 500 down as much as 3.2% intraday and the Dow down more than 1,100 points (-2.2%) at its low; estimates of the Nasdaq's intraday drawdown ranged from roughly 1% to 4.6% across outlets and were not reconciled this run. Chip and AI names, which had powered Monday's Huang-reinstatement rally, led the reversal on the way down and lagged on the recovery: Nvidia closed down another 3.91%, its third straight weak session since Monday's peak. By the close, the Dow had erased nearly all of its loss to finish +0.33% at 50,182.95 — its second consecutive close above 50,000 — while the S&P (-0.25% to 7,182.78) and Nasdaq (-0.62% to 23,585.80) posted smaller net losses than the intraday swing implied.

Second-order consequence: the day's price action argues that two distinct risks are now priced separately rather than netted against each other — a tightening Fed (inflation-positive data pushes hikes, not cuts, because this cycle's inflation is running above target) and a tariff regime whose final rates, timing and retaliation risk won't be known for two more months. That combination is classically stagflationary on paper — inflationary from tariffs, growth-negative from both the tariffs and higher rates — yet the index-level recovery suggests investors are for now treating Wednesday's news as noise to buy rather than a regime change, a read this brief treats as interpretation, not settled fact. The reinstated Nvidia trade, barely a week old, is the most exposed position to that read being wrong: another leg down in chip names on anything less than optimistic — holiday retail data, OPEC+'s Sunday decision, or a hot session once markets reopen Monday — would reopen the question Monday's rally was supposed to close.

Watch: whether Black Friday's half session and the weekend's first holiday-spending reads (Mastercard SpendingPulse, Adobe) hold up against the tariff overhang; whether Nvidia's three-session slide since the reinstatement peak stabilizes; Sunday's OPEC+ 2027 framework vote.

Politics

The Section 301 tariff proposals (see Lead) are explicitly built to replace the IEEPA tariffs the Supreme Court invalidated in February, and they are already drawing the same two-sided political reaction that earlier rounds did: congressional Democrats and the governments of several of the 14 targeted economies have objected, while USTR frames the move as redress for "unreasonable" trade practices identified over an eight-month investigation. Separately, Sen. Elizabeth Warren's position that Nvidia's reinstatement of Jensen Huang "does not change" the company's outstanding obligation to answer Congress on export enforcement (issue No. 42) gained a concrete forward date this run: Senate Banking Chairman Tim Scott has requested Huang testify publicly Dec. 3 and Commerce Secretary Lutnick on Dec. 10, with Huang having separately agreed in principle to appear per Politico's Nov. 18 reporting — though the committee had not finalized a date as of that report. Business

Best Buy's fiscal Q3 (reported Tue. 11/24, after issue No. 42's press time) beat decisively: revenue of $10.24B against roughly $9.88B consensus, comparable sales up 6.1% versus a 2.3% estimate — the company's strongest comp growth in five years — and adjusted diluted EPS of $1.62 versus $1.49 expected, up 16% year-over-year. Management raised full-year adjusted EPS guidance to $6.90-$7.05 from $6.70-$6.90 and lifted comp-sales guidance to 3.4%-4.0%, though Q4 comp guidance of 2%-4% implies some deceleration from Q3's pace, and inventory rose 11% heading into the holiday quarter. It was the first earnings report under new CEO Jason Bonfig, who succeeded Corie Barry on Nov. 1. Walmart's beat-and-raise (issue No. 42) and Best Buy's beat now both land ahead of today's Black Friday half session; AAA's pre-holiday read had gas at a still-elevated $3.82/gal on Nov. 15 against a softening travel forecast (see Board), a backdrop against which the weekend's first Mastercard SpendingPulse and Adobe Analytics tallies — not yet published — will be read.

Technology

Beyond the Lead, the most consequential update is a correction to issue No. 42's own flagged item: that issue reported, single-sourced and unverified, that Commerce had added Megaspeed to its Entity List the same day as the Huang reinstatement. This run found the opposite from the Financial Times: Commerce has in fact held off on new Entity List additions for roughly two months, leaving Megaspeed off the list despite the Pentagon, State and Energy (and in one account Treasury) departments pushing for its addition over alleged Nvidia chip-diversion to China. The Justice Department is separately leading a criminal probe into Megaspeed; Nvidia says it found no evidence of diversion on its own review and placed the company on its internal "no-sell" list in mid-2025. On Huang's Congressional calendar: Scott's office has now attached specific requested dates (Dec. 3 Huang, Dec. 10 Lutnick) to what issue No. 42 described only as an expanding three-stage schedule (see Politics/Board). SpaceX's 15th integrated Starship test flight (Nov. 13-14) completed a 22.5-hour orbital flight and achieved the first tower-arm catch of an orbital Starship on return — a notable capability milestone this brief had not yet reported. No frontier AI-model release or VC mega-round cleared this brief's sourcing bar as verified new information this run; several circulating claims were single-sourced from low-quality aggregators and are omitted rather than reported with a weak flag.

Geopolitics

Reaction to the new Section 301 tariff proposals (see Lead/Politics) extends beyond domestic politics: several of the 14 targeted governments, including major trading partners in Asia and Europe, have objected publicly, though specific government-by-government responses were not independently catalogued this run. Sunday's OPEC+ ministerial is the geopolitical event most directly in this brief's window: the group's seven core members held December output flat at their Nov. 1 meeting, deferring the harder question — whether to restore another tranche of 2022-vintage halted capacity — to Sunday's full ministerial, which will set the 2027 framework and is shaped by an ongoing audit of members' production capacity that the continuing Iran war has complicated. No standalone China/Taiwan or Ukraine item cleared this brief's bar this run; the widened European-US-Ukraine track noted in issue No. 42 remains the latest known development there, unchanged.

Iran & Hormuz

Attacks on Gulf shipping continued at an elevated pace through the back half of November: UKMTO logged incidents near Oman's Khasab on Nov. 21 and a Nov. 22 incident "involving military forces" in the Gulf of Oman near Chahbahar, close to the U.S. Navy's blockade line — wording that may, but is not confirmed to, indicate direct U.S. military action against a vessel. UKMTO's own October-November comparison, cited in reporting reviewed this run, put October incidents at 31 and November's running total at 19 as of Nov. 18 — a pace ABC News described as the highest since the Iran war began in late February, though this run could not independently verify the raw counts against a UKMTO bulletin. Less-corroborated single-source reports describe further incidents Nov. 25-26 (see Board); Iran's account continues to attribute strikes to sea mines where UKMTO describes projectile strikes, an attribution gap unreconciled as in every prior issue. UANI's transit counts (see Board) are now five days stale; no fresher figure was located. Oman and Qatar remain the mediation channels; no direct U.S.-Iran meeting readout located this run.
