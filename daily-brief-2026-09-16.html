Lead

10-YEAR TOPS 5% FOR THE FIRST TIME SINCE 2007, HOURS BEFORE THE FED

The 10-year closes above 5% for the first time since 2007, hours before a Fed almost certain to hike into an oil shock the market just watched a diplomatic off-ramp close on

Tuesday did what Friday's oil-driven relief rally couldn't undo. The 10-year Treasury yield touched 5.04% intraday and closed at 5.01%, its first close above 5% since July 2007. [fact] The proximate trigger wasn't a new inflation print — it was the collapse of the one de-escalation catalyst markets had been leaning on since last week. Oman's foreign minister, Badr Albusaidi, announced Sept. 13 that the Salalah talks between Gulf Arab states and Iran — meant to produce a temporary arrangement letting commercial traffic back through Hormuz via a split Iranian-entry, Omani-exit corridor — were indefinitely postponed, one day before they were due to convene, "in the interests of consensus." [fact, CNN] No party has said what broke down.

With that off-ramp gone, oil resumed the climb Friday's meeting-hope reversal had briefly interrupted. Brent, which settled Friday at $104.61, closed Tuesday around $108.75 and was trading near $107.77 in early Wednesday dealing — back inside the range that's held since the strait entered its seventh month of closure. [fact] WTI has no clean settle to report this run; two vendor feeds diverge, $103.52 and $104.68, a gap wide enough to flag rather than average. [flag] A tanker strike underscored that the underlying risk hadn't moved: Iranian state media said the Panama-flagged, Indian-crewed products tanker El Gaia was hit near Hengam Island on Sept. 13, with 13 of 14 crew rescued and one still missing. [fact, CNN/CBS]

That combination — a stalled diplomatic track plus a fresh strike — is what pushed the long end through 5%, not a domestic data surprise. [read] The mechanism is direct: energy-driven inflation that was already running hot in Friday's CPI print (core +0.3% m/m, reported last issue) now has no near-term supply-side relief valve, which raises the odds the Fed has to lean harder and longer than a one-and-done hike. CME FedWatch has odds of today's 2 p.m. decision at 92-93% for a 25bp move to 3.75-4.00% — a hike that would be the first since 2023 and the first move of any kind since the Fed's July hold. [fact] The 2-year, at 4.39%, and 30-year, at 5.25% (both Tuesday 3:30 p.m. snapshots), show a curve that's risen across the board rather than flattened sharply — a different signature than Friday's session, when the short end reportedly moved harder than the long end. [read: the curve shape argues the market is now pricing a sustained tightening cycle, not just a single supply-shock response]

Equities read it as a threat rather than a formality being priced in. The Dow fell 0.63% to 52,093.11, the S&P 500 lost 0.45% to 7,585.73, and the Nasdaq dropped 0.78% to 25,981.57 — a third straight down session heading into the decision, a sharp reversal from Friday's snap-back rally that had treated the same Fed-hike odds as background noise next to hopes of Gulf diplomacy. [fact] The difference this time: there's no oil-reversal story to hide behind. S&P futures ticked up modestly pre-market Wednesday, but that looks more like positioning ahead of the 2:30 p.m. press conference than a read on the decision itself. [read]

The setup into today's statement is now the mirror image of what this issue flagged Friday. Then, the risk was the Fed hiking into a shock that might already be turning. Now, with the Salalah track stalled and a tanker freshly hit, the Fed is walking into a decision with the supply-side risk pointed the wrong way — meaning today's dot plot and Chair Warsh's press-conference language on the path beyond one hike matter more than the hike itself, which the market has already fully priced. [read] Watch specifically whether the SEP shows one hike-and-done or a series; a hawkish dot plot on top of a 92%-priced hike is the scenario that could push the 10-year toward levels not seen since before the 2008 financial crisis.

A 10-year above 5% is not an abstraction for the reader's portfolio: mortgage rates, corporate borrowing costs and Treasury refinancing costs all reprice off this level, and the move happened in a single session rather than drifting there over weeks — the kind of gap move that tends to force repositioning rather than allow it. [read] The last sustained period above 5% preceded the 2007-08 credit crisis, though the comparison is about the yield level, not the underlying cause; this move is oil- and geopolitics-driven, not a housing-credit event, and Lennar's earnings tonight (see Business) will be one of the first reads on whether builders are already underwriting a higher-for-longer rate path into their margin guidance. [read]

Politics

The Supreme Court on Sept. 14 blocked the Trump administration's bid to let the U.S. Postal Service withhold delivery of mail ballots under a new executive-order rule, ruling the change couldn't be applied to the 2026 midterms regardless of its underlying legality. [fact, confirmed across CNN, NBC, NPR, CNBC] Justice Kavanaugh, joining the majority, wrote separately that the rule might fall within the Postal Service's statutory authority in principle but that state and local election officials lacked time to implement it before November. [fact] Trump called the ruling a "disaster" and a "big loss for Republicans." [fact] The decision is most consequential in the eight states — including California, Nevada and Oregon — that vote almost entirely by mail. Iran & Hormuz

The market mechanism is in the Lead; the diplomatic and ground-level detail is here. Oman's Sept. 13 postponement of the Salalah talks came with no stated cause beyond a need for "consensus," and no new date has been set; diplomats had already been cautioning that a full reopening requires a separate US-Iran understanding neither side has advanced. [fact] The proposed arrangement under discussion — ships entering the strait via Iranian waters and exiting mostly through Omani territory — remains unimplemented. [fact] The Sept. 13 strike on the tanker El Gaia near Hengam Island is the most recent confirmed attack on shipping in the strait itself, distinct from the Bab el-Mandeb/Red Sea theater, where Houthi forces continue consolidating control of Yemen's coastline seized in the prior issue's window — no new territorial change reported there since. [continuity] The counters (transits, dark-fleet screen, war-risk premia) are in the Board; none moved enough this run to suggest either escalation or a return toward the pre-postponement outlook. With Day 199 of closure now logged by two separate trackers in agreement, the practical situation is unchanged even as the diplomatic track worsens: single-digit daily transits against an 85/day pre-crisis baseline, insurance pricing that's kept most conventional carriers out of the strait for over a week without a fresh quote, and no operator this issue tracked reporting a return to normal routing.
