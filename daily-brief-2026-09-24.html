Lead

RATES REPRICE HAWKISH

The hawkish repricing hits full stride — a 2007-high 10-year, a hot PMI, a claims beat, and rising odds of a second straight Fed hike

Three weeks ago the Federal Reserve, under new Chair Kevin Warsh, raised the federal funds rate a quarter point to 3.75%-4.00% — its first hike since July 2023, passed 12-0 on Sept. 16. [fact] This week the data caught up to the signal. Wednesday's S&P Global flash PMI showed the composite index at 58.4, up from 56.0 in August and the strongest private-sector expansion since July 2021; manufacturing jumped to 57.0 from 53.9 (consensus was 53.6), the best reading since May 2022; services rose to 58.7 from 56.5 (consensus 56.0), the fastest expansion in more than five years. [fact] New orders grew at their fastest pace in nearly four and a half years, and employment growth hit its highest level since February 2021. [fact] This morning's initial jobless claims added to the picture: 193,000 against a 215,000 consensus, with the 4-week moving average down to 207,000 and continuing claims (week ending Sept. 17) at 1,347,000 — a clean beat, on every measure, that argues against any near-term labor-market cooling. [NEW]

The bond market's reaction has been unambiguous. The 10-year Treasury yield closed Wednesday at 5.135%, its highest level since July 2007, having jumped more than 13 basis points intraday as the PMI print hit; the 5-year crossed 5% for the first time since 2007 in the same session. [fact] Markets are now pricing roughly 70% odds of another quarter-point hike at the Oct. 27-28 FOMC meeting, which would be back-to-back increases — a sharp reversal from where futures sat as recently as early 2026, when cuts were still the base case. [read] The Fed's own September dot plot pointed the same direction before this week's data arrived: the median participant projection has one more 2026 hike taking the funds rate to 4.10%, a flat 2027, and cuts not resuming until 2028-29. [fact] Core PCE — the Fed's preferred gauge — was already running at 3.3% as of the July read, up from 3.0% in December 2025; the August print lands Sept. 30, the first inflation data point since the hike and the next test of whether Wednesday's move was a one-print overreaction or the start of a trend. [fact/flag]

Equities took the yield move badly: the S&P 500 fell 0.75% Wednesday to 7,706.03, the Nasdaq Composite dropped 1.13% to 26,936.04, and the Dow lost 352 points (0.68%) to 51,511.59 — a session Yahoo Finance and others attributed directly to the PMI-driven yield spike overwhelming any relief from Middle East diplomacy or the AI trade. [fact] Futures are pointing lower again Thursday morning (S&P -0.4%, Nasdaq -0.5%), with today's Trump-Xi summit adding a second live variable investors have to underwrite before the open. [fact] The mechanism worth sitting with: a hawkish Fed repricing that shows up first and hardest in the long end of the curve — the 10-year outpacing the 2-year's move — is a different regime than the 2022-23 hiking cycle, where front-end policy moves dominated. [read] If growth data keeps running hot into an economy already near full employment (the claims beat is one more data point for that), the term premium embedded in Wednesday's move has room to build further before the Sept. 30 print gives the market a cleaner read on whether the Fed is fighting a demand problem or a supply one. [read] For a portfolio priced off five-week-old cut expectations, that's the trade that just broke. [read]

One caveat worth carrying into the PCE print: the flash PMI's own price components suggest this is at least partly an inflation story rather than a pure growth story. Input costs across goods and services rose at the fastest pace since October 2022, driven by fuel, transport and wage pressures; selling-price inflation also picked up from August, though it stayed below the March-July rates. [fact] A hot composite driven partly by cost-push pressure feeding through supply chains is a different signal for the Fed than one driven by demand alone — worth distinguishing before assuming Wednesday's yield move is purely a "growth is strong" repricing. [read]

Geopolitics

The first concrete outcome of the Trump-Xi summit is already in: Treasury Secretary Bessent said this morning the US and China have agreed to extend their trade truce by two months, through Jan. 10, pushing back what had been a November expiration. [NEW, multi-sourced: CNBC, Bloomberg, Nikkei] The original truce dates to an October 2025 agreement; today's extension removes a near-term tariff-snapback risk investors had been pricing against a hard November deadline, though it is an extension of the status quo rather than a resolution of the underlying tariff, rare-earth-export and technology-control disputes. [read] Beyond the truce, previews across multiple outlets converge on critical minerals, technology controls and Taiwan as the summit's other top agenda items; Iran and Ukraine are also expected to come up, though neither is expected to produce a deliverable from this specific meeting. [fact] The South China Sea and Korean peninsula round out the expected agenda, with no outcome reported on either as of this morning's press time. Iran & Hormuz

Tuesday's three-hour, Qatar-mediated New York meeting between US envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner and Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi is the most direct US-Iran contact reported since the war began in February — even though it was conducted through intermediaries rather than face to face. [NEW] Iranian state television framed it as Tehran laying out conditions for reopening the strait: lifting the naval blockade, releasing frozen Iranian assets, and ending the war "on all fronts" — a maximalist opening position, not an agreed framework. [read] Trump called the session "very productive" and said a follow-up meeting is coming soon; no breakthrough was announced, and none of the numbers in this issue's Hormuz counters (transits, dark-fleet share, premia) show any change yet that would corroborate progress. [flag] That keeps this diplomatically separate from the Iran-Oman corridor track, which remains where issue No. 17 left it: agreed in principle in August, contingent on US compliance with a lapsed interim deal, and still without on-record Omani confirmation of any near-term reopening. [continuity] Worth noting the scale mismatch between diplomacy and physical supply: Tehran's opening conditions (naval blockade lifted, assets unfrozen, war ended "on all fronts") describe a full settlement, while the actual near-term relief to global crude flows this week came from a single Saudi pipeline restart, not from any Hormuz-specific breakthrough (see Board and Business). [read] The market's own pricing — a still-elevated 7.5%-10% war-risk premium, no fresher transit or dark-fleet data confirming any change in the strait itself — treats Tuesday's talks as exactly what they were: a conversation, not a resolution. [read]
