Lead

MARKETS

Friday's numbers settle the argument over last week's rout — Brent $98.32, WTI $90.83, the 10-year near 4.86% — and hand the week's real test to Tuesday's CPI print

Monday opens on a market that spent the weekend digesting, not moving. Thursday's AI-capex panic — a $3 trillion, 3.4%-to-5.5% single-session drop across the Dow, S&P 500 and Nasdaq, the worst day for US equities since April 2025 — and Friday's partial recovery on a blowout October jobs report are now fully reconciled in the official tape: the S&P 500 closed the week at 7,376.86, the Nasdaq Composite at 24,579.42, the Dow at 51,285.73, with the index down 2.7%, down 3.4% to 5.5% (sources vary by measurement window) and down 0.9% respectively for the five sessions. The VIX, which spiked as much as 70% intraday Thursday before settling up 69.7% to 28.18, retraced most of that move Friday, closing at 25.64 — still nearly double its pre-Thursday level, a sign options markets are still pricing elevated near-term risk even as the index-level panic has passed. Crude and rates, both flagged unresolved in Sunday's issue because of conflicting source data, now have clean Friday settles: ICE Brent at $98.32, up a token three cents on the day; NYMEX WTI at $90.83, up 31 cents; the 10-year Treasury yield near 4.86%, up roughly 2 basis points on the day but down about 7 on the week.

The read worth sitting with on a quiet Monday: none of Friday's confirmed levels suggest the market treated Thursday as a fundamentals event. Oil barely moved on a day when equities were recovering from their worst session in 19 months — if Thursday's selloff had reflected a genuine growth scare, crude would typically have fallen alongside stocks, not held flat. Instead, Thursday looks increasingly like what this brief called it Sunday: a positioning unwind concentrated in AI-capex-adjacent names (chips, memory, neoclouds) that got flushed hard and then found buyers the next day once October's payrolls — +165,000 against a consensus clustered near 59,000–70,000, unemployment down to 4.1% — gave index-level investors a reason to step back in. That framing makes Tuesday's October CPI print, due 8:30 a.m. ET, the week's actual test rather than a recap of last week. A single forecaster checked this run, TD Securities, expects headline CPI to cool to 0.30% month-over-month from September's 0.65%, with core at 0.21% — a deceleration, if it holds, but still consistent with year-over-year inflation running at 3.6%–3.7%, well above the Fed's 2% target and with no FOMC meeting until Dec. 9 to react to it either way [single-sourced]. A cooler-than-expected print would reinforce Friday's soft-landing read — strong jobs, falling inflation — and could extend the relief rally into the chip and neocloud names that led Thursday's drop; a hotter print reopens the harder question of whether the Fed can keep cutting into an economy still adding jobs at Friday's pace without reigniting the inflation argument markets had, until Thursday, mostly stopped pricing.

The one genuine overnight development is geopolitical, and it argues the same disconnect runs through the oil market specifically: Iran's Revolutionary Guard Navy claimed Sunday that three tankers caught fire or exploded in the Strait of Hormuz after entering what it called an unauthorized route, blaming naval mines and disabled navigation systems — a claim that is, in substance and almost word-for-word in phrasing, the third iteration in eleven days of an assertion first made Oct. 29–30 and repeated Nov. 3, with no vessel names, flags or casualties disclosed in any of the three instances and none independently verified by UKMTO or any other named tracker [single-sourced to Iranian state media]. That Brent moved three cents on a day its front month also carried this claim is itself informative: traders are treating the IRGC's repeated, unverifiable tanker claims as noise rather than confirmed supply disruption, consistent with how this brief's Hormuz counters have read for weeks — elevated crisis indices and a near-closed strait by transit count, but a market that has stopped reacting to each new claim on its own.

Breadth is the other thing worth watching into Monday's open, even without a fresh print to confirm it: Thursday's drop was concentrated in a specific cluster — Nvidia down roughly 9%, Micron and AMD both down more than 15% intraday before paring losses, the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index off 12%, and neocloud names Nebius, Iren and CoreWeave each down double digits — rather than broad-based across the index, and Friday's rebound in the same names (Nvidia, AMD and Intel each recovering in the high-single to high-single-digit percentage range) tracked that same narrow list. A market moving on CPI Tuesday in the same narrow cluster that moved Thursday and Friday would support the positioning-unwind read; a move that broadens out to the rest of the index either way would suggest something closer to a genuine repricing of growth or inflation expectations is underway.

Watch: Tuesday's CPI as the week's real catalyst; whether Monday's quiet session holds or gives way to continued unwind in the chip and neocloud names Thursday hit hardest; and whether UKMTO or another independent tracker ever corroborates any of the IRGC's three tanker claims, which would change the risk calculus for crude considerably if it happened.

Economics

Beyond the Lead: October CPI (Tue., 8:30a ET) is this week's only first-tier print, with October PPI following Friday. Both releases sit inside a compressed data calendar still catching up from the earlier-year shutdown delay that pushed October's jobs report into this month, which means the usual spacing between releases is compressed too — PPI, normally a few days removed from CPI, lands just three days later this cycle. TD Securities' single-sourced forecast — headline +0.30% m/m, core +0.21% m/m, both decelerating from September's +0.65% headline pace — was the only October CPI preview this run's research turned up; no multi-bank survey or Dow Jones consensus figure could be independently confirmed, so treat the forecast as directional rather than a market consensus [single-sourced]. Iran & Hormuz

See the Lead for this run's central Hormuz development — the IRGC's Sunday claim of three tankers hit by mines on an "unauthorized route," the third near-identical iteration of that claim in eleven days (Oct. 29–30, Nov. 3, Nov. 8), with no ship identities, flags or casualties confirmed in any instance and no independent corroboration from UKMTO or another named tracker. Separately, UKMTO logged a tanker fire off Khasab, Oman, reported Nov. 7 — a day before the IRGC's claim and, on the available reporting, a distinct incident; UKMTO itself has not verified the fire's cause [single-sourced]. Lloyd's List Intelligence reporting found this run puts overall strait traffic near 20% of pre-war levels; Lloyd's List has separately put the pre-war baseline near 120–130 vessels a day, which would put current traffic in the mid-20s per day if that baseline and percentage share a denominator — a calculation this brief flags rather than performs, since the two figures came from different reports this run [not independently reconciled]. That 20% estimate cannot be reconciled with either the IMF PortWatch transit count (1 vessel Nov. 1 against an 85/day baseline) or straits.live's dark-tanker screen (53.8% as of Nov. 4) because all three track different things on different cadences — see the Board's methodology note. The strait has now been closed to ordinary commercial shipping since Feb. 28, with intermittent, contested reopening efforts in the months since. The Qatar/Pakistan-mediated reopening framework shows no dated update this run; sticking points remain unchanged from mid-October (sanctions relief, frozen-asset release, an end to attacks as the price of reopening shipping).
