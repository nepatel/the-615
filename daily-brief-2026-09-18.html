Lead

THE FED HIKES FOR THE FIRST TIME SINCE 2023 — AND THE MARKET RALLIES ANYWAY

The FOMC raises rates a quarter point to 3.75%-4%, its first increase in three years, and stocks post their best stretch in weeks as falling oil and a bullish Nvidia forecast overshadow tighter policy

The FOMC voted 12-0 on Wednesday to raise the federal funds target range by 25 basis points to 3.75%-4%, the first increase since 2023 and a reversal of the cutting bias that has otherwise defined this cycle. [fact] The move followed through on odds that had climbed above 85% after the hot core CPI and PPI prints this issue's prior edition covered; Fed official Kevin Warsh, briefing reporters after the decision, said inflation "is still too high," defending the hike even as the statement described spending as resilient, productivity as strong, and capital investment as robust — a Committee tightening from a position of growth strength, not weakness. [fact/quote] The accompanying dot plot showed 16 of 18 participants penciling in at least one further increase this cycle, four of those seeing two more; markets are now pricing continued tightening rather than a pause. [fact]

Equities did not treat the hike as a headwind. The session after the decision, Thursday, all three major averages extended their advance: the Dow rose 0.61% to 51,778.04, the S&P 500 gained 1.12% to 7,637.72, and the Nasdaq Composite added 1.69% to 26,418, a third straight day of gains. [fact] Oddly, the VIX rose 3% to 17.71 on the same session — a move that doesn't fit the simple "stocks up, vol down" script and suggests options markets are still pricing meaningful two-sided risk into next month rather than settling into complacency. [read] Two separate tailwinds look more responsible for the rally than the Fed decision itself. First, Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang told investors Thursday he expects the company to sell twice as many chips next year, re-igniting the AI capex trade, lifting Nvidia itself roughly 2% to around $218 and pulling Samsung and SK Hynix higher into Friday's Asia session. [fact] Second, oil extended its slide for a third straight session — Brent down 2.2% to $102.57 and WTI down 1.9% to roughly $100.05 as of Friday morning — on reports of additional Saudi crude reaching global markets via Oman workarounds, even as the Gulf diplomacy that helped trigger last Friday's oil reversal stalled (see Iran & Hormuz). [fact]

Put together, the mechanism looks like this: the Fed hiked into an inflation picture that was substantially an energy story, and the energy story is now unwinding on its own, independent of the rate decision, while a chip-demand narrative gives risk assets a reason to rally that has nothing to do with monetary policy either way. [read] That's the same "one meeting behind" risk flagged in issue No. 14, now sharper rather than resolved: if oil keeps falling through October on supply workarounds rather than a real Hormuz settlement, a Fed that hiked on a shock already fading by the time it acted will have tightened into a softening inflation impulse, a mismatch the dot plot's four-hikes-pending signal makes more consequential, not less. [read] The dollar's move to a seven-week high post-hike adds a second-order channel worth watching — a stronger dollar and higher policy rate pulling against EM and multinational earnings translation even as the S&P celebrates a hardware-demand story. [read] There's a consumer-facing version of the same tension: a 25bp hike raises the floor under variable-rate borrowing (credit cards, HELOCs, adjustable mortgages) just as retail gasoline rose 16 cents on the week and Lennar cut its delivery guidance for the second straight quarter (see Business) — tighter policy and softer housing arriving together rather than one easing the other. [read] None of this is resolved: the next FOMC meeting's date was not confirmed this run, and whether Nvidia's Thursday pop survives a full trading session — rather than a premarket or single-day reaction, the pattern that reversed both Oracle's and Adobe's post-earnings moves two issues ago — is an open question this issue will track into next week. Technology

Nvidia's Jensen Huang told investors Thursday he expects the company to sell twice as many chips next year, a forecast that lifted Nvidia itself roughly 2% to around $218 for a second straight gain and carried into Friday's Asia session, lifting Samsung Electronics, SK Hynix, and other regional chipmakers and contributing to a 0.8% rise in MSCI's broader Asian equities gauge. [fact] It's the same demand-outpacing-supply narrative that briefly lifted Oracle's cloud-backlog beat two issues ago before a full trading session gave the entire premarket move back; whether Huang's forecast holds up past Thursday's single session, rather than reversing the way Oracle's did, is the open question this issue is tracking into next week. [read] Huang's doubled-demand comment is a verbal forecast made to investors, not a revised SEC filing or formal guidance update; it builds on the roughly 70% revenue-growth figure Nvidia projected for its coming fiscal year last month, with Thursday's remark suggesting upside to that number if production capacity keeps pace — a capacity caveat that has been the recurring constraint on the AI-hardware trade all year and is not resolved by Thursday's comments alone. Iran & Hormuz

The Oman-brokered meeting flagged in issue No. 14 as scheduled for Monday, Sept. 14, in Salalah — Gulf foreign ministers and Iran's Abbas Araqchi discussing a temporary arrangement routing ships into the strait via Iranian waters and out via Omani waters — was postponed indefinitely; Omani FM Badr Albusaidi said only that the postponement was "in the interests of consensus," without elaborating. [NEW] Diplomats briefed on the effort say a full reopening still requires a separate US-Iran understanding, with Washington wanting any deal to also address Iran's nuclear program and Tehran wanting the port blockade lifted and oil exports resumed — a gap that predates and outlasts Monday's non-meeting. [fact] Separately, Iranian state media claimed a commercial vessel was struck near Qeshm around Sept. 13, killing one crew member; US Central Command called the Iranian account false and attributed the strike to an Iranian drone instead — the two narratives remain unreconciled.
