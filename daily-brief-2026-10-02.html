Lead

NIKE'S TARIFF-CREDIT BEAT, AND A FED SIGNAL THAT HASN'T BEEN RETESTED

Nike's "beat" is a tariff check, not a turnaround — and yesterday's PCE-driven Fed signal still hasn't been retested by a fresher market read

Nike's fiscal Q1 2027 results, released after Thursday's close, are a useful lesson in reading past a headline number. GAAP diluted EPS came in at $0.72 against a roughly $0.13 consensus — a beat so large it would normally be a market-moving surprise. [fact] It isn't, because $986 million of it is a one-time benefit tied to the expected recovery of tariffs paid under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act — the same legal authority now before the Supreme Court in the pending tariff case. That one item added roughly 900 basis points of gross margin and about $0.52 per share. [fact] Strip it out and adjusted EPS lands near $0.20, still ahead of the $0.12-$0.13 ex-item Street estimate, but a far more ordinary beat. Revenue of $10.97 billion against a $10.85 billion consensus is the cleaner read on the underlying business, and it's a modest beat, not a blowout. [fact] The market treated it that way: shares fell roughly 3.5% in premarket trading despite the headline numbers. [fact] The mechanism is specific to companies with large exposure to tariffs the administration has signaled it expects to refund or reverse — expect more of these one-time, non-recurring "tariff recovery" line items to show up in retail and apparel earnings over the next two quarters as IEEPA litigation plays out, each one requiring the same discount. [read] Nike's own tariff exposure is still live and forward-looking regardless of the one-time credit: roughly half its footwear sourcing runs through Vietnam, which carries a 46% tariff rate, against 18% for China-sourced goods, and prior guidance had put the gross tariff hit at $1 billion with a margin headwind raised once already from about 75 to 120 basis points. [continuity] None of that changes with yesterday's print.

The more load-bearing macro story is still Wednesday's August PCE data and the roughly 35-37-point swing it produced in Fed-hike odds for the Oct. 27-28 meeting, reported in this brief's previous issue. [continuity] That move hasn't been retested with a fresher snapshot: this run could not locate a rate-odds reading newer than late Wednesday/early Thursday, so the roughly mid-30s hike probability carried in issue No. 22 stands as the last confirmed data point, not a number reconfirmed this morning. [flag] That matters because today's own calendar carries the heavier catalyst: September nonfarm payrolls at 8:30a ET, not yet released as of writing. A soft payrolls print on top of Wednesday's cool PCE would reinforce the hold case the market has already started pricing; a firm one reopens the hike debate on a second channel, independent of what crude does. [read] Crude itself is harder to read this morning: a single, unreconciled source put Brent near $91.93, well below yesterday's reported $100.36, with no settle located and no corroborating quote found — this brief is not treating that gap as a real overnight move until a second source or a settle confirms it. [flag] The other overnight story, the Yankees' 4-0 elimination of the Red Sox, is lower stakes than all of the above but lands in the same 24 hours as a reminder that October in New York runs on three clocks at once: the Fed's, the market's, and the sports calendar's — and only two of those have a scheduled release today.

Economics

September ISM Manufacturing PMI printed 54.6, below the roughly 55.2 consensus and down from August's 55.6 — an eight-month streak above the 50 expansion threshold, but the New Orders Index fell to 53.7 from 56.7 and Backlog of Orders dropped to 51.8, both signaling the pace of expansion is slowing even as the headline stays positive. [NEW, fact] One source's sector labeling was inconsistent (referencing "Q3" rather than the September monthly print); this brief reads it as the September figure pending confirmation, consistent with the Oct. 1 calendar slot.
